# 2. Introduction

The search for habitable worlds represents one of the most profound and pressing challenges in modern observational astrobiology. Identifying exoplanets capable of sustaining life requires the robust detection of atmospheric biosignatures—gases whose presence strongly implies a biological origin when found in specific environmental contexts. In alignment with the primary science goals of the upcoming Ariel mission, the ultimate objective of this endeavor is not merely to identify individual planetary candidates, but to conduct a comprehensive chemical census of exoplanetary atmospheres. By characterizing a large and diverse population of worlds, we aim to understand the underlying physical and chemical processes governing planetary formation and evolution, thereby providing the statistical context necessary to constrain the frequency and conditions of life-bearing environments in our galaxy.

Existing searches for habitable exoplanets have included extensive studies leveraging transmission spectroscopy, which have yielded interesting results such as the detection of water vapor, carbon monoxide, and methane in various exoplanetary atmospheres (de Kok et al., 2013). However, these studies have predominantly focused on gaseous giants (Hot Jupiters) due to their larger transit depths and high signal-to-noise ratios (SNR). Biosignature detection efforts on smaller, rocky planets have historically struggled with low-resolution spectra or prohibitive observation times, requiring novel methodologies to classify transmission spectra with low SNR according to their potential to contain biosignatures (Duque-Castaño et al., 2024).

While current efforts face the challenge of low-resolution data, upcoming space missions such as the Ariel Space Telescope are going to have high-resolution spectra that existing models will not be fully equipped to process efficiently without computationally expensive retrieval frameworks. Moving toward high-resolution spectra provides the critical benefit of resolving fine, overlapping absorption bands of complex molecules, allowing for the disentanglement of atmospheric degeneracies and more precise identification of trace gases like methane and ozone (Yip et al., 2021). 

In this work, we aim to design a machine learning model capable of distinguishing between biosignature and non-biosignature environments using high-resolution (HR) transmission spectra from an Ariel-like dataset. This constitutes a supervised binary classification problem dealing with high-dimensional numerical spectral data, where the output is a discrete label identifying whether the planetary atmosphere is an interesting candidate for follow-up observation.

To do so, we use the `multirex` library to generate synthetic high-resolution spectra. `multirex` is an open-source Python framework that leverages the TauREx 3 radiative transfer code to rapidly simulate the transit of starlight through a planetary atmosphere, robustly modeling molecular absorption and scattering. We evaluate a suite of four machine learning models—Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (XGBoost), Random Forest, a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and a 1-Dimensional Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN). By applying a rigorous Principal Component Analysis (PCA) preprocessing pipeline to isolate relevant chemical signals, we prioritize high true positive detection (recall) to minimize the risk of missing potentially habitable worlds, while extensively analyzing model error rates across diverse regions of the physical parameter space (e.g., planetary radius and stellar temperature).

In this paper, Section 3 details the background and related work, Section 4 details the dataset and feature engineering, Section 5 describes the methodology and model architectures, and Section 6 presents the results, including a comprehensive error analysis and model calibration evaluation.
