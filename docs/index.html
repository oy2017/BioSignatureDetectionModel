Ariel · machine-learning triage · simulator-to-mission reliability

When can a simulator-trained screen be trusted?

Ariel will deliver spectra of about a thousand exoplanets, and machine-learning screens trained on simulated spectra have been proposed to triage them. They are validated on the simulator that trained them. There will be no Ariel data to validate them on before the decisions are made. We ask what happens when the physics differs from the simulator — in ways we anticipate and in ways we deliberately hold out — and deliver the answer as a table a mission can act on.

The answer has three regions

For each way the simulator can be wrong, we measure what the screen loses, what no retraining can recover, what a randomized training grid buys back, whether a decline rule can tell, and what removes the loss. The map that comes out has three regions.

Region 1

Mismatch you modelled

Clouds, haze, stellar contamination, noise level, a different radiative-transfer code, other opacity tables. Randomized training plus a confidence-based decline rule recovers most of the loss. What remains is information the mismatch destroyed; only a better observation — or training on the alternative code or tables — gets it back.

Region 2

Mismatch that helps

Quenched, disequilibrium chemistry makes the carbon-rich label easier, at every vertical-mixing strength tried: the deep water depletion of carbon-rich atmospheres is carried up to where the spectrum forms. The design rule: train on equilibrium.

Region 3

Physics the simulator omitted

Nothing done to the training set helps; the screen is confidently wrong; only a distance-based novelty alarm sees it; only fixing the forward model repairs it. The worked case: two molecules carbon-rich atmospheres are known to carry (HCN, C₂H₂) that the field's standard Ariel training grid omits — and that put a carbon-rich screen at chance on the planets it exists to find.

Two screens under test

Theirs

The consortium's Tier-1 molecular screen

Mugnai et al. 2021 (AJ 162, 288) train default scikit-learn classifiers on simulated seven-point Tier-1 spectra to flag H₂O, CH₄, CO₂ or NH₃ above an abundance threshold — the published strategy for choosing which planets are re-observed. Its code is not public; we rebuild it from the paper's text and list every deviation.

Ours

A carbon-rich screen

Is C/O > 1? A chemical regime rather than a molecule, and a stated Ariel science target. Equilibrium chemistry, the Ariel layout, noise shaped by the consortium's own simulator, a tuned gradient-boosted classifier on per-spectrum-normalized bins.

One is simple and theirs; one is tuned and ours. Where both break in the same places the result is about the setup, not the model. Where they differ, the difference is a finding.

The reliability envelope

columnwhat it reports
lossaccuracy the mismatch removes from the screen, on the same planets
irreduciblethe part no retraining recovers — the ceiling set by a model trained at the test condition
absorbwhat a training grid that randomizes the uncertain ingredients buys back
detectwhether a decline rule ranks the screen's errors, by confidence or by distance from the training set
fixwhat removes the loss: training on it, a better observation, or a change to the forward model

Evaluated at the binning where the triage decision is made (Tier 1, seven points), on the mission's actual target list, with decline thresholds fixed on clean data and nothing tuned on the shifted sets.

The procedure, reusable on any screen

  1. Render the test planets under each mismatch: clouds, haze, spots, noise, another code, other tables, quenched chemistry, missing absorbers, compounds.
  2. Bound what any retraining could recover with a model trained at the test condition — every recovery is measured against that ceiling, never against clean accuracy.
  3. Randomize the uncertain ingredients jointly in one training grid; measure the in-domain cost and the share of each ceiling reached.
  4. Hold each axis out in turn: does robustness transfer to physics the screen never saw?
  5. Compare decline rules — confidence margin, ensemble disagreement, Mahalanobis, k-NN — against the clean selective baseline at equal coverage.
  6. Evaluate at the tier where the decision is made and on the mission's real target list; split by host type and chemistry assumption.

Two habits run through all of it: expectations are committed before a run and misses are reported as misses — several of ours are, including a rule from our own earlier work that turned out to be an artefact of the wrong denominator — and every relative number names its reference.

What is new, and what is not

No method here is new. Domain randomization, selective prediction, distance-based out-of-distribution scores, target-trained oracle bounds and held-out-axis evaluation are standard, and the paper cites their origins. What is new is the question asked of an exoplanet screen at all; physics-side mismatch axes where prior work shifts instrument noise; the absorb-or-detect-or-fix framing with held-out axes; the envelope as a mission deliverable; and the finding about the field's standard training grid. Closest prior work: Ardévol Martínez et al. 2022, the Ariel Data Challenge datasets, and misspecification detection for simulation-based inference in cosmology.
