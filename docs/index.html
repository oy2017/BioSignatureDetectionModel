Ariel · machine-learning classifiers · can they be trusted?

Can a simulator-trained classifier for Ariel spectra be trusted?

Machine-learning classifiers have been proposed to sort the thousand planets of Ariel's quick-look survey by composition. They are trained and tested on simulated spectra, because no real ones exist yet. We rebuilt the classifier the Ariel consortium proposed, confirmed it reproduces the published results, and tested it against the ways a simulator can be wrong.

What we found

Reproduced

The published classifier, rebuilt

Its code was never released. Rebuilt from the paper element by element, it matches all 48 published accuracies within 3.7 points. A mistaken reading of the recipe missed by 10 points on average, so the match is a real test.

Trustworthy

Robust where one might worry

Swapping the opacity database or the radiative-transfer code cost it about 3 points; two omitted absorbers cost nothing.

Not trustworthy

Confidently blind to haze

At a haze as weak as the least hazy hot Jupiters observed, it declared methane and water absent on nearly every planet that had them, became more confident, and gave no warning, although the molecular bands were intact. The cause: its three optical photometric inputs. Removing them, or training with haze, largely fixes it.

Does the answer carry over?

No. A second classifier, built for Ariel's carbon-to-oxygen question, failed in nearly the opposite places: two molecules missing from the standard training grid cost it 26–30 points on carbon-rich real targets, while haze cost it only 5 points at full resolution. One counterexample is enough: a stress test of one classifier cannot stand in for another, so each classifier needs its own before it helps choose which planets get deeper observations.

The stress test, reusable on any classifier

  1. Change the simulator one way at a time: clouds, haze, star spots, noise, a gain ramp, another opacity database, another radiative-transfer code, missing absorbers, and combinations.
  2. Measure the accuracy lost on the same planets and the same noise.
  3. Bound how much of that loss any retraining could recover, with the design retrained at the test condition.
  4. Train on a randomized grid, with each ingredient held out in turn, to see what robustness buys and whether it transfers.
  5. Ask whether the classifier knows: confidence, ensemble spread, distance scores and conformal prediction, scored against the clean baseline.
  6. Commit expectations before running, and report the misses.
