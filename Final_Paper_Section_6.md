# 6. Results and Discussion

## 6.1. Overall Performance and Model Hierarchy
The comprehensive evaluation across five independent test sets revealed a clear performance hierarchy among the evaluated machine learning architectures. The XGBoost classifier emerged as the top-performing model, achieving a mean accuracy of **88.67% (± 1.52%)**. It was closely followed by the Random Forest model at **86.31% (± 1.29%)**. The neural network architectures, while still highly effective, ranked third and fourth, with the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) achieving **85.23% (± 0.92%)** and the optimized 1D-CNN reaching **82.97% (± 1.35%)**.

To rigorously evaluate the statistical significance of these performance differences, pairwise McNemar's tests were conducted on the models' predictions. The test confirmed that while the performance difference between the two tree-based ensembles (XGBoost and Random Forest) was not statistically significant ($p \approx 0.072$), XGBoost significantly outperformed both the MLP ($p < 0.001$) and the 1D-CNN ($p < 0.001$). Furthermore, the MLP demonstrated a statistically significant accuracy advantage over the 1D-CNN ($p < 0.001$).

These results underscore the efficacy of decision-tree-based ensembles for this specific spectral classification task. The confusion matrix for the best-performing XGBoost model is presented in **Figure 1**, illustrating a high true positive rate (Recall = 89.19%) and a well-balanced precision-recall trade-off (F1-Score = 0.888).

## 6.2. The Critical Role of PCA in Feature Extraction
A central finding of this research is the undeniable importance of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) as both a dimensionality reduction and denoising step. While the best-performing models utilized PCA-transformed data, models trained on raw spectral data performed significantly worse, with the raw CNN peaking at only 70.02% accuracy.

Quantitative correlation analysis confirms that the first two principal components (PC0 and PC1) are physically tied to broad systematics rather than chemical features. Although these components explain over 99.25% of the total spectral variance, they showed practically zero quantitative correlation with the target biosignature labels (Pearson correlation coefficients of $r = 0.0045$ and $r = 0.0322$ respectively). By analyzing the component loadings and reconstructing the spectra, we determined that PC0 acts as a nearly perfect proxy for the mean transit depth ($r = 0.961$) and PC1 captures the overall spectral slope. Thus, they encode broad physical properties like planetary radius and stellar continuum level rather than specific, high-frequency chemical absorption features.

The final feature set was constructed using the next 100 principal components (indices 2–102), which cumulatively explain approximately 0.745% of the total variance. **Figure 2** shows the PCA scree plot of explained variance across the principal components, justifying this choice. Additionally, **Figure 3** illustrates the PCA reconstruction and residual error across wavelengths for a representative test spectrum (calculated as `((PCA - true spectrum)/True spectrum) - 1`), demonstrating that vital absorption features are preserved while broad-spectrum noise is discarded.

## 6.3. Error Analysis and Physical Dependencies
To understand model failure modes, a physics-based analysis was conducted by examining how classification errors varied with physical parameters. A summary of the parameter ranges explored is provided in **Table I**.

**Figure 4** presents a triangle plot of planet and stellar parameters (Planet Radius, Planet Mass, Star Temp), with data points colored based on correct versus incorrect test set predictions. This visualization reveals that errors are most frequent for planets with very low atmospheric temperatures or higher surface gravity, where atmospheric scale heights are compressed, leading to weaker absorption signals. 

Furthermore, **Figure 5** provides a similar triangle plot focusing on atmospheric parameters (mixing ratios of CH4 and O3). As expected, the model struggles near the predefined habitability thresholds (Log(CH4) = -6.0 and Log(O3) = -7.0). This highlights the inherent ambiguity in marginal biosignature detections, where the signal-to-noise ratio of specific absorption features is lowest, and demonstrates how habitability definitions heavily influence model accuracy.

## 6.4. Application Trade-offs: Accuracy vs. Recall
While XGBoost demonstrated the highest overall accuracy, the MLP model exhibited a uniquely valuable characteristic for mission-critical applications: a significantly higher mean recall of **94.56% (± 1.46%)**. In the context of searching for biosignatures, where the cost of a false negative (missing a potentially life-bearing planet) is exceptionally high, this makes the MLP a compelling alternative for initial candidate discovery.

Beyond raw classification accuracy, the reliability of model-predicted probabilities is a critical factor for mission planning and resource allocation. We assessed this via calibration curves and Brier scores, which quantify the alignment between predicted probabilities and actual biosignature occurrence. The XGBoost model demonstrated exceptional calibration, achieving the lowest Brier score (0.072) and maintaining a near-perfect diagonal on the reliability diagram (Figure [Calibration_Plot]). 

In contrast, the neural network models exhibited poor calibration. While the MLP achieved high recall, its calibration curve revealed overconfidence on incorrect predictions. The 1D-CNN exhibited a complete failure to discriminate (Brier score: 0.251), resulting in a severely squashed calibration curve restricted between predicted probabilities of 0.3 and 0.5. Rather than producing confident or "U-shaped" extreme predictions, the CNN was entirely "undecided." Due to the curse of dimensionality—being trained end-to-end on noisy, high-dimensional raw spectra (over 5,000 bins) with a relatively limited sample size—the model failed to extract meaningful features. Consequently, it defaulted to predicting the average dataset probability (~0.5) for nearly every sample, never generating a confident prediction.

This disparity in reliability further positions XGBoost as the optimal candidate for the final vetting stage of planetary candidates. While the MLP could serve as an initial, highly sensitive "first-pass" filter to capture all potential candidates, the higher-precision and well-calibrated XGBoost model is required for the subsequent, more rigorous vetting where high-confidence probability estimates are necessary to justify follow-up spectroscopic observations.
