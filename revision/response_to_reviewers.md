## Reviewer 1

### R1-1 — Overall assessment: are the central claims sufficiently supported?

> **Reviewer wrote:** "The manuscript presents a technically well-executed machine learning benchmark and the comparison among XGBoost, Random Forest, MLP, and CNN is carefully conducted. The statistical analyses are generally appropriate, the experimental pipeline is well documented, and the manuscript is clearly written. However, I believe the central scientific claims are presently insufficiently supported. The primary issue is not the implementation of the machine learning algorithms themselves but rather whether the experiments actually demonstrate what the manuscript claims. In its current form, the work demonstrates that an XGBoost classifier can successfully recover predefined biosignature labels from spectra generated within a single synthetic simulation framework. It does not yet demonstrate that the proposed methodology identifies biosignatures in actual exoplanet transmission spectra or that the mechanistic interpretation of the PCA representation is correct. These issues require additional experimental validation before the manuscript is suitable for publication."

**Response.** I agree with this assessment, and I am grateful for it — it correctly identifies that the original manuscript claimed more than the experiments showed. Rather than defend the original scope, I have narrowed the claims to match the evidence: the paper now presents a calibrated in-domain benchmark whose behaviour under domain shift is *measured*, not a demonstration of biosignature detection. This reframing runs through the **Title**, the **Abstract** (opening and closing paragraphs), **Section 1** (task framing and contributions), and **Section 5**. The two specific charges the reviewer raises — generalisation beyond the simulator, and the correctness of the PCA interpretation — are answered directly by new experiments, described under R1-2/R1-3 (Section 4.6) and R1-5 (Section 4.2). I would add one experiment that speaks to the reviewer's underlying question of whether the decision boundary reflects atmospheric physics or simulator-specific regularities. A band-occlusion test (Section 4.3, Table 6) masks one octave of the spectrum at a time and re-evaluates the frozen pipeline. Masking 3.9–7.8 μm — the octave containing the 4.74 μm ozone and 7.7 μm methane bands — drives both label-diagnostic subsets below chance, while masking 0.5–1.0 μm costs the ozone subset only six points. The pipeline therefore depends most on the wavelengths where the label-defining molecules actually absorb. This is interventional rather than correlational evidence, which the earlier error-versus-parameter analysis was not.

### R1-2 — The study is confined to a single synthetic ecosystem

> **Reviewer wrote:** "The most significant concern is that the entire study is developed, trained, validated, and tested exclusively within a single synthetic ecosystem. Every transmission spectrum is generated using the TauREx/MultiREx forward model, every class label is derived directly from the same simulated atmospheric parameters, and every evaluation is performed on additional spectra generated under essentially identical modeling assumptions. Consequently, the classifier is never evaluated outside the statistical distribution of the simulator that created both the inputs and the labels. High predictive accuracy under these conditions demonstrates internal consistency within the simulation environment but does not establish that the learned decision boundary represents genuine atmospheric physics rather than simulator-specific statistical regularities. The central question for a biosignature classifier is not whether it generalizes to additional TauREx simulations, but whether it generalizes to real exoplanet observations. The manuscript does not presently address this question."

**Response.** This is a fair and important point, and I have addressed it with experiments that genuinely leave the simulator. A new **Section 4.6** characterises the frozen pipeline across seven axes of domain shift (the full set, with the tables that quantify each, is detailed under R1-3); the two that depart most directly from the training simulator are highlighted here. The held-out planets are recomputed with an **independent radiative transfer code** (Exo-Transmit) (27), which costs 4.1 accuracy points, and regenerated with an **alternative molecular opacity compilation** (ExoMol/HITRAN line lists), which costs 16.1 points (22.9 with ozone also replaced). This speaks directly to the reviewer's deeper question of whether the learned decision boundary reflects genuine physics or simulator-specific regularities: the opacity-swap result shows it is *partly* simulator-specific — transferability is limited principally by the molecular line lists rather than by the classifier — so rather than assert robustness I now report that limit quantitatively. I now describe the classifier honestly as an *in-domain triage tool, not a detector for real observations* (Abstract, Section 1, Section 4.6, Section 5). The second half of this concern — that the labels are derived from the same simulation parameters — is a labelling issue and is answered under R1-4.

### R1-3 — Demonstrate robustness under realistic domain shift (seven axes)

> **Reviewer wrote:** "This distinction is especially important because real Ariel observations will differ substantially from the synthetic spectra used in this work. Ariel will observe heterogeneous transmission spectra containing instrumental systematics, wavelength-dependent detector effects, correlated noise, stellar contamination, imperfect calibration, incomplete molecular opacity databases, cloud and haze variability, photochemical complexity, and three-dimensional atmospheric structure. In contrast, the present study assumes one-dimensional atmospheres, Gaussian noise with a fixed signal-to-noise ratio, predefined atmospheric compositions, and idealized observing conditions. These assumptions substantially reduce the complexity of the classification problem. Before this work can be considered applicable to Ariel, the authors should demonstrate that the classifier remains robust under realistic domain shift. At minimum, the manuscript should include experiments using spectra generated by independent radiative transfer codes, alternative molecular opacity databases, different cloud and haze prescriptions, different stellar contamination models, varying spectral resolutions and signal-to-noise ratios, more realistic instrumental systematics, and domain-shift or transfer-learning experiments. Without such validation, the manuscript demonstrates performance only within a single synthetic environment rather than on the class of observations for which the framework is ultimately intended."

**Response.** I took this list as the core of the revision. A new **Section 4.6 (Robustness under Domain Shift)** covers all seven requested axes, evaluating the frozen pipeline against the in-domain baseline; Table 7 summarises the axes and Tables 8–11 and Figure 10 give the detail:

1. **Independent radiative transfer code** — Exo-Transmit (−4.1 points), with the opacity tables held byte-identical so that the numerical scheme is the principal difference; I report −4.1 as an upper bound, because Exo-Transmit holds gravity constant with altitude and that could not be matched (27).
2. **Alternative molecular opacity database** — ExoMol POKAZATEL / YT34to10 / UCL-4000, and separately HITRAN ozone (−16.1 / −22.9 points) (53–57).
3. **Cloud and haze prescriptions** — a grey deck and a Lee–Mie haze, paired per planet (Table 9) (58).
4. **Stellar contamination** — a physical Transit Light Source Effect model driven by BT-Settl/PHOENIX spot and faculae spectra (Table 10) (42, 59).
5. **Resolution and signal-to-noise** — resolution degradation and white/correlated noise sweeps (Table 8).
6. **Realistic instrumental systematics** — ExoRad2 with a published-parameter Ariel payload (Table 11) (31, 60).
7. **Domain shift** — an out-of-envelope (planet-radius) extrapolation split (Section 4.6).

I also characterise *how* the pipeline fails: the degradation is graceful and reduces to a few measured mechanisms — feature-amplitude suppression, chromatic distortion, and a decision-threshold bias, with the opacity axis crossing from a threshold bias into loss of label-defining signal once ozone is also replaced. I further re-ran the parameter-space error analysis of Section 4.3 under the injected white and correlated noise and found the added error spreads roughly uniformly across parameter space rather than concentrating on the already-marginal planets, so the clean-data error structure is not amplified by observational noise (Section 4.6). Three of the seven axes rest on stated approximations, which I name in Sections 4.6 and 5 rather than leaving implicit: the stellar-contamination model uses solar-metallicity spectra with adopted spot contrasts; the instrument-noise model uses an Ariel payload reconstructed from published parameters; and the resolution axis degrades an existing grid by binning rather than regenerating spectra natively, which conflates resampling with true resolution loss to the extent of 2.6 accuracy points.

On the resolution axis specifically, I have also corrected a claim the original manuscript made about how realistic my spectra are. I had described the R = 200 grid as sitting at the upper end of Ariel's Tier 3 resolution. It does not: Tier 3 is modeled at R = 100 for the highest-resolution spectroscopic channel, and R = 200 is the upper limit of that instrument's as-designed dispersion. The grid is therefore optimistic by about a factor of two relative to the configuration the mission plans, and binning to R = 100 costs 10.5 accuracy points. It is also uniform, whereas the instrument delivers R ≈ 30 beyond 3.9 μm, R ≈ 15 over 1.1–1.95 μm, and broadband photometry below 1.1 μm. Both facts are now stated in Sections 3.1 and 3.5 rather than glossed. Two effects the reviewer names in passing — fully three-dimensional atmospheric structure and photochemical complexity — lie beyond a 1-D forward model and are not among the seven tested axes; these are retained as explicit limitations (Sections 4.3 and 5) rather than presented as addressed.

### R1-4 — The positive class is a labelling rule, not a detection

> **Reviewer wrote:** "A related concern is the definition of the positive class. The manuscript defines biosignatures using fixed abundance thresholds (CH₄ > 10⁻⁶ and O₃ > 10⁻⁷), and every atmosphere satisfying these thresholds is labeled as positive. Consequently, the classifier is not discovering biosignatures but rather learning a predefined labeling rule imposed during simulation. This distinction is important because Ariel itself does not operate by measuring atmospheric abundances and applying deterministic thresholds. Ariel measures transmission spectra, after which atmospheric abundances must be inferred through retrieval algorithms that incorporate observational uncertainties, parameter degeneracies, stellar context, and competing abiotic explanations. The proposed machine learning framework therefore learns to reproduce a synthetic labeling convention rather than the operational biosignature identification process that Ariel will ultimately employ. Additional experiments using retrieval-derived abundances with realistic uncertainties or probabilistic labels would substantially strengthen the relevance of the proposed framework."

**Response.** The reviewer is right, and I concede the point rather than contest it. The task is renamed throughout — from "biosignature detection" to *recovery of an abundance-threshold labelling* — in the Title, Abstract, Section 1, and an explicit statement in **Section 3.1**. I then added two analyses that measure how far this labelling convention actually governs the result: a **threshold-sensitivity** test showing that accuracy varies by under two points as both cutoffs move by half a dex (±0.5 dex) (Sections 3.1 and 3.5), and a **margin analysis** showing that within 0.25 dex of the cutoff the classifier does not beat a majority-class baseline — i.e. those near-threshold labels are genuinely ambiguous (Section 4.3). The margin analysis speaks directly to the reviewer's concern about near-threshold cases; genuinely probabilistic, retrieval-derived labels are the natural next step, which I could not complete this cycle and name openly as the primary remaining item (Section 5). Generating them requires a nested-sampling retrieval for every planet and retraining under the resulting label uncertainty; I have begun prototyping that retrieval on the committed test planets, and scaling it to the full training set is the immediate next stage of the work.

### R1-5 — The PCA interpretation is not supported by the mathematics

> **Reviewer wrote:** "I also have substantial concerns regarding the interpretation of the PCA representation. The manuscript repeatedly argues that the first principal components primarily encode broad physical structure while higher-order components encode chemically informative absorption features. However, this interpretation is not supported by the mathematics of principal component analysis. Principal components are orthogonal linear combinations of all original wavelength variables. They are not independent physical variables, nor do they uniquely correspond to individual atmospheric processes. Every principal component generally contains different weighted mixtures of continuum structure, planetary radius, atmospheric temperature, molecular absorption, scattering, clouds, and measurement noise. Consequently, methane and ozone information is expected to be distributed across multiple principal components rather than residing exclusively within higher-order components. Demonstrating that PC0 correlates strongly with mean transit depth does not imply that chemically relevant information is absent from that component, nor does it establish that chemistry has been isolated into PCs 2–101. These interpretations require considerably stronger evidence than presently provided. The manuscript should include loading-vector analyses, variance decomposition by physical parameter, reconstruction studies after selectively removing principal components, and explicit quantification of how methane and ozone absorption features project onto individual principal components before drawing these mechanistic conclusions."

**Response.** Conceded. The interpretation is **withdrawn** — removed from the Abstract and Sections 3.2, 4.1, 4.2 and 5. Section 4.2 now reports only where the label-discriminative information is measured to sit, and the four requested analyses together give a sharper result than the original claim did: variance rank is not merely an imperfect guide to discriminative value but an actively misleading one, since a conventional 95%-variance cut would keep only PC0, which holds 97.1% of the variance and classifies at chance, and discard the entire low-variance tail that carries the label. The analyses are: **loading-vector analysis** (Figure 5), **variance decomposition by physical parameter** (Section 4.2), **reconstruction / selective component removal** (Table 5), and **explicit CH₄/O₃ projection onto the components** (Figure 4), together with a per-component discriminative-power analysis (Figure 3) that replaces the Pearson-correlation argument the reviewer rejected. These confirm the reviewer's expectation: no component isolates the label (the largest single-component AUC is 0.66), the two highest-variance components classify at chance and are removable without loss, and each molecule's imprint, while concentrated in only two or three components, spans the full variance range — 67% of the ozone imprint falls on the highest-variance component, the rest on a few components from the low-variance tail. The loading-vector analysis adds a result worth stating on its own: the low-variance loadings are recognizably molecular in shape — PC3 matches the methane imprint at |r| = 0.82 and PC4 matches ozone at 0.49 — yet only about 5% of PC3's variance and 6% of PC4's is driven by the molecule it resembles, the rest coming from the other parameters that project onto the same direction. A component can therefore look like methane without its score measuring methane, which is precisely the mixing of continuum, planetary and molecular contributions the reviewer described. I cite the established precedent that low-variance components can be important predictors (45). I have also added the spectral-domain complement to these component-domain analyses: the band-occlusion test of Section 4.3 (Table 6) shows which wavelength regions the fitted pipeline depends on, reported with the caveat that masked spectra are out of distribution for the frozen PCA basis, so it identifies dependence rather than intrinsic information content. Section 4.2 also now defines the spectral imprint and the effective participation ratio — the partial spectral response to each molecule, and the reciprocal of its summed squared projections onto the components — so the projection figures the reviewer asked for can be checked directly.

### R1-6 — Whitening does not selectively amplify chemistry

> **Reviewer wrote:** "This concern becomes even more significant following the whitening procedure. After PCA, every retained principal component is standardized to unit variance before training the neural network models. While this may improve optimization for gradient-based learning, it fundamentally changes the geometry of the feature space. Whitening does not selectively amplify chemically informative features. Instead, it amplifies every low-variance principal component equally, regardless of whether that component contains molecular absorption features, continuum information, numerical artifacts, simulator-specific structure, or measurement noise. Since each principal component is itself a linear combination of all original wavelengths, whitening rescales mixtures of physical and chemical information rather than independently equalizing 'physical' and 'chemical' features. Consequently, the mechanistic explanation presented in the manuscript—that whitening specifically increases the influence of chemically informative components—is not mathematically demonstrated."

**Response.** Conceded. The claim that whitening amplifies chemically informative components, and the "chemically relevant" language that went with it, have been removed. Whitening is now described only as what it is — a plain optimisation step for the gradient-based models that rescales every low-variance component equally, regardless of content (Section 3.2, Abstract). An on/off ablation establishes its real effect: XGBoost is unaffected, the MLP is equally good without it (dropping the two highest-variance components instead), and only the CNN genuinely needs it (Sections 3.2, 4.1, 4.2). Since the recommended model, XGBoost, uses no whitening at all, this concern does not reach the headline result — a point I now make explicitly.

### R1-7 — Conceptual inconsistency in the variance-ordering rationale

> **Reviewer wrote:** "More fundamentally, the manuscript appears to contain a conceptual inconsistency. It argues that PCA naturally orders the data such that dominant physical information resides in the first principal components while chemically relevant information occupies lower-variance components. However, the subsequent whitening operation deliberately removes precisely this variance hierarchy by forcing every retained component to have identical variance. If the PCA variance ordering reflects meaningful atmospheric physics, it is unclear why the subsequent learning algorithm should deliberately eliminate that ordering. Conversely, if equalizing all component variances improves classification, this suggests that explained variance itself may not correspond to discriminative importance for biosignature detection. These two interpretations cannot simultaneously serve as the mechanistic explanation for the reported performance. The manuscript should explicitly resolve this conceptual inconsistency through additional ablation studies comparing whitening, removal of leading principal components, supervised dimensionality reduction techniques such as Linear Discriminant Analysis or Partial Least Squares, and alternative feature weighting strategies. These experiments are necessary to determine whether whitening is compensating for limitations of PCA or whether PCA is genuinely isolating chemically meaningful structure."

**Response.** I adopt the reviewer's own resolution: explained variance is unsupervised while discriminative power is supervised, so there is no contradiction once I drop the claim that the variance ordering is physically meaningful (which R1-5 does). Section 4.2 now reports all four requested ablations: **whitening on/off**, **removal of leading principal components** (Table 5), **supervised dimensionality reduction** (PLS and LDA at matched dimensionality, holding the classifier fixed), and **alternative feature-weighting** schemes. The linear supervised methods plateau at 68–74% against roughly 89% for the nonlinear ensemble, which shows that PCA is an adequate basis rather than a transformation that isolates chemistry, and that the task is substantially nonlinear — exactly the distinction the reviewer asked me to establish.

### R1-8 — Whitening may reduce robustness on real data

> **Reviewer wrote:** "An equally important concern is that the whitening procedure may substantially reduce robustness to real observational data even if it improves performance within the synthetic simulation environment. Within TauREx, whitening appears to enhance classification by increasing the influence of low-variance components that contain discriminative methane and ozone information. However, because the entire pipeline is trained and evaluated on spectra generated by the same simulator, there is no evidence that these low-variance components represent exclusively chemically informative features. In real telescope observations, low-variance principal components are also expected to contain detector artifacts, calibration residuals, correlated instrumental noise, stellar contamination, imperfect molecular opacity models, and other observational effects that are absent or greatly simplified in the simulations. Whitening cannot distinguish chemically meaningful variance from these alternative sources of variability; it amplifies all low-variance components equally. Consequently, a preprocessing strategy that improves classification by amplifying subtle methane and ozone signatures in synthetic data may equally amplify observational noise and instrumental artifacts when deployed on real Ariel spectra. The present manuscript provides no evidence that whitening enhances physically meaningful biosignature information rather than simulator-specific variance. Addressing this issue requires experiments on independent simulation environments and, where possible, observational spectra to determine whether whitening genuinely improves out-of-distribution generalization rather than simply increasing performance within the synthetic training distribution."

**Response.** This was a valuable prediction, and my experiments confirm it. A controlled comparison of two otherwise-identical neural networks that differ *only* in whether the standardisation is applied (each trained five times to average over initialisation) shows that the standardised network begins marginally ahead on clean data but ends well behind under perturbation, the curves crossing by an effective SNR of 12 in four of five restarts (Section 4.6, "Whitening reduces robustness"). The same effect appears under a genuine change of forward model: moving to the independent Exo-Transmit code, the whitened network loses far more accuracy than the unwhitened one. The independent-simulation-environment test the reviewer named is therefore done; the observational-spectra half cannot yet be closed because no real Ariel data exists, which I state plainly (Section 5). This result reinforces my recommendation of XGBoost, which uses no whitening.

### R1-9 — Overall recommendation (major revision)

> **Reviewer wrote:** "Overall, I believe the manuscript contains a promising machine learning framework and an extensive computational evaluation. However, the central scientific questions remain unresolved. Specifically, the manuscript has not yet demonstrated that the classifier generalizes beyond the synthetic TauREx environment, that the PCA interpretation accurately reflects the underlying atmospheric physics, or that the whitening procedure preferentially enhances chemically meaningful information rather than simulator-specific low-variance structure. These are not matters of presentation but of experimental validation. Accordingly, I recommend major revision. Before the manuscript can be considered for publication, the authors should perform the additional validation experiments outlined above, including independent simulation studies, realistic domain-shift analyses, robustness testing under observational systematics, mechanistic analyses of the PCA representation, and targeted ablation studies of the whitening procedure. These experiments are necessary to establish that the reported performance reflects robust biosignature detection rather than successful classification within a single internally consistent synthetic simulation framework."

**Response.** I hope the revision meets the bar the reviewer set. Each of the three questions named here is now answered by experiment rather than by presentation: generalisation beyond TauREx by the independent-code and opacity-swap studies and the full seven-axis domain-shift characterisation (Section 4.6; R1-2, R1-3); the PCA interpretation by withdrawing the unsupported attribution and running the four mechanistic analyses (Section 4.2; R1-5); and the whitening procedure by the on/off and robustness ablations (Sections 3.2, 4.2, 4.6; R1-6, R1-7, R1-8). In keeping with the reviewer's point that these are matters of validation rather than wording, I have also narrowed the claims to exactly what the experiments support. The one item I could not complete this cycle — retrieval-derived labels — is conceded openly (R1-4, Section 5), and I would be glad to pursue it as the next stage of the work.

**Section 4.5 now quantifies the operating-point trade-off it previously only described.**

The section argued that the decision threshold would be moved in deployment — lowered for a recall-first pass over the Tier 3 catalogue, raised when down-selecting for Tier 4 or retrieval — without saying what any of those choices cost. A new **Figure 9** reports the precision–recall curve for XGBoost on the pooled test sets with candidate operating points marked. Raising recall to 95% requires lowering the threshold to 0.18 and costs eight points of precision (81.0%); 99% recall requires 0.03 and costs twenty (69.0%).

The same figure overlays the held-out planets re-rendered with an untrained-for cloud deck at 10⁴ Pa, and that comparison produced a result worth reporting in its own right: the thresholds themselves move. Reaching 95% recall under the deck requires a cut at 0.07 rather than 0.18 and returns 67.4% precision rather than 81.0%. A threshold selected on clean synthetic spectra would therefore deliver neither the recall nor the precision it was chosen for. This is the operational counterpart of the calibration finding in Section 4.4, and it is now noted in the Conclusion.

**Figure 6 has been re-drawn, and Section 3.1 now states a sampling limitation I found while preparing this revision.**

In checking the figure set against the delivered dataset, I found that the parameter draws are not mutually independent: planet radius, stellar temperature and semi-major axis are collinear (Pearson r ≥ 0.97), and planet mass, stellar radius and base pressure form a second coupled group (r ≥ 0.85). The six planetary and stellar parameters therefore explore roughly three independent directions rather than six. This was not intended, and arises in the generation step rather than from the sampling bounds of Table 1.

The previous version of Figure 6 plotted four parameters drawn from only two of those directions, so three of its six panels were degenerate diagonals carrying no information. It now plots the independent directions — planet radius, planet mass, and the measured per-spectrum feature amplitude — and every panel is informative. The new version also makes the amplitude relationship visible directly: amplitude rises with radius and falls with mass, as scale-height compression predicts, and the errors concentrate along the low-amplitude edge of the radius panel. The mass panel does not follow that pattern — the highest masses carry the weakest features yet show no excess error, while the excess sits at the lowest masses — so the gravity half of the scale-height argument is not supported by that axis. Section 4.3 now reports this discrepancy rather than presenting the mechanism as uniformly confirmed.

I have verified that the coupling does not affect the reported results. It does not reach the labelling chemistry: CH₄ and O₃ abundances are uncorrelated with the coupled directions (|r| < 0.02), and a classifier given the bulk parameters alone, with no spectrum, performs at chance (51.9% against a 50.3% base rate). It constrains which parameter combinations are represented — combinations such as a large planet around a cool star do not occur — rather than the accuracy reported. Section 4.3 accordingly now states that a physical dependence attributed to one parameter is a statement about the sampled direction that parameter belongs to rather than about that parameter in isolation, and Section 2 notes that the grid spans a broad range of properties but with correlated combinations.
