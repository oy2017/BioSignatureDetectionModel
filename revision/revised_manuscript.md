## Abstract

Characterizing exoplanetary atmospheres efficiently is essential for future survey missions such as Ariel, where only a subset of planets can receive the most detailed follow-up observations. Existing classification models have shown promise for this task but have predominantly been trained on low-resolution spectra, whereas Ariel anticipates higher-dimensional spectra for classification. There is therefore a need not only for models that can prioritize such synthetic spectra by biosignature-labelling status, but—since these models would inform how scarce follow-up time is spent—for a clear account of how far such a tool can be trusted before it is applied to real observations. Training data here derives from a single radiative transfer code, and the class labels are a deterministic function of the abundances used to generate them, so this work characterises the performance achievable under idealised conditions rather than demonstrating biosignature detection.

Synthetic spectra were simulated at a resolving power of R = 200 (550 spectral bins), matching the highest-precision benchmark mode (Tier 3) planned for Ariel, using MultiREx / TauREx 3; after invalid spectra were cleaned, four supervised classifiers—XGBoost, Random Forest, Multi-Layer Perceptron, and 1D Convolutional Neural Network—were trained on PCA-transformed spectral features. PCA reduced the 550-bin spectra to 102 components; a secondary standardisation of those components was applied for the gradient-based models, which are sensitive to the large disparity in component variance, while the tree ensembles are invariant to it.

The gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble (XGBoost) achieved the strongest overall performance, with a top mean accuracy of 88.17% (± 1.99%) and an F1-score of 88.24% (± 2.22%), decisively outperforming the Multi-Layer Perceptron (79.34% ± 1.47%) and the 1D-CNN (75.53% ± 1.05%) and holding a narrower, paired-test edge over the Random Forest (86.51% ± 1.96%). The tree ensembles exploited discriminative structure distributed across many low-variance components without rescaling, whereas the neural networks required explicit standardisation to remain competitive and still trailed. No single component isolates the biosignature signal: the two components carrying 98.4% of the spectral variance classify at chance and are removable without loss of accuracy. XGBoost produced the best-calibrated probability estimates among the models tested (Brier score 0.0861, with a near-diagonal reliability curve).

The central contribution of this work is a template for responsible evaluation: rather than reporting in-distribution accuracy alone, it characterises *when* and *how* a synthetic-trained triage classifier can be trusted—through a seven-axis map of its behaviour under domain shift and an explicit treatment of probability calibration, supported by an analysis of where its errors fall in physical parameter space. These results position XGBoost as a calibrated in-domain triage tool. Across the seven perturbation axes its accuracy degrades gracefully and traceably: three of the seven axes each reduce to a distinct mechanism—feature-amplitude suppression by aerosols, chromatic distortion by stellar contamination, and a decision-threshold bias under alternative opacity data—while the observational-noise axes act through the first, driving diagnostic features below the noise floor, with time-correlated systematics the dominant noise vulnerability and realistic Ariel instrument-noise coloring costing only about one accuracy point beyond white noise. Evaluating the frozen pipeline on spectra recomputed for the same planets with an independent radiative transfer code reduces accuracy by 4.1 points and an alternative molecular opacity compilation by 16.1; optically thick aerosols push it monotonically toward chance as feature amplitude vanishes, while calibration-type errors are tolerated. It is thus offered as a characterised triage benchmark—reliable within its training domain and with its limits measured rather than assumed—rather than as a biosignature detector for real observations.

## 1. Introduction

With more than 6,000 confirmed exoplanets discovered to date and major NASA and ESA research programs now explicitly focused on planetary habitability and the search for life, the characterization of exoplanetary atmospheres has become a central problem in modern observational astrobiology (1, 2). Identifying exoplanets capable of sustaining life requires the robust detection of atmospheric biosignatures—gases whose presence strongly implies a biological origin, but only when interpreted within sufficient geological, atmospheric, and stellar context, since many candidate biosignature gases can be produced or mimicked abiotically (3, 4). The upcoming Ariel (5, 6) mission accordingly aims not merely to identify individual planetary candidates but to build a comprehensive chemical census of exoplanetary atmospheres—an undertaking whose scale makes automated, and trustworthy, triage of its targets a practical necessity rather than a convenience.

The Ariel mission, launching in 2029, will survey approximately 1,000 transiting exoplanets using visible-to-infrared photometry and medium- and low-resolution infrared spectroscopy spanning 0.5–7.8 μm during a nominal four-year science mission (5). To optimize valuable telescope time, the mission employs a multi-tiered observation strategy: Tier 1 acts as a broad reconnaissance survey, Tier 2 conducts a deeper baseline chemical survey across hundreds of atmospheres, and Tier 3 is strictly reserved for the most detailed, high-resolution observations of exceptional targets (5, 7). Because the most detailed follow-up is highly resource-intensive, a rigorous triage process is required to rank the Tier 3 benchmark planets and determine which warrant dedicated Tier 4 phase-curve observations or follow-up with ground-based Extremely Large Telescopes (ELTs). By simulating data at R = 200, this study develops such a machine learning triage filter and, as importantly, evaluates how far it can be trusted before real observations of this caliber exist to test it. Without such empirical high-resolution spectra, models of this kind remain under active development, necessitating the use of synthetic data to build and stress-test these predictive algorithms in advance of the mission.

Existing exoplanet atmosphere studies have used transmission spectroscopy and high-resolution Doppler spectroscopy to detect molecular species such as H₂O, CO, and CH₄, with many of the strongest detections coming from large, close-in giant planets whose extended atmospheres produce stronger observational signals (8, 9). However, extending these techniques to smaller, rocky, potentially habitable planets remains difficult because their atmospheric signals are weaker and more easily affected by noise, clouds, stellar contamination, and observational time limits (3, 10). High-resolution spectroscopy offers a promising pathway because it can resolve molecular absorption into many individual spectral lines, producing molecule-specific patterns that can be isolated through Doppler shifts and cross-correlation techniques (9, 11). This makes HRS useful for identifying trace atmospheric species and helping reduce degeneracies when combined with lower-resolution spectra, although it does not by itself confirm biological activity (3, 11). It is emphasised here that the present work operates in a different regime: the R = 200 spectra studied here represent the high-resolution end of Ariel's low-to-medium-resolution survey, not the R ~ 10⁴–10⁵ Doppler-resolved spectroscopy described above. Throughout, "high-resolution" is used relative to the R ≈ 10–100 data used in prior biosignature-classification studies.

Although real exoplanet atmosphere spectra are publicly available, existing observations do not yet provide the kind of large, uniform, biosignature-labeled dataset needed for supervised biosignature-candidate screening. The NASA Exoplanet Archive's Atmospheric Spectroscopy Table compiles downloadable transmission, eclipse/emission, and direct-imaging spectra from peer-reviewed studies, but these observations vary across target type, facility, instrument, wavelength coverage, spectral resolution, and spectrum type (12). Much of the comparative broadband transmission-spectroscopy literature has focused on hot Jupiters and other large, highly irradiated planets because their stronger atmospheric signals make them easier to characterize; for example, Sing et al. (13) analyzed ten hot Jupiters across 0.3–5 μm, while the atmospheric properties of smaller rocky exoplanets remain largely unconstrained (14). While ground-based high-resolution spectroscopy has successfully detected molecules such as carbon monoxide in hot-Jupiter atmospheres, these observations are typically limited to favorable targets and do not yet provide broad coverage across planetary types or atmospheric compositions (9). Ariel will address this problem at the population level by obtaining a large, homogeneous survey of exoplanet atmospheres across a broad visible-to-infrared wavelength range, enabling comparative studies of atmospheric composition, clouds, and thermal structure (5, 7). Because Ariel-like surveys and synthetic spectral grids can be large and computationally demanding to analyze, they motivate the development of efficient machine-learning methods that can assist atmospheric retrieval and classification (15, 16).

In this work, a machine learning framework is designed that recovers a predefined abundance-threshold biosignature labelling from high-resolution synthetic transmission spectra representative of an Ariel-like dataset. Because the labels are computed directly from the abundances supplied to the forward model, the classification task is the recovery of a predefined labelling convention from spectra, not the inference of abundances from observations; the latter is what Ariel will ultimately require, via retrieval, with its attendant uncertainty, parameter degeneracy, and abiotic alternatives. To generate these data, the MultiREx library is used, an open-source Python framework that leverages the TauREx 3 radiative transfer code to rapidly simulate planetary transits and model molecular absorption and scattering (17, 18). These high-dimensional spectra are processed using a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) pipeline for dimensionality reduction. In evaluating this framework, balanced classification performance and probability calibration are emphasised rather than any single metric, while noting that the operating threshold can later be tuned to prioritize recall—minimizing the risk of discarding potentially habitable worlds—at deployment (Section 4.5). This is supported by an extensive analysis of model error rates across diverse physical parameter spaces (e.g., planetary radius and stellar temperature).

The closest precedent for this work is Duque-Castaño et al. (17), who used MultiREx-generated synthetic spectra to classify low-SNR, low-resolution transmission spectra of Earth-like/TRAPPIST-1e-style planets as biosignature candidates, framing the task as triage for follow-up rather than full retrieval. This work adopts their MultiREx-based generation, their CH₄/O₃ chemistry, and their candidate-screening framing. Where that and related work report in-distribution accuracy, however, the question that decides whether such a tool can actually be used is left open: when, and how, does it fail? Answering that is the aim of this work. The trained pipeline's robustness is characterised under seven axes of domain shift—an independent radiative transfer code, an alternative molecular opacity compilation, clouds and hazes, physical stellar contamination, spectral resolution and signal-to-noise, radiometric instrument noise, and out-of-envelope extrapolation—and its degradation is shown to be graceful and traceable rather than opaque. Two further elements underpin this evaluation: the analysis works at a simulated resolving power of R = 200, representative of Ariel's Tier 3 benchmark, where each spectrum spans hundreds of correlated wavelength channels and dimensionality reduction becomes essential rather than optional; and a controlled, like-for-like comparison of four architectures across two families—two tree-based ensembles (Random Forest and gradient-boosted XGBoost) and two neural networks (an MLP and a 1D-CNN)—is run on a single shared PCA-derived feature space, isolating model family from preprocessing. The resulting advantage of tree ensembles over neural networks on PCA-projected tabular features is itself a well-established pattern (19, 20); the contribution here is not that result but the calibrated, robustness-characterised evaluation built around it. Together these establish not a validated detector but a calibrated benchmark whose reliability and failure modes are mapped—the bar such a tool must clear before it can be trusted on real, heterogeneous spectra.

**Contributions.** Stated concretely, this work contributes:

1. **A domain-shift robustness map** for a synthetic-trained biosignature-triage classifier: the frozen pipeline is stress-tested across the seven perturbation axes named above, and its degradation is shown to be graceful and to reduce to a few identifiable mechanisms rather than opaque failure.
2. **Probability calibration as a first-class evaluation criterion**, measured and reported alongside accuracy, because a triage tool's confidence estimates—not only its labels—drive how scarce follow-up time is allocated.

Supporting both, a physics-based error analysis locates where the in-domain failures fall: predominantly at low feature amplitude and near the labelling thresholds, which is where weak or ambiguous signal is expected. It is offered as corroborating evidence rather than as an independent contribution, since it accounts for part of the in-domain error structure rather than all of it (Section 4.3).

In this paper, Section 2 outlines the state of existing machine learning research for exoplanet habitability and makes the case for high-resolution ensemble classification; Section 3 describes the synthetic dataset, its assumptions, the PCA-based feature extraction, the four model architectures, and the validation framework; Section 4 presents the results, including a rigorous statistical comparison using McNemar's tests, a physics-based error analysis, and a characterisation of robustness under seven axes of domain shift. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper by summarizing key findings and outlining avenues for future research.

## 2. Background

Previous work on exoplanet atmospheric analysis has often focused on atmospheric retrieval, where models infer physical and chemical properties such as molecular abundances, cloud opacity, and temperature from observed spectra. Traditional Bayesian retrieval methods are powerful because they produce posterior probability distributions rather than simple labels, but they can also be computationally expensive, especially when the parameter space is large or highly degenerate. Márquez-Neila et al. (21) showed that supervised machine learning could reduce this computational burden by training a Random Forest model on a precomputed grid of atmospheric models. Their approach retrieved atmospheric parameters from exoplanet spectra more quickly than standard retrieval methods while preserving some interpretability through feature importance. However, this type of retrieval-focused method is designed to estimate atmospheric parameters, not necessarily to serve as a rapid binary screening tool for prioritizing biosignature follow-up observations.

More recent studies have explored deep learning and other machine learning approaches for exoplanet spectra, especially in the context of Ariel-like datasets. Yip et al. (22) investigated how deep learning models can be interpreted when applied to exoplanet atmospheric retrievals, highlighting both their predictive power and the concern that neural networks often function as "black boxes." Their work is relevant to this study because it shows that model evaluation should go beyond accuracy alone: researchers must also understand when models fail, which spectral regions influence predictions, and whether predictions are physically meaningful. Similarly, the Ariel Data Challenge and related studies have emphasized that machine learning can help analyze large synthetic spectral datasets, but many of these approaches focus on retrieval or posterior estimation rather than the narrower task of identifying spectra that deserve follow-up observation. This interpretability concern is directly relevant to the present work and is discussed further in Section 4.3, which examines how model errors map onto physical parameter space as a means of partially validating the physical plausibility of model predictions.

The most directly related work is Duque-Castaño et al. (17), who developed a machine-learning framework for classifying low-SNR synthetic transmission spectra according to whether they contained potential biosignatures or bioindicators such as methane, ozone, and water. Their study used MultiREx to generate large numbers of synthetic spectra and framed machine learning as a way to identify "interesting" planets for follow-up, rather than as a replacement for detailed atmospheric retrieval. This is especially important because biosignature searches are limited by telescope time: a model that can quickly flag promising targets could make future observing campaigns more efficient. However, their study was limited to a specific Earth-like/TRAPPIST-1e-style setup using low-resolution spectra, and did not directly compare the same set of models across the broader parameter space or the higher-resolution regime anticipated for next-generation surveys such as Ariel. Building on these approaches, this project evaluates tree-based ensembles, neural networks, and PCA-based feature extraction as a supervised classification pipeline for synthetic transmission spectra, with particular attention to recall and error patterns across physical parameter space.

## 3. Methods

This study designs a machine learning pipeline that recovers a predefined abundance-threshold biosignature labelling from high-resolution transmission spectra representative of an Ariel-like dataset. A suite of machine learning models is assessed on mock data simulated via the MultiREx library, using the following methodologies.

### 3.1 Dataset and simulation parameters

The synthetic dataset of atmospheric transmission spectra was generated using the MultiREx (v0.3.1) Python library, which leverages the TauREx 3 radiative transfer code (18). TauREx 3 is a modular framework designed to simulate the transit of starlight through a planetary atmosphere by integrating the Beer–Lambert law across discrete atmospheric layers. It incorporates a wide range of physical effects, including pressure-broadened molecular absorption, Rayleigh scattering, and collision-induced absorption (CIA), to produce physically consistent transmission spectra. While TauREx 3 is highly robust, its accuracy is fundamentally limited by the underlying molecular cross-section data and the assumption of a 1D, spherically symmetric atmosphere (18, 23). The molecular cross-sections used to generate every spectrum in this work are the Exo-Transmit opacity compilation distributed with MultiREx (24, 25, 26); this source is named explicitly because Section 4.6 shows that substituting an alternative compilation is the single largest source of out-of-domain error, so the choice is not incidental to reproducibility. Furthermore, certain edge cases in the parameter space—such as atmospheres with extremely high base pressures or those with no significant absorption features—can occasionally result in numerical instability or physically implausible transit depths (> 1.0). To maintain the integrity of the dataset, these invalid spectra were identified and removed during the data-cleaning phase described below.

The parameters for the dataset used in this study were drawn from the Atmospheric Big Challenge (ABC) Database (Tier 2) introduced by Changeat and Yip (15), and the data were generated using the TauREx 3 radiative transfer code (18). A training set of 2,696 simulated planetary spectra was generated (reduced from 3,000 after removing spectra with physically impossible transit depths > 1.0 or NaN values), as well as five independent testing sets, each containing approximately 540 clean spectra (2,697 in total). Each sample contained a one-dimensional transmission spectrum (transit depth as a function of wavelength) with 550 spectral bins spanning wavelengths from 0.5 to 7.8 micrometers at a spectral resolution of R = 200, with a constant signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 15 applied to all observations, following the low-SNR transmission-spectrum framework explored by Duque-Castaño et al. (17). By adopting a uniform R = 200 grid, the synthetic dataset approximates the upper end of Ariel's Tier 3 benchmark resolution (R ≈ 200 in AIRS-CH0, with lower native resolution in the other channels), allowing prioritization strategies to be tested on data approaching the highest quality the mission will produce. This is an idealization: the real mission delivers heterogeneous resolution across its instruments, which is not modelled here.

For supervised classification, binary labels were assigned based on the presence of a specific biosignature pair, methane and ozone, following the binary classification framework described by Duque-Castaño et al. (17). A spectrum was labeled as a "Biosignature" case only if both gases were present above predefined abundance thresholds:

- Methane: log mixing ratio > −6.0
- Ozone: log mixing ratio > −7.0

All planets that did not meet both conditions simultaneously were labeled as "Non-Biosignature." In addition to the spectra, several physical parameters—including planetary radius, stellar temperature, and the abundances of other trace gases (e.g., H₂O, CO₂, CO)—were simultaneously varied along the pre-defined grid outlined in Table 1.

Because the labels are computed directly from the abundances supplied to the forward model, the classification task is the recovery of a predefined labelling convention from spectra, not the inference of abundances from observations. Ariel will require the latter, via retrieval, with the attendant uncertainty, parameter degeneracy, and abiotic alternatives. The particular cutoffs are a convention inherited from prior work rather than a physical boundary. Moving both cutoffs together by up to half a dex and relabelling changes overall accuracy by less than two points (86.99% to 88.91%), so the reported performance is not an artefact of the specific values chosen; the advantage over a majority-class baseline is largest at the original cutoffs, where the class balance enforced by the generation plan holds.

To guarantee a balanced dataset (50% positive, 50% negative) and prevent majority-class bias, a stratified generation plan consisting of four disjoint composition profiles was enforced:

1. **Biosignature Profile (50.0% of dataset):** CH₄ is drawn log-uniformly from [−6.0, −3.0], and O₃ from [−7.0, −1.0] (both gases strictly above thresholds).
2. **Non-Bio Methane Profile (16.66%):** CH₄ from [−6.0, −3.0], O₃ from [−10.0, −7.0] (methane only).
3. **Non-Bio Ozone Profile (16.66%):** CH₄ from [−9.0, −6.0], O₃ from [−7.0, −1.0] (ozone only).
4. **Non-Bio None Profile (16.68%):** CH₄ from [−9.0, −6.0], O₃ from [−10.0, −7.0] (both below thresholds).

By simulating these specific, disjoint profiles using the `explore_multiverse` function in MultiREx, the overall abundance boundaries across all planets are made to span the full [−9.0, −3.0] and [−10.0, −1.0] grids while maintaining a highly balanced, robust training split. All other physical parameters (planet radius, mass, stellar temperatures, and background gas abundances) were drawn from the absolute bounds outlined in Table 1 across all four profiles. Each is uniform over its tabulated range, but the draws are not mutually independent of one another; the structure this imposes on the sampled space, and its consequences for the analyses below, are set out in Section 3.5.

### 3.2 Preprocessing and Feature Engineering

To account for differences in scale across planets and host stars, Z-score standardization was applied to the raw spectral flux values. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was then used to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset. PCA is an orthogonal linear transformation that projects the data into a new coordinate system via the eigendecomposition of the data's covariance matrix, producing uncorrelated components ordered by explained variance.

Following the PCA transformation, a secondary standardization step was applied to the retained components using a StandardScaler fit on the training set and applied to all test sets, rescaling the 102 components to zero mean and unit variance. This standardisation places all retained components on a common scale. It is an optimisation remedy for the gradient-based architectures, which are dominated by the two highest-variance components in its absence; it makes no distinction between components on the basis of their content, rescaling every low-variance component equally regardless of whether it carries molecular absorption, continuum structure, or noise. The tree ensembles are unaffected: XGBoost returns identical metrics with and without this step at every component range tested, confirming the expected invariance of node splitting to monotonic per-feature rescaling. The step is also substitutable: discarding the two highest-variance components without standardisation achieves the same MLP accuracy as standardising the full set (both near 79%), addressing the same conditioning problem by another route. An ablation over the full component set confirms the step's role for the neural networks that retain the dominant components: removing the standardisation lowers neural-network accuracy substantially (MLP 79.34% → 74.81%; 1D-CNN 75.53% → 64.51%), while the scale-invariant tree models are unaffected (Section 4.2).

The final feature set retained the first 102 principal components. These components preserve the dominant variation in the spectra while reducing computational cost and limiting overfitting. To validate that these 102 components are sufficient to reconstruct the spectra, Figure 1 illustrates a PCA reconstruction and residual error across wavelengths for a representative test spectrum. The top panel demonstrates that a spectrum reconstructed using these principal components recovers all major molecular absorption features. The bottom panel confirms this, showing a flat, low-magnitude fractional residual error centered at zero, indicating that the chosen number of components provides a near-lossless compression of the spectrum.

### 3.3 Machine Learning Architectures

Two families of machine learning models—tree-based ensembles and neural networks—were evaluated, spanning four architectures to determine which approach was best suited for this spectral classification task. These models were selected to cover a spectrum of complexity, from robust ensemble methods capable of handling non-linear decision boundaries to deep neural networks that can automatically learn hierarchical feature representations.

Tree-based ensembles were included as established nonlinear supervised-learning baselines: Random Forests are robust ensembles of decision trees, while XGBoost is a scalable gradient-boosted tree method widely used in machine-learning applications (28, 29). Because Random Forest methods have also been applied to exoplanet atmospheric spectra, they provide a relevant benchmark for this type of spectral analysis (21). Prior work on exoplanet transmission spectra has shown that spectral data are highly correlated and can be represented using lower-dimensional principal-component representations, supporting the use of PCA-transformed inputs (30, 31). An MLP was included as a fully connected neural-network baseline for learning nonlinear relationships among the PCA components (32). Finally, the 1D-CNN was included because convolutional models have been applied to spectroscopic exoplanet data to detect molecular patterns in the spectral dimension and handle challenging noise conditions (33).

#### 3.3.1 Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (XGBoost)

An ensemble of decision trees was trained using the XGBoost framework. This approach was selected for its ability to model complex, non-linear relationships and to perform well on difficult cases near the detection thresholds (29). The grid search evaluated learning rates of 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2, maximum tree depths of 3, 5, 7, and 10, estimator counts of 100, 200, and 300, and subsample ratios of 0.8 and 1.0. The optimal configuration was determined to be 200 estimators with a maximum tree depth of 5, a subsample ratio of 0.8, and a learning rate of 0.2, utilizing a standard log-loss objective function.

#### 3.3.2 Random Forest

A Random Forest classifier was evaluated as an additional tree-based ensemble approach. Implemented using scikit-learn (34), this model constructs a multitude of decision trees during training, each on a random subset of the data and features, and outputs the consensus prediction. This approach is highly robust against overfitting and effectively captures complex, non-linear relationships in high-dimensional spectral data. The hyperparameters for the Random Forest model were optimized using an exhaustive grid search with stratified 3-fold cross-validation, encompassing 108 distinct combinations across estimator counts of 100, 200, and 300, maximum depths of 10, 20, 30, or unbounded (None), minimum sample requirements of 2, 5, or 10 for node splitting, and 1, 2, or 4 samples per leaf. The model maximized performance using 300 decision trees with unbounded depth, requiring at least 2 samples per leaf and 2 samples per internal node split.

#### 3.3.3 Deep Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP)

Training was performed for up to 100 epochs and controlled dynamically using early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs based on validation loss. The MLP grid evaluated hidden layer configurations of 256-128, 256-128-64, and 512-256-128-64 neurons, dropout rates within 0.2 and 0.4, batch sizes within 32, 64, and 128, and learning rates of 0.001 and 0.0005. The winning configuration utilized a three-layer funnel with 256, 128, and 64 neurons, respectively. Each dense layer was followed by Batch Normalization to stabilize training, a ReLU activation function, and a 0.3 dropout rate to mitigate overfitting (32). The network was optimized using the Adam algorithm with a learning rate of 0.001 and trained in batches of 128 (35).

#### 3.3.4 Convolutional Neural Networks (1D-CNN)

To explore end-to-end learning directly from spectra, convolutional neural networks were initially trained on the raw, non-PCA spectral data. Both standard 1D CNNs and deeper 1D ResNet-style architectures with residual connections were tested. The standard 1D-CNN consisted of two convolutional layers with filters of 32 and 64 respectively. Each convolutional layer was followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, 1D max pooling, and dropout (p = 0.3). To evaluate deeper feature extraction, a ResNet architecture was implemented employing residual blocks with skip connections (36) to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, utilizing a Gaussian smoothing step (kernel size 3) prior to scaling to reduce high-frequency noise. Ultimately, models trained directly on the raw high-dimensional spectra failed to converge on generalizable features, yielding performance akin to random guessing (~50% accuracy). However, when the 1D-CNN was instead trained on the PCA-transformed feature space (specifically components 0–101), performance improved dramatically, validating the necessity of dimensionality reduction for datasets of this size.

### 3.4 Validation Framework

Model performance was evaluated using a hybrid validation approach dictated by the data requirements of the respective architectures. For the tree-based ensemble models (XGBoost and Random Forest), hyperparameters were systematically optimized using an exhaustive grid search with stratified 3-fold cross-validation. A 3-fold split was specifically chosen over higher fold counts (e.g., 10-fold CV) to balance robust internal validation while maintaining a sufficiently large training set per fold. However, initial cross-validation trials with the deep neural networks (MLP and 1D-CNN) revealed severe underfitting due to data starvation when fragmenting the relatively small dataset (2,696 samples). Maximizing the size of the training set was critical for stable deep learning performance. Therefore, the neural network architectures were optimized and evaluated using a single-split validation approach, with 80% of the data reserved for training and 20% for validation and early stopping.

To assess robustness, five disjoint, independently generated test sets (~540 spectra each, with no overlap with the training set or with one another) were evaluated; the PCA basis and StandardScaler were fit on the training set only and applied unchanged to each. Each model was evaluated on all five sets, and the mean and standard deviation of four key metrics—Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score—are reported in Section 4. Finally, a physics-based analysis was conducted by examining how classification errors varied with physical parameters such as stellar temperature and gas abundance, allowing the identification of regions in parameter space where the models were most likely to fail.

All data generation, modeling, and evaluations were performed under a unified software environment using Python v3.10.6. The synthetic spectra were generated using MultiREx v0.3.1 built on the TauREx v3.3.2 radiative transfer engine. The tree-based ensembles were developed using scikit-learn v1.7.2 (for Random Forest) and XGBoost v3.2.0. The deep learning architectures (MLP and 1D-CNN) were compiled and trained using TensorFlow v2.21.0 and scikeras v0.13.0 wrappers. To guarantee absolute algorithmic reproducibility across all partitions, initializations, and cross-validation folds, a global seed lock was enforced throughout (SEED = 42).

## 4. Results and Discussion

### 4.1 Overall Performance and Model Hierarchy

To ensure a fair comparison, all four architectures were trained and evaluated using the unified 102-component feature space (indices 0–101). Under these identical conditions, the gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble (XGBoost) demonstrated the strongest overall performance, achieving a top mean accuracy of 88.17% (± 1.99%) and an F1-score of 88.24% (± 2.22%). XGBoost decisively outperformed the Multi-Layer Perceptron (79.34% ± 1.47%) and the 1D-CNN (75.53% ± 1.05%), and held a narrower, paired-test edge over the Random Forest (86.51% ± 1.96%) that did not reach significance on an aggregate bootstrap comparison. This gap is attributed to the way each model family handles the multi-scale variance structure of the PCA representation. As Section 4.2 shows, the two highest-variance components carry 98.41% of the spectral variance but classify at chance when used alone, while label-discriminative structure is distributed across the low-variance components. Tree-based models, whose node-splitting is invariant to per-feature scale, exploit those low-variance components natively, with no rescaling required. The neural networks cannot: without the secondary per-component standardization, they are dominated by the high-variance components and their accuracy falls to 74.81% (MLP) and 64.51% (1D-CNN). The whitening step rescues them by placing all components on a common scale—raising the MLP to 79.34% and the 1D-CNN to 75.53%—but does not close the gap to the trees. This residual deficit is consistent with the broader finding that tree ensembles outperform neural networks on structured, medium-sized tabular feature spaces (19, 20), and is likely compounded by the modest size of the training set (2,696 samples), which constrained the neural architectures (Section 3.4). The trees' scale-invariance thus makes them robust to a variance structure that the neural networks can accommodate only with explicit whitening, and even then less effectively.

To rigorously evaluate the statistical significance of these results, a dual-validation approach was employed. First, pairwise McNemar's tests (37) were conducted on the models' predictions to evaluate paired classification agreement. These tests confirmed that the gradient-boosted decision trees (XGBoost) maintain a highly statistically significant predictive advantage over both the Random Forest model (p = 0.0084) and the MLP (p = 0.0002). While the Random Forest and MLP are statistically similar to each other (p = 0.0818), both architectures demonstrated a highly statistically significant advantage over the 1D-CNN (p < 0.0001), confirming the CNN's position at the bottom of the predictive hierarchy in this high-resolution feature space. However, to verify that these findings remain robust when evaluating the balance of precision and recall across the aggregate population, a bootstrapping analysis (38) with 10,000 iterations was performed to calculate the 95% confidence intervals for the F1-scores. This aggregate analysis revealed that the F1-score confidence intervals of the two top-performing ensembles (XGBoost and Random Forest) exhibit an overlap, with the F1-score difference being statistically non-significant at the population level (p = 0.1924).

This divergence between McNemar's paired test (which showed highly significant case-by-case XGBoost superiority at p = 0.0084) and the bootstrap F1 test is a known statistical phenomenon. While the bootstrap test operates on aggregate, unpaired metrics and introduces sampling variance that dilutes minor differences, McNemar's test evaluates paired case-by-case disagreements. This indicates that, although the aggregate population F1-scores are statistically close, on a planet-by-planet basis XGBoost's predictive edge over Random Forest is systematic and statistically significant (p < 0.01); XGBoost is therefore treated as the most robust candidate for mission triage. (These pairwise comparisons are not corrected for multiple testing, and the XGBoost–Random Forest advantage—statistically significant on the paired McNemar test but not on the aggregate bootstrap F1 comparison—is therefore best read as suggestive rather than decisive.)

### 4.2 The Role of PCA in Feature Extraction

Dimensionality reduction was necessary for the neural architectures, and the location of label-discriminative structure within the reduced space is characterised below. While the best-performing models utilized PCA-transformed data, the 1D-CNN trained on raw spectral data performed significantly worse, with the raw CNN peaking at only 50.56% (± 1.58%) accuracy across the five independent test sets.

The contribution of PCA can be decomposed into two distinct steps. Trained on the raw 550-bin spectra, the 1D-CNN failed to learn generalizable features, reaching only 50.56% (± 1.58%) accuracy—barely above chance. Projecting onto the 102 principal components raised it to 64.51% (± 0.79%), and the subsequent per-component standardisation raised it further to 75.53% (± 1.05%). The same step lifted the MLP from 74.81% (± 2.61%) to 79.34% (± 1.47%). Dimensionality reduction and variance equalization thus contribute separately to neural-network performance, while the scale-invariant tree models—unaffected by either standardization—already operate near their ceiling on the PCA features.

An earlier interpretation of this feature space is not adopted here: principal components are orthogonal linear combinations of all wavelength channels and do not correspond to individual atmospheric processes, so the leading components are not attributed to physical structure and the remainder to chemical absorption. The analysis is instead restricted to the location of label-discriminative information, which is directly measurable. PC0 remains a useful descriptive landmark—it is a near-perfect proxy for the mean transit depth (r = 0.9998)—but this describes PC0 alone and is not used to infer that chemistry is absent from it.

No individual principal component is strongly discriminative: the maximum single-feature AUC across all 102 components is 0.663 on the training set (0.654 on held-out data), while the component carrying 97.1% of the variance is indistinguishable from chance (AUC 0.506) and the next (AUC 0.530) is no more informative than the average low-variance component (mean AUC 0.531 across components 2 to 101). Classification performance arises from aggregating many weakly informative components rather than from a few dominant ones. Fourteen of the twenty most discriminative components fall outside the twenty highest-variance components, and retaining only the two highest-variance components yields 52.13% accuracy—chance, on balanced classes—while discarding them costs nothing (88.58% → 88.25%). Variance rank and discriminative rank are therefore substantially decoupled in this feature space, as Figure 3 shows component by component.

The molecular signals are not isolated in the low-variance "chemistry" tail either. Projecting each molecule's spectral imprint onto the components (Figure 4) shows that the imprints are in fact concentrated in only a few components—an effective participation ratio of about two—but not where the earlier interpretation placed them. Sixty-seven percent of the ozone imprint and roughly fifteen percent of the methane imprint fall on PC0, the highest-variance component the earlier interpretation had called purely physical; the remainder sits in a handful of low-variance components (methane mostly in PC3 and PC2, ozone in PC4) rather than spread across all of PCs 2–101. This is consistent with PC0 classifying at chance: ozone's imprint is a negligible fraction of PC0's total variance, which is dominated by the bulk planetary parameters that set the mean transit depth (principally planet mass and base pressure, with planet and stellar radius), all held uncorrelated with the biosignature label by the generation plan—so PC0 tracks planetary bulk properties rather than the label even while it absorbs most of ozone's continuum-level shift. No component is a clean single-molecule channel either: even the most methane-associated component (PC3) has only about 5% of its variance driven by methane, and the loading vectors (Figure 5), while interpretable in gross shape (a nearly flat leading component, a spectral slope in the next, molecular-band-like shapes in several low-variance components), match no molecule cleanly. The earlier draft's attribution—PC0/PC1 physical, chemistry confined to PCs 2–101—is therefore refuted from both sides: a large share of the chemistry leaks into PC0 because these molecules shift the continuum level, and where the chemistry does sit in the tail it occupies a few specific components rather than the whole of it. (A separate accounting of where each molecule *drives* variance, summed across components, spreads over roughly thirty to forty principal components at individually low weight; this and the concentrated imprint are consistent, being different normalisations of the same non-isolation.)

To test whether the variance ordering is an appropriate selection criterion, principal components were compared against a supervised projection at matched dimensionality, holding the classifier fixed. Restricted to two components, partial least squares reaches 60.70% against 52.13% for the two highest-variance principal components. Beyond approximately five components the two projections become indistinguishable, and at 102 components they differ by 0.33 percentage points. Principal component analysis is therefore an adequate basis that retains the discriminative information once sufficient dimensions are kept, rather than a transformation that isolates it. That the task is intrinsically nonlinear is confirmed directly: linear classifiers on the same 102 features plateau far below the trees—linear discriminant analysis at 68.2% and linear PLS-DA at 73.9%, against roughly 89% for gradient-boosted XGBoost—as expected from the label itself, which is positive only where methane and ozone both exceed their thresholds, a conjunction whose positive region is not linearly separable. The discriminative structure must therefore be recovered by combining many components nonlinearly rather than read from any single linear projection. A further ablation weighted the 102 components by alternative schemes with the classifier held fixed: XGBoost is essentially unchanged across all of them (88.0–88.3%, consistent with the near scale-invariance of tree splits to per-feature weighting), and for the neural network no scheme improves on the standardisation—weighting each component by its discriminative power is comparable to it, while emphasising the high-variance components collapses accuracy (MLP 59.4%). No re-weighting isolates a better signal, consistent with the standardisation being optimisation conditioning rather than signal selection. Reporting the single-feature and ablation analyses together is necessary because either alone misleads: univariately every component is weak (mean AUC 0.518 for the leading pair versus 0.531 for the tail), but multivariately the leading pair is inert while the tail is collectively strong—single-feature AUC cannot see interactions, and the ablation cannot resolve individual components. Low-variance principal components acting as important predictors is a long-recognised phenomenon in principal-component regression (39).

### 4.3 Error Analysis and Physical Dependencies

To understand model failure modes, a physics-based analysis was conducted by examining how classification errors varied with physical parameters. A summary of the parameter ranges explored is provided in Table 1. Figure 6 presents a triangle plot of planet and stellar parameters (planet radius, planet mass, star temperature, atmospheric temperature), with data points colored by correct versus incorrect test-set predictions; Figure 7 provides a plot of classification errors against the atmospheric mixing ratios of CH₄ and O₃.

Accuracy is highest for planets in the interior of the sampled parameter ranges and falls toward both extremes. Predictive performance is weakest in two specific regimes. First, the models struggled with planets possessing low atmospheric temperatures and small radii (and thus high surface gravities): binning the pooled test set into quintiles, the error rate is 14.6% for the coldest fifth of atmospheres and 15.7% for the smallest fifth of planets, against 7.4–11.1% in the middle quintiles. This aligns with physical intuition: cooler temperatures and higher gravities compress the atmospheric scale height, which flattens the transmission spectrum and mutes the amplitude of diagnostic chemical absorption features beneath the noise floor. Binning instead by the measured per-spectrum feature amplitude reproduces the effect directly—14.8% error in the lowest fifth against 8.0% in the middle—so amplitude suppression is the dominant driver at the low-amplitude end, where the error rate nearly doubles. It does not, however, account for the whole in-domain error structure: errors rise again at the opposite extreme, to 14.3% for the hottest fifth of atmospheres and 14.1% for the largest fifth of planets, so the dependence on each physical parameter is U-shaped rather than monotonic and the edges of the sampled envelope are harder than its interior for reasons the scale-height argument does not explain. Second, misclassifications clustered heavily around the predefined biosignature boundaries (log[CH₄] ≈ −6.0 and log[O₃] ≈ −7.0). This highlights the inherent difficulty of classifying marginal, low-abundance cases in low-SNR (SNR = 15) data, where atmospheric signals are easily masked by simulated instrument noise. The two regimes are distinct in origin: the first is suppression of feature amplitude relative to the noise, the mechanism that also governs the aerosol degradations quantified in Section 4.6, whereas the second is a limit of the labelling convention rather than of the spectra, since near the cutoff the atmospheres being separated are physically near-identical—as the margin analysis below quantifies.

This dependence is quantified directly. Binning the test set by how far a planet's abundances lie from the labelling cutoff, accuracy rises from 61.9% within 0.25 dex of the cutoff to 95.3% at distances beyond one dex. Because class balance varies between bins, each bin is compared against its own majority-class baseline: in the nearest bin the classifier does not exceed that baseline at all, and its mean predicted probability is 0.515. Within a quarter-dex of the cutoff the labelling separates atmospheres that are physically near-identical, so no spectrum can recover the distinction; the errors concentrated there reflect the arbitrariness of a threshold convention rather than a limitation of the classifier. This regime accounts for 8.8% of the test set. The converse also follows: because abundances are drawn uniformly across several decades, 64.8% of test planets lie at least one dex from the cutoff, where the deciding abundance differs by more than an order of magnitude between the classes. Overall accuracy is therefore weighted toward well-separated cases, and the headline figure should be read alongside this breakdown—the method separates chemically distinct atmospheres reliably and approaches the labelling convention's own resolution limit as the distinction narrows.

**Model limitations.** The framework uses a binary classification approach, optimized for rapid target filtering but lacking the quantitative depth of traditional retrieval methods. Unlike Bayesian frameworks that provide full posterior probability distributions and chemical abundance estimates (40), the present models provide a discrete label that cannot convey statistically rigorous confidence intervals for individual molecular species. Furthermore, the reliance on a fixed labelling pair (CH₄/O₃) may be susceptible to "false-positive" configurations; for instance, high-metallicity atmospheres can exhibit spectral features that mimic the flat baseline of cloudy terrestrial worlds, potentially leading to misclassifications in the absence of a multi-label regression framework (41).

**Simulation limitations.** A primary limitation is the reliance on synthetic data generated via TauREx 3, which assumes a 1-D, spherically symmetric atmospheric profile. Real exoplanetary atmospheres are inherently 3-D environments characterized by complex day–night temperature gradients, inhomogeneous cloud coverage, and photochemical hazes (42). These effects introduce spectral asymmetries and systematic biases not captured in 1-D radiative transfer models, and the domain-shift experiments in Section 4.6 quantify how far several of them move the classifier's accuracy. The costs of these idealisations are therefore measured rather than merely acknowledged, but they remain idealisations, and performance on real, heterogeneous data may differ from the in-domain figures reported here (43).

### 4.4 Probability Calibration and Mission Triage Reliability

As established, the ultimate goal of developing these machine learning models is to inform automated triage for missions such as Ariel. Ariel will employ a hierarchical observational strategy to optimize telescope time: Tier 1 conducts a broad reconnaissance of roughly 1,000 planets, Tier 2 provides a deeper chemical survey for a subset of approximately 500 atmospheres, and Tiers 3 and 4 are strictly reserved for repeated, high-resolution benchmark observations and phase curves of the most exceptional targets (5, 44). Tier 3 provides high-resolution (R ≈ 100–200) benchmark observations for a select sample of 50 to 100 planets. Because both Tier 4 telescope time and the supercomputing hours required for full Tier 3 Bayesian atmospheric retrievals are finite resources, it is critical to aggressively prioritize the high-resolution Tier 3 catalog. When selecting a machine learning model to serve as this intra-Tier 3 prioritization filter, the decision depends heavily on the operational cost of classification errors.

When evaluated across the five independent test sets, the two tree-based ensembles—XGBoost (88.17%) and Random Forest (86.51%)—emerged as the top two models in overall predictive accuracy. Their operational reliability, however, diverges. As a primary measure of calibration, the reliability diagram (Figure 8) is used, comparing predicted confidence against empirical outcome frequency independently of raw accuracy. It is computed on all five test sets pooled (2,697 planets) and binned by equal counts rather than equal width: XGBoost's predicted probabilities are strongly bimodal, with roughly a third of planets below 0.05 and a third above 0.95, so equal-width bins would leave only a handful of planets in the mid-range and the resulting scatter would be binomial noise rather than miscalibration. With ~270 planets in every bin, XGBoost's curve tracks the diagonal across the whole probability range, no bin departing from its own predicted rate by more than 0.08. The Random Forest curve is far steeper than the diagonal—predicting 0.14 where nothing is positive and 0.86 where everything is, a compression of its probabilities toward 0.5 that reaches 0.26 at its widest. The MLP and 1D-CNN instead concentrate their predictions into a narrow band near 0.5 that spans outcomes from roughly 0.2 to 0.8, resolving the middle of the range poorly.

Brier scores (XGBoost 0.0861, Random Forest 0.1304, MLP 0.1466, 1D-CNN 0.2625) are reported as a corroborating summary statistic, but interpreted with one important caveat. The Brier score is a strictly proper scoring rule that combines calibration (reliability) with discriminative power (resolution); a lower score can therefore reflect stronger discrimination rather than better calibration alone (45, 46). Because XGBoost is also the most accurate classifier here, part of its Brier-score advantage is attributable to discrimination, not calibration per se, and the Brier ranking should not be read as a pure calibration ranking. The expected calibration error—the mean absolute gap between predicted and observed rates across the bins of Figure 8, which isolates the calibration component—separates the two effects: XGBoost 0.032, 1D-CNN 0.102 (± 0.016), MLP 0.128 (± 0.018), Random Forest 0.176, the neural-network figures being means over training restarts. This ordering is not the accuracy ordering. The Random Forest separates the classes better than either neural network (AUC 0.949 against 0.904 and 0.881) yet is the worst calibrated of the four, so calibration quality on this dataset is demonstrably not a by-product of discriminative power; XGBoost leads on both. That is why XGBoost's calibration is treated as a robust empirical result here rather than an artifact of a single conflated metric.

The Random Forest's weaker calibration is consistent with a known property of bagging ensembles: averaging predictions from trees trained on distinct bootstrap resamples compresses ensemble probabilities toward the center, undervaluing high-confidence and overvaluing low-confidence detections (46). The neural networks miscalibrate in a different way: rather than compressing smoothly, both pile their predictions into a narrow band around 0.5 across which the true positive rate varies roughly fourfold, so their probabilities carry little information in the middle of the range even though their overall calibration error is smaller than the Random Forest's. Post-hoc methods such as Platt scaling or isotonic regression could narrow these gaps, so XGBoost's advantage is one of convenience and robustness rather than an insurmountable barrier; however, such calibrators add a separate fitting stage whose reliability depends on scarce held-out calibration data (isotonic regression in particular is prone to overfitting when that data is limited) (46). A model that is already well-calibrated avoids this added stage and its failure modes. XGBoost is therefore adopted as the primary recommendation—not because the alternatives cannot be calibrated, but because it delivers reliable probabilities without an additional calibration step. This is an empirical result on the present synthetic dataset, not a theoretical guarantee, since gradient-boosted trees are not inherently calibrated. This calibration advantage is also measured in-distribution: under injected observational systematics it degrades faster than accuracy—at an effective SNR of 10 the Brier score rises from 0.080 to 0.139 while accuracy falls eight points (Section 4.6)—so calibration should be re-established on data representative of the deployment distribution rather than assumed to transfer.

### 4.5 Application Trade-offs: Accuracy vs. Recall

While the present models were optimized for balanced F1-performance during the tuning phase, the final deployment on the Ariel mission would likely involve threshold moving to align with specific scientific objectives. During an initial pass over the Tier 3 catalog, the classification threshold could be lowered to prioritize recall, reducing the risk that a promising candidate is prematurely discarded. Conversely, when down-selecting the final few targets for the most resource-intensive follow-up—Tier 4 phase curves, ground-based ELT time, or full Bayesian retrievals—the threshold could be raised to maximize precision, reserving those scarce resources for only the highest-probability candidates. This flexibility, combined with XGBoost's superior calibration, supports mission-specific risk management.

### 4.6 Robustness under Domain Shift

The classifier is characterised here as an in-domain triage tool, not a demonstration of biosignature detection on real observations. Across seven perturbation axes its accuracy degrades gracefully and traceably rather than catastrophically. Three of the seven axes each reduce to a distinct mechanism: suppression of feature amplitude (aerosols), chromatic distortion (stellar contamination), and a shift of the decision threshold (alternative opacity data). The observational-noise axes act through the first of these—resolution loss and additive noise push diagnostic features below the noise floor—so among noise sources the dominant vulnerability is time-correlated systematics, while realistic instrument-noise coloring is a minor additional penalty; the remaining two axes (an independent radiative transfer code and out-of-envelope extrapolation) are comparatively minor residuals. In every experiment the trained pipeline is left untouched: the models are trained once, and the entire preprocessing chain (raw scaler, PCA basis, and post-PCA scaler) is fit on clean training data only and applied unchanged; only the evaluation data is altered. The baseline throughout is 88.9% accuracy (Brier 0.080), measured on the same committed planets. Injected perturbation strengths are anchored to the measured per-spectrum noise floor (median 1.04×10⁻⁴, itself only 15.8% of the total spectral scatter).

**Injected observational systematics.** Six perturbation families were each swept over five strengths and applied only to the test sets at the raw transit-depth stage (Table 6; the full sweeps are shown in Figure 9). Calibration-type errors are largely tolerated—a baseline offset and a gain ramp cost under three points—while correlated noise is the dominant vulnerability, costing 22 points at an effective SNR of 5 and remaining worse than white noise at equal SNR (66.9% vs 73.9%). Resolution degradation and white noise fall in between.

**Out-of-envelope extrapolation.** Training restricted to planets with radius ≤ 15 R⊕ and tested on radius > 15 gives 75.8%, against a same-sized random-split control at 84.1% and the full in-distribution 88.9%. Holding the training-set size fixed, the extrapolation penalty is −8.3 points; the remainder of the naïve gap is reduced training data. Because the generating parameters are not drawn independently (Section 3.5), this split is not a radius extrapolation alone: the held-out planets are also hotter and orbit hotter stars than anything in training, so −8.3 measures the cost of leaving the sampled envelope along several coupled directions at once rather than along radius by itself.

**Whitening reduces robustness.** A controlled comparison in which two otherwise-identical neural networks differ only in whether the standardisation is applied—each trained five times to average over initialisation—shows that the step reduces robustness. Averaged over restarts, the standardised network begins 3.6 ± 3.1 accuracy points ahead on clean data and ends 11.0 ± 1.7 points behind at an effective SNR of 5, with the curves crossing by SNR 12 in four of five restarts; normalised by headroom above chance, it degrades 1.6–2.8 times faster in every perturbation family with a measurable effect. The same asymmetry appears under a change of forward model rather than injected noise. Re-scoring both networks on spectra recomputed for the same planets with the independent radiative transfer code described below, the standardised network loses 8.6 points (77.5% ± 0.9 to 68.9% ± 0.7) while the unstandardised one loses 1.0 (72.9% ± 6.4 to 71.9% ± 6.2), again over five restarts. This is a test of the standardisation step against a genuinely independent simulator rather than against perturbations injected into the same one, and it agrees with the injected-noise result. Both support the concern that rescaling every low-variance component equally raises the weight of components dominated by systematics as much as those carrying signal—and the clean-data benefit that motivated the standardisation is itself marginal once training variability is accounted for. The recommended model, XGBoost, uses no standardisation, so this does not affect the headline result.

**Independent radiative transfer code.** To separate the influence of the radiative transfer implementation from that of the underlying opacity data, transmission spectra were recomputed for the held-out test planets with Exo-Transmit (47), an independently developed code. The molecular cross sections were held fixed: the opacity tables used by the training simulator originate from Exo-Transmit and were supplied to it unchanged, so the radiative transfer implementation is the only quantity that differs. The two codes agree closely on the spectra themselves (median per-planet correlation 0.997, mean transit depths within 0.7%), and classification accuracy falls from 88.9% to 84.8%, a reduction of 4.1 points. This is an upper bound on the numerical-scheme difference: Exo-Transmit treats gravity as constant through the atmosphere whereas the training simulator integrates hydrostatically, and isolating this effect (all molecular opacity disabled) shows spectral contrast differing by 4–12% while mean depths agree to 0.4%.

**Alternative molecular opacity data.** The H₂O, CH₄, and CO₂ tables were replaced with ExoMol line lists—POKAZATEL, YT34to10, and UCL-4000 respectively (48, 49, 50)—supplied in TauREx format from the ExoMolOP database (51), with the code and structure fixed, and the planets re-rendered and paired (validated to reproduce the originals to 1.2×10⁻¹⁴). Accuracy falls from 88.9% to 72.8% (−16.1); replacing ozone with HITRAN as well—extending the substitution to both label-defining molecules—lowers it to 66.0% (−22.9). This failure is a bias shift rather than signal loss: median feature amplitude barely moves (0.95× → 0.93×), but the predicted-positive rate moves from 49.5% to 61.8% (three molecules) and to 44.0% (with ozone) against a true rate of 50.3%. Comparing the two experiments, substituting the opacity data costs roughly four times as much accuracy as substituting the radiative transfer code (six times with ozone replaced): the method's transferability is limited principally by uncertainty in molecular line lists rather than by the choice of forward model, a constraint shared by any retrieval-based analysis of real observations.

**Clouds and hazes.** Aerosols were introduced into the forward model—a grey cloud deck at fixed cloud-top pressures and, separately, a Lee et al. (52) parameterisation of Mie extinction for a photochemical haze—with the classifiers trained only on aerosol-free spectra. The held-out planets were re-rendered with the aerosol added and nothing else changed, so each spectrum is paired with a clear-sky spectrum of the same planet (Table 7). Performance is essentially unaffected by mild aerosols (a deck at 10⁵ Pa costs 2.3 points; a haze retaining 95% of the clear feature amplitude costs 0.04, within measurement noise), becomes substantial once an aerosol removes roughly a fifth of the feature amplitude, and reaches about −25 points at strong muting for both prescriptions. Below ≈10² Pa the deck suppresses feature amplitude to under a third of its clear value and accuracy approaches chance because almost no diagnostic structure remains—a limit of retrievable signal rather than a specific failure of the classifier. Usability therefore depends primarily on how much feature amplitude the aerosol leaves intact.

**Physical stellar contamination.** Stellar contamination was modelled via the Transit Light Source Effect (53): unocculted starspots and faculae make the transit chord's stellar spectrum unrepresentative of the disc, multiplying the measured transit depth by a wavelength-dependent factor ε(λ) = 1 / [1 − f(1 − F_spot(λ)/F_phot(λ))]. The surface fluxes were taken from BT-Settl/PHOENIX model spectra (54), so the spot and faculae spectra carry their own molecular bands. Applying cool spots (T_spot = 0.85 T_eff) and hot faculae (T_eff + 100 K) to the held-out spectra through the frozen pipeline, accuracy falls from 88.9% to 85.7% at 2% spot coverage and to 65.7% at 20% (Table 8). Unlike the aerosol case, median feature amplitude is essentially unchanged (~0.04 throughout), so the loss is chromatic distortion—structured, feature-like contamination added by the star—rather than suppression of the planetary signal. The spot and faculae spectra are solar-metallicity BT-Settl models with adopted temperature contrasts; a metallicity-matched grid is a fidelity refinement.

**Realistic instrument noise.** Instrument noise was modelled with ExoRad2 (55), the open radiometric engine underlying the Ariel radiometric model (56), driven by an Ariel payload built from published channel parameters (VISPhot, FGS1, FGS2, NIRSpec, and the two AIRS spectrometer channels). The resulting noise-to-signal ratio is strongly wavelength-dependent—a factor of 29.5 across 0.5–7.8 µm, concentrated in the AIRS bands that carry the methane, carbon dioxide, water, and ozone features. Applied to the held-out spectra and compared against white noise at the same median level (Table 9), this realistic coloring reduces accuracy by only 0.8 to 1.3 points. Realistic instrument-noise coloring is therefore a minor additional penalty; the dominant noise vulnerability is the time-correlated systematics identified above, which cost up to 22 points. The Ariel payload is reconstructed from published parameters, not the consortium's official radiometric file; the modelled quantity is the noise's wavelength shape, applied at a swept overall level.

**Synthesis.** Taken together, the seven axes place the method within a quantified envelope. Its degradation is graceful, and three of the seven axes each reduce to a measured mechanism—feature-amplitude suppression (aerosols drive accuracy smoothly toward chance as amplitude vanishes), chromatic distortion (stellar contamination adds structure while leaving amplitude intact), and a decision-threshold bias (opacity substitution shifts the predicted-positive rate at near-constant amplitude). The observational-noise axes act through the first mechanism, since resolution loss and additive noise both bury diagnostic features below the noise floor. The largest sensitivities, comparable in magnitude at their worst strengths, are to the molecular opacity data (−22.9 points), strong aerosols (−25.6) or stellar contamination (−23.2), and time-correlated instrument noise (−22.0); realistic instrument-noise coloring and an independent radiative transfer code are comparatively minor. Within its training domain the pipeline is a fast, calibrated triage tool—it ranks candidates by biosignature-labelling status with reliable confidence, which is what prioritising scarce follow-up time requires—and, unlike prior work, its failures outside that domain are now measured and reduce to a few identifiable mechanisms, so where it can and cannot be trusted is known rather than assumed. That is the contribution: a useful, well-characterised triage tool, not a detector validated on real observations.

## 5. Conclusion

This study evaluated the feasibility of using machine learning to recover a threshold-based biosignature labelling from exoplanet transmission spectra within an Ariel-like observational framework. By training and comparing four models—XGBoost, Random Forest, Multi-Layer Perceptron, and a 1D Convolutional Neural Network—on synthetic spectra generated with MultiREx and processed through a PCA-based feature extraction pipeline, this work established a calibrated benchmark for this task and characterised its behaviour under domain shift.

Among the evaluated approaches, the tree-based ensembles (XGBoost and Random Forest) substantially outperformed the deep neural networks. Because their node-splitting is invariant to per-feature scale, the tree models used the low-variance components without any rescaling, whereas the neural networks required explicit standardisation of the PCA features even to remain competitive and still fell short. Beyond aggregate accuracy, XGBoost's sequential boosting yields substantially better probability calibration (0.0861 Brier score) than Random Forest's compressed bagging averages (0.1304), and it is the primary triage recommendation within this synthetic benchmark, pending validation on real observations.

No principal component isolates the biosignature signal: the two components carrying 98.4% of the variance classify at chance and are removable without loss, and while each molecule's spectral imprint is concentrated in only a few components, those are not confined to the low-variance tail—67% of the ozone imprint falls on the highest-variance component the earlier interpretation had called purely physical. Classification succeeds by nonlinearly combining many individually weak components; the secondary standardisation is an optimisation step for the scale-sensitive neural networks, not a chemistry-selective operation, and the recommended model uses none. This replaces an earlier feature-engineering interpretation that attributed the leading components to physical structure and the remaining components to chemical absorption, an attribution the mathematics of PCA does not support.

Under domain shift the pipeline degrades gracefully and traceably rather than catastrophically. Three of its seven axes each reduce to a distinct mechanism: suppression of feature amplitude (aerosols), chromatic distortion (stellar contamination), and a decision-threshold bias (alternative opacity data). The observational-noise axes act through the first, as resolution loss and additive noise drive diagnostic features below the noise floor; among these, time-correlated systematics are the dominant vulnerability, while realistic Ariel instrument-noise coloring, modelled with a radiometric simulator, costs only about one point beyond white noise.

This physical legibility extends to the error analysis: model failures are not random but cluster for planets with low atmospheric scale heights, where spectral features are intrinsically weak, and near the labelling thresholds, where the distinction being drawn is one the spectra cannot carry. This alignment between part of the in-domain error structure and known atmospheric physics indicates that, within its training distribution, the model keys on physically meaningful signal—feature amplitude relative to the noise—rather than arbitrary artefacts, though that mechanism accounts for the low-amplitude tail rather than the full error budget; the domain-shift experiments of Section 4.6 show where that signal is nonetheless tied to simulator-specific choices, most notably the molecular opacity data.

Several limitations remain. The class labels are a deterministic function of the abundances used to generate the spectra, so the task is the recovery of a labelling convention rather than the inference of abundances from observations, which Ariel will require via retrieval. The models are trained on spectra from a single radiative transfer code; evaluated on spectra recomputed for the same planets with an independent code, accuracy falls from 88.9% to 84.8%, and evaluated with an alternative molecular opacity compilation, to 72.8%. The training data contain no aerosols (their out-of-distribution cost is quantified in Section 4.6, though training with aerosols was not attempted), and the atmospheres are one-dimensional. Two of the seven robustness axes carry stated approximations: the stellar-contamination model uses solar-metallicity BT-Settl spectra with adopted spot/faculae contrasts, and the instrument-noise model uses an Ariel payload reconstructed from published parameters rather than the consortium's official radiometric file.

The clearest next steps are to replace the deterministic labels with retrieval-derived labels carrying realistic uncertainty—the primary remaining item—and to upgrade the two approximate robustness models (a metallicity-matched stellar grid and the official Ariel instrument file). Further interpretability analysis linking model decisions to specific spectral regions would strengthen confidence in the physical validity of the predictions.

Overall, this work offers a template for responsible evaluation: a triage classifier whose reliability envelope is measured rather than assumed, across seven axes of domain shift and with calibrated probabilities that make its confidence usable for allocating scarce observing time. As machine-learning triage is increasingly proposed for missions like Ariel, it is this kind of quantified reliability, rather than headline accuracy alone, that will determine whether such tools can responsibly guide the search for potentially habitable worlds.
