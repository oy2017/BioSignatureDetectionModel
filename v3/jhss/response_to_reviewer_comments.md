### A2. External validation on WASP-39 b

*"He recommends that if you externally validate your results, the manuscript would then generate enough knowledge to become publishable as an original paper. He is of the opinion that you pursue this avenue, since you have already put in considerable work. He suggests using WASP-39 b, which is an exoplanet included in Ariel's planned target program, and it has already been observed by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). However, Ariel and JWST are separate space telescopes. WASP-39 b is not part of the Ariel instrument or system; it is a planetary target that Ariel can observe. This makes WASP-39 b particularly relevant to the manuscript because the same planetary atmosphere can be studied using existing JWST observations and simulated Ariel measurements. The author could compare the classifier's predictions against JWST-derived atmospheric properties, **provided the observed spectra are made compatible with the classifier's input requirements** with adequate justification of the compatibility procedure. That would provide an independent observational test of the simulator-trained classifier, rather than another sensitivity test using entirely simulated spectra."*

**Response.** Done. The JWST benchmark spectrum of WASP-39 b (the four Early Release Science modes re-reduced uniformly by Carter & May et al. 2024, extended to 7.8 µm with the MIRI LRS spectrum of Powell et al. 2024) was converted to the classifier's 104-bin Tier-3 input, and the conversion is justified three ways.

The procedure (Section 2.6, paragraph 2): JWST products are transit depths, the quantity the classifier was trained on, so no conversion of quantity is needed. Each Tier-3 bin takes the inverse-variance-weighted mean of the JWST points inside it, partial overlaps weighted by the overlap fraction; modes are aligned through their overlaps onto NIRISS order 1, which for WASP-39 b recovers the PRISM offset of −177 ppm that Carter & May report from their joint fit; bins with no JWST data are filled flat at the spectrum's mean and flagged, and the number of filled bins is reported with every verdict (one of 104 for WASP-39 b); per-spectrum normalisation then removes any global offset, as in training.

The justification (Section 2.6, paragraph 3): (a) a round trip on a spectrum whose answer is known, the benchmark's own best-fit model binned directly onto the 104 bins against the same model sampled at each JWST mode's wavelength grid with the per-point errors and pushed through the conversion, which differ by 22 ppm rms and 104 ppm at worst (a MIRI bin), with no verdict changed; (b) a sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations (the individual reductions of each mode, two NIRISS pipelines, fitted and fixed limb darkening, offsets on and off, model and flat fill, with and without MIRI, PRISM alone, and the Na, K, SO₂ and CO bins masked singly and together), which measures how much each verdict depends on the reduction, following Roy-Perez et al. (2026), who showed that WASP-39 b's retrieved abundances move by more than an order of magnitude with the reduction; (c) three noise regimes, JWST precision as delivered, Ariel Tier-1 precision (the payload model's per-bin noise at the planet's Tier-1 transit count, added as 500 to 1,000 Gaussian draws, giving a detection frequency per molecule), and Tier-2 and Tier-3 counts for context.

The outcome (Section 3.5, "WASP-39 b"): at JWST precision three of four learners call H₂O present and all four call CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at the 10⁻⁴ threshold, which agrees with the published composition on every label the literature decides (the manuscript no longer says the composition was "reproduced", since one learner misses the water and the Tier-1 detection frequency is 58 %); CO₂ at 10⁻⁵, where its published abundance of about 10⁻⁴·⁵ puts it, is present for two to three learners; NH₃ is absent in all 24 configurations and with each of three MIRI reductions; the consortium's own band metric agrees with the majority. The multilayer perceptron calls H₂O absent with a score of 0.00 in 23 of 24 configurations and on the benchmark's own model binned directly with no conversion, so the miss is not the conversion's; it is the shape of a real hot Saturn, whose spectrum is 3.7 times more muted than its clear twin at the published abundances (68 % bootstrap interval 3.67 to 3.79), beyond the most muted atmosphere the population can draw at its bulk parameters (2.0), while sitting inside the training distribution by every input-space rule. Under Ariel Tier-1 noise at WASP-39 b's one transit, H₂O is detected in 58 % of draws with all inputs and 86 % without the three optical points, while the clear twin is detected in 100 %.

Because one planet is a weak external test, ten further planets with public JWST transmission spectra were converted by the same procedure (TOI-270 d, K2-18 b in three reductions, TOI-1231 b, TOI-421 b, HAT-P-18 b, WASP-166 b, and WASP-107 b, HD 189733 b, HAT-P-26 b and GJ 3470 b with AIRS-only coverage), with labels from the published retrievals frozen before any verdict was computed, the published abundances behind each label listed in Table 2, and ambiguous labels left unscored. Over the 51 scored labels of the 23-reduction panel the all-input classifier was right on 69 %, the share of absent labels, and the AIRS-only variant on 80 %; three of the counted misses are a coverage artefact of the AIRS-only planets, which the manuscript keeps in the count and reports without (Section 3.5, "The panel"; Table 7). The eight methane reductions are five planets (nine planets were added in a second batch after the routing was designed, and the result holds on that batch), and the manuscript reports the result per planet as well as per reduction and states the effective sample. The conversion's validation holds at near-complete coverage; at the panel's partial coverage the control is each planet's clear twin through the same mask, and the limitation is stated (Section 4). The expectations for the WASP-39 b test and the panel, committed before the runs, and their outcomes are rows A1 to P6 of Table C1.

**Where.** Section 2.6 (paragraphs 1 to 4); Table 2; Section 3.5 ("WASP-39 b", "The panel", "The methane planets"); Table 7; Table C1 (rows A1 to P6); Figure 4.

### A3. Identifiability

*"Then, two main advances beyond the WASP-39 b validation would be required: First, establish identifiability. Determine whether physically distinct atmospheric compositions can produce indistinguishable Ariel spectra, and quantify how frequently such ambiguity occurs under explicitly defined atmospheric populations. This would identify a fundamental limit on molecular classification rather than merely another sensitivity to simulation parameters."*

**Response.** Done, on the whole test population, with two instruments (Section 2.4). The first is a Δχ² test: for each planet and each molecule, the best-fitting atmosphere of the opposite label, with the molecule held at the 10⁻⁴ boundary and the population's nuisance parameters free (temperature over 0.7 to 1.05 T_eq, grey cloud top over 5 × 10² to 10⁶ Pa, the other three abundances over 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻², and an additive offset), scored against the true spectrum at the planet's Tier-1 per-bin noise. The second is a nested-sampling retrieval of every one of the 955 test planets with the same free quantities, which gives for every label the posterior probability of the true class, the P-statistic of Bocchieri et al. (2023) evaluated for the true class. The two agree in rank (Spearman 0.80 to 0.89), and the retrieval calibrates the test's scale, which proved to be wrong by an order of magnitude (the posterior crosses 0.95 at Δχ² of 0.45 to 0.98, not at the nested-model value 3.84); the uncalibrated prevalence first computed is reported in the Discussion only as the value the calibration replaced.

The population is explicitly defined: POP-I, the consortium's own test prior, at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, with the payload noise model of Section 2.2. Under it (Section 3.3; Table 5), 32 % of CH₄ labels, 54 % of H₂O, 44 % of CO₂ and 32 % of NH₃ cannot be decided at the 0.95 level, 35 to 62 % when decidability is defined by a Bayes factor against the retrieval prior's odds, with present labels undecidable more often than absent ones; 17 % of planets have all four labels decidable and 3 % have none. Two properties of the retrieval are stated with the result (Section 2.4): the posteriors are of the noise-free spectrum, so decidability is decidability in expectation, and the abundance prior gives absent labels a head start of 0.71. On a random 200 planets retrieved on the classifier's own noisy realisation (Section 3.3, last paragraph), the undecidable fractions move by at most 5 points, 5 to 12 % of labels change class each way, and the retrieval's accuracy falls 3 to 6 points, so the population figures hold while the per-label classes carry that uncertainty. Decidability is set first by the label's distance from the 10⁻⁴ boundary (labels within half a decade of it are undecidable in 89 to 92 % of cases, one to two decades away in 12 to 36 %) and second by the planet's noise. The retrieval's own accuracy, 92 to 94 % (97 to 98 % on absent labels, 82 to 88 % on present ones), is the ceiling the spectra allow under this prior, and the classifier sits 9 to 16 points below it. This is the fundamental limit the editor asked for, stated as a prevalence under a named population; the manuscript says that the true joint distribution of Ariel's targets is unknown (Section 2.4, last paragraph; Section 4) and measures in Section 3.5 how far the real planets sit from the prior.

**Where.** Section 2.4; Section 3.3; Table 5; Figure 3a; Table C1 (rows B1 to B7).

### A4. A remedy with measurable value

*"Second, establish a remedy with measurable value. Develop and evaluate a diagnostic that recognizes when the classifier is likely to be unreliable, and demonstrate that its use improves molecular classification or target selection compared with existing methods."*

**Response.** Done, with the value measured in the mission's own currency, Tier-2 targets lost at a fixed 60 % selection, against three existing methods: the classifier alone, the five decline rules of the original note (margin, ensemble, Mahalanobis, k-NN, PCA), and a retrieval of every planet. The answer has three parts, the first of which is a negative result that the paper reports rather than omits.

First (Section 3.6, paragraph 2; Table C2), on clean, noise-free simulated spectra the retrieval of every planet is the selection oracle, as a correctly specified retrieval must be: ranking by its present-posterior loses 0 of 359 CH₄-bearing targets, against 20 for the classifier, at 365 core-hours for the population. No diagnostic on the classifier can improve on that, and no cheap rule routes a smaller retrieval budget much better than a random one (the distance rules by 2 to 4 targets, within three standard deviations of the random draw; the confidence rules not at all), because the confidence rules send the budget to the undecidable labels, where the retrieval cannot help either.

Second (Section 3.6, paragraph 1; Table 8; Figure 3c), the existing retrieval shares the classifier's failure: run with the training forward model on the hazed spectra of all 954 planets, it calls every present label absent (recall below 1 %) and is confidently wrong on 99 % of present labels, worse than the classifier; with the haze density as one free parameter it recovers 222 of the 224 methane labels the clean spectra decide.

Third (Section 3.6, paragraphs 3 and 4; Table 9; Figure 5), the diagnostic: a one-number optical-excess score, the three photometric points against the NIRSpec continuum as a z-score on the clean training set, chosen before the runs because Section 3.2 had located the haze failure in those inputs. It flags 100 % of hazed and 66 % of spotted planets at a 10 % clean false-alarm rate, and fewer than 3 % of clouded and noisier ones. On a mixed population in which a fifth of the planets are hazed, routing the 20 % of planets it ranks most suspect to a retrieval with the haze density as a free parameter loses 13 ± 2 of 359 CH₄ targets, against 29 ± 4 for the classifier alone, 20 ± 4 for a random budget, 20 to 22 for the margin and ensemble rules, 69 for the existing retrieval of every planet, and 12 ± 2 for an oracle that knows which planets are hazed; its recall on the hazed targets is 80 %, against 3 % for the classifier and the confidence rules and 81 % for the oracle. The existing Mahalanobis rule, used as a router, loses the same number of targets within noise (13 ± 2) with 64 % of the hazed targets found, so the manuscript states that the optical score's advantage over the existing distance rules is its recall and selectivity on the hazed planets, not the selection itself, and that the improvement over the existing methods comes from the use (routing to a retrieval that models the departure) rather than from the score. The same budget routed to the existing clear-model retrieval loses 71 ± 8 targets, worse than no retrieval, which the abstract now states as the central result. Without any retrieval, routing the flagged planets to the AIRS-only variant raises CH₄ recall under haze from 12 to 54 % and reduces the targets lost from 38 to 26, is never worse than the classifier on clean or noisier spectra, and needs no retraining. The expectations for the mixed-population test were committed after a pilot of the same test on the 124 planets whose posteriors existed first and before the population run, which the manuscript now says (Section 2.7); four of five were met in full and the fifth for one of the three distance rules (Table C1, rows E3-i to E3-v).

What the diagnostic does not do is also stated, in the abstract and in Section 3.6: it does not beat a departure-aware retrieval of every planet, which loses nothing at 635 core-hours, so its value is at a budget below that; it does not help the irrecoverable class, which the classifier's own confidence recognises only in part (AUROC 0.73 to 0.77 for three molecules, 0.6 for water), a flag learned from the spectrum does no better, and even perfect recognition would recover four of 359 methane targets at a 20 % budget (Section 3.6), so the paper's recommendation is that undecided planets reach Tier 2 as undecided rather than dismissed; and on the real panel the optical flag alone fired on two of the eight methane reductions, while routing by it or by the input-disagreement flag recovers methane on five of the eight and raises the scored labels from 37 of 53 to 44 of 54, at a cost of 4.6 points of clean accuracy in simulation (Section 3.6, last paragraph; Table 7, last column). The mixed-population test with the retrieval budget hazes the planets at one density that the score flags on every hazed planet, so matching the oracle there is the expected outcome; the limitation is stated in Section 4. A second density, 2 × 10⁶ m⁻³, where the haze costs 12 points of accuracy, was added to the test (Section 3.6): there the flag's sensitivity falls from 98 to 60 % at the same clean decline rate, the classifier's CH₄ ranking loses no more targets than on clean spectra, the loss moves to H₂O, and the routing without a retrieval recovers little of it, so the diagnostic's benefit is specific to the departure it detects fully, and the text says so.

**Where.** Section 2.7; Section 3.6; Tables 8, 9 and 10; Figure 5; Table C1 (rows C1 to E3-v); Table C2.

### A5. The two classes of error

*"The most interesting possible outcome is that you discover two distinct classes of error: 1. Recoverable computational errors: The spectrum contains sufficient molecular information, but the classifier interprets it incorrectly because of its training assumptions. 2. Irrecoverable information limitations: Different atmospheric compositions produce observations that cannot be distinguished at the instrument's precision, making a definitive molecular classification unjustified. The manuscript already provides preliminary evidence for the first category through its haze-retraining experiments. You has not systematically investigated the second. Distinguishing these two categories, quantifying their prevalence, and developing a method to recognize them would be a much stronger scientific contribution than the present sensitivity analysis. It would also produce a result with implications beyond Ariel, potentially extending to other simulator-trained scientific inference systems."*

**Response.** This became the paper's spine, its title and its abstract. The definition (Section 2.5): an error is recoverable if the posterior probability of the true class on the same spectrum exceeds 0.95 (a retrieval of the expected spectrum gets the label right), undecidable if it lies between 0.05 and 0.95, and misled if below 0.05; under a departure the departure-aware Δχ² at the calibrated threshold is used. Because the posterior is of the noise-free spectrum, the manuscript states that the recoverable shares are upper bounds and measures the gap on the 200-planet subsample retrieved on the classifier's own realisation (Section 3.3, last paragraph: 18.5 % of the learners' errors recoverable by the expected posterior, 14.9 % decidable by the realisation's). The split of a departure's errors into those shared with the clean run and those introduced was made after the runs and is labelled as such.

Prevalence (Section 3.4; Table 6; Figure 3): three quarters of the classifier's errors on clean spectra are irrecoverable. Haze and unocculted spots remove no information the labels need, and 57 to 87 % of the errors they introduce are recoverable; a deep cloud deck removes information on absent labels (51 to 82 % of its introduced errors recoverable); threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction and leaves 10 to 56 % recoverable. Table 6 reports the same five quantities for every departure, and states that the spot and deck classes assume the departure's strength is known. The clearest demonstration is a pair of retrievals on the same 954 hazed spectra: with the training forward model the retrieval is confidently wrong on 99 % of present labels; with the haze density added as a single free parameter it recovers the density on every planet and 222 of the 224 CH₄ labels the clean spectra decide, within 5 points of its clean accuracy on every molecule.

Recognition (Section 3.6): at deployment the two classes are told apart by a retrieval on a flagged planet, and the paper measures what each cheaper candidate can and cannot do. The shift detectors (the optical score and the distance rules) find the recoverable class under haze; the irrecoverable class is marked in part by the classifier's own confidence and by nothing cheap better than it, and recognising it is worth a withheld verdict rather than a retrieval (Section 3.6), which the abstract now states, because those planets should not be dismissed.

On real data (Section 3.5): the methane misses on the three sub-Neptunes are recoverable, since the information is in the spectrum (the AIRS-only learner finds it) and retraining with hazes repairs the planets whose spectra are muted beyond the simulator's prior and not the two that are not; whereas the five real warm giants are muted 3.1 to 5.3 times beyond their clear twins, in every bootstrap resample beyond what the population can produce at their bulk parameters, a limit of the training prior that no normalised-input rule can see (Figure 4). The implication beyond Ariel has its own paragraph in the Discussion (Section 4, "Beyond Ariel") and closes the Conclusion: before a simulator-trained classifier is used to make a selection, the question is which of its answers the data decide, and that question is answered by the data, not the classifier.

**Where.** Title; Abstract; Section 1, paragraph 4; Section 2.5; Section 3.4; Section 3.5; Section 4; Table 6; Figures 3 and 4; Table C1 (row B4).

## Part B. The reviewer's written report

### B1

*"The manuscript presents a technically substantial investigation of the robustness of machine-learning classifiers trained on simulated exoplanet transmission spectra. The author independently reconstructs previously published Ariel molecular classifiers, reproduces their reported accuracies, and subjects them to fifteen departures from the assumptions of the training simulator. The analysis includes atmospheric haze, stellar contamination, additional molecular absorbers, alternative opacity databases, radiative-transfer implementations, instrumental noise, and combinations of these perturbations. The author further investigates confidence calibration, out-of-distribution detection, domain randomization, and the effects of excluding particular spectral inputs. The computational work is extensive, and the independent reproduction of 48 published classification accuracies within 3.7 percentage points is a useful technical accomplishment."*

**Response.** Thank you. The reproduction is kept as Section 3.1 and Appendix A, shortened, and was regenerated from scratch on a second machine (agreement to 0.05 points on average) for the revision.

**Where.** Section 3.1; Appendix A; Figure 1.

### B2

*"Nevertheless, the central question for publication is not whether the work was carefully executed, but whether it establishes a sufficiently novel scientific proposition."*

**Response.** Agreed. The revision's propositions are new measurements rather than the proposition the reviewer rejects in B3; they are listed under B3 and B13 below.

### B3

*"The manuscript's principal conclusion is that classifiers trained on simulated spectra may perform poorly when confronted with spectra generated under different physical assumptions, that these failures may occur without corresponding reductions in predictive confidence, and that individual classifiers require independent robustness testing. These observations are consistent with the established literature on distribution shift, model misspecification, uncertainty calibration, and simulation-to-reality transfer. Indeed, the manuscript explicitly acknowledges that robustness testing is established practice and cites previous investigations of model misspecification in simulation-based inference and exoplanet atmospheric retrieval. Consequently, the general proposition that simulator-trained classifiers cannot be assumed reliable outside their training distributions is neither new nor particularly surprising. Demonstrating this principle in another application does not, by itself, constitute a substantive scientific advance."*

**Response.** Agreed, and the revised paper no longer rests on that proposition. Its principal conclusions are population-level and decision-level measurements that were not available before: (1) under the consortium's own test prior, a third to a half of the four molecular labels cannot be decided at Tier-1 precision, and three quarters of the classifier's errors fall on those labels, where no method reading the spectrum can fix them at that precision (Section 3.3, Table 5; Section 3.4); (2) the departures separate into the two classes, haze and spots leaving the labels decidable and noise removing them (Section 3.4, Table 6); (3) a retrieval of every planet, the method the classifier was proposed to replace, is the Tier-2 selection oracle on clean noise-free spectra at two days of computing, yet fails more confidently than the classifier under the one departure the real sub-Neptunes show (Section 3.6, Table 8); (4) the real JWST spectra show the same two classes, with the methane misses recoverable and the giants' muting a prior limit (Section 3.5); (5) a flag routed to a departure-aware retrieval allocates a partial retrieval budget within noise of an oracle, while the same flag routed to the existing retrieval is worse than nothing (Section 3.6, Table 9). Two results were not anticipated by the pre-registered expectations and are reported as surprises in the Discussion: the cheap identifiability test is wrong in scale by an order of magnitude against the posterior, and the real warm giants lie outside the simulator population in feature amplitude while inside it by every bulk parameter and every distance rule.

**Where.** Abstract; Section 1, paragraphs 4 and 5; Sections 3.3 to 3.7; Section 4.

### B4

*"However, the manuscript contains one result that deserves closer consideration than the broader robustness analysis. In Section 3.3, the author finds that relatively weak atmospheric haze causes the classifiers to miss methane and water even when the corresponding infrared molecular absorption features remain substantially intact. The author attributes this failure to three optical photometric inputs, demonstrating that removing these inputs reduces the haze-associated accuracy loss from 17.7 to 5.6 percentage points, while restricting classification to the AIRS channels reduces the loss to approximately 0.5 percentage points. The additional experiments in which hazy optical points are transplanted into otherwise clean spectra, and clean optical points are restored to hazy spectra, strengthen this attribution. This is more informative than merely demonstrating reduced accuracy under distribution shift: it identifies a specific architectural vulnerability in the published classifier design."*

**Response.** This result is kept (Section 3.2, "where the haze failure came from") and is the starting point of the revision: the optical-excess diagnostic of Section 3.6 is built on it, the AIRS-only variant is the fallback tested on real spectra in Section 3.5, and the retrieval of Section 3.4 is what turns the attribution into a classification of the error.

**Where.** Section 3.2; Sections 3.4 to 3.6.

### B5

*"Nevertheless, even this finding requires a stronger interpretation to qualify as a novel scientific contribution. The observation that a classifier trained without chromatic atmospheric haze may assign inappropriate significance to haze-sensitive optical measurements is physically intelligible from the construction of the training data. The author has demonstrated the magnitude of the vulnerability for a particular implementation, but has not established a new physical mechanism, a general mathematical principle governing the failure, or a previously unrecognized limitation of atmospheric spectroscopy. Moreover, the proposed remedies—incorporating haze into training or excluding or down-weighting affected inputs—are familiar approaches to domain randomization and feature selection. The analysis therefore identifies a useful implementation-specific weakness without clearly advancing the underlying science of classification or atmospheric inference."*

**Response.** The finding is now interpreted in three ways it was not before. It is classed: the retrieval shows that haze leaves the labels decidable, so the haze errors are a property of the classifier and not of the spectra, whereas the errors noise introduces are the opposite (Section 3.4, Table 6). It is shown on real planets: the three optical points decide WASP-39 b's Tier-1 outcome (H₂O detected in 58 % of draws with them, 86 % without), and the five methane-bearing sub-Neptunes are missed with the optical inputs on seven of eight reductions, all five planets by majority, and found with the AIRS channels alone on six of eight and four of the five planets (Section 3.5, Table 7). And it is shown to propagate to the existing method: a retrieval with the training forward model fails on the same hazed spectra more confidently than the classifier does, so the vulnerability belongs to the forward model's assumptions and not to the learner's architecture (Section 3.6, Table 8). The remedies are familiar, as the reviewer says, and the revision does not claim them as new; what is new is their value measured in targets at a fixed budget against the existing methods (Section 3.6, Tables 9 and 10), including the result that the most familiar remedy, a retrieval fallback, is worse than nothing unless the retrieval models the departure. Retraining with haze is no longer presented as a remedy at all but as the test of whether a real error is recoverable (Section 2.6, last paragraph; Section 3.5).

**Where.** Section 3.4; Section 3.5; Section 3.6; Tables 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10.

### B6

*"A more consequential issue concerns the distinction between robustness within a simulation framework and predictive validity for actual Ariel observations. As the author acknowledges in Section 3.7, all evaluated spectra are simulated, including those assigned to known Ariel targets. The study therefore compares classifiers trained under one set of modeling assumptions with spectra generated under alternative assumptions, rather than independently testing their predictions against observations of real planetary atmospheres. This distinction is fundamental. A classifier that performs poorly under a particular simulated haze prescription is not necessarily equally unreliable under the actual distribution of atmospheric hazes encountered by Ariel. Conversely, a classifier that performs well under the tested perturbations cannot be presumed reliable against physical processes absent from all the simulations. The study quantifies sensitivity to selected modeling choices but does not independently establish the relationship between simulated robustness and real-world classification reliability."*

**Response.** This is the point the external test answers (Part A2 above), and the answer is specific rather than general. On the one real planet inside the training domain, WASP-39 b, the classifier is mostly right, and its one wrong learner is wrong for a reason the paper locates in the real spectrum's muting rather than in the data, the conversion or the noise. On the real planets that carry the molecule the classifier was built to find, the methane sub-Neptunes, it is wrong on seven of eight reductions, by the mechanism the simulated haze had predicted; the clear twins of those planets through the same coverage and noise are found in 67 to 100 % of draws, so the misses are not the coverage or the noise. And the five real warm giants with published abundances are muted beyond the simulator population's reach at their bulk parameters, which measures directly, in one quantity, how the real distribution differs from the training prior (Section 3.5, "Muting"; Figure 4), with bootstrap intervals on every muting value. The paper does not claim that the simulated departures represent Ariel's target distribution; the limitations in Section 4 say they do not, and Section 3.5 measures the distance where JWST allows it.

**Where.** Section 2.6; Section 3.5; Section 4; Table 7; Figure 4.

### B7

*"The manuscript occasionally moves beyond this evidentiary boundary by framing the results in terms of whether classifiers can be trusted for Ariel target selection. Trustworthiness is a substantially broader claim than robustness to a finite set of perturbations. It requires some independent basis for believing that the tested departures, their parameter ranges, their prevalence, and their combinations adequately represent the physical conditions encountered in the intended application. Although the author anchors certain haze strengths to published atmospheric retrievals, this does not establish the joint distribution of atmospheric conditions across Ariel's target population. Without such information, the observed accuracy losses cannot be translated directly into expected mission-wide classification errors or incorrect target-selection decisions. The manuscript therefore demonstrates conditional failure under specified simulated conditions, not the operational reliability or unreliability of the proposed classifiers."*

**Response.** Accepted. "Trust" is removed from the title and the text. Every prevalence is reported under a named population, the consortium's POP-I prior at Tier-1 transit counts (Section 2.4, last paragraph). The mixed-population results are given at three stated hazed fractions, 10, 20 and 50 %, rather than one (Section 2.7; Section 3.6), and the paper draws no mission-wide error rate from them. The limitations in Section 4 state that the true joint distribution of atmospheric conditions among Ariel's targets is unknown, that the muting census of Section 3.5 is the measurement of how far eleven real planets sit from the prior and not a replacement for it, that the mixed population is hazed at one density and particle size, and that the haze strengths were anchored through an order-of-magnitude conversion.

**Where.** Title; Abstract; Section 2.4; Section 2.7; Section 3.6; Section 4.

### B8

*"The second classifier, designed to identify carbon-rich atmospheres, is introduced to demonstrate that robustness profiles are classifier-specific. The author reports that adding HCN and C₂H₂ substantially degrades carbon-rich classification while producing little corresponding degradation in the molecular-presence classifiers. This comparison is potentially interesting, but the two systems differ simultaneously in their prediction targets, training distributions, atmospheric chemistry, machine-learning architectures, and noise assumptions. The author appropriately acknowledges that these differences prevent causal attribution of the contrasting behavior. Consequently, the comparison establishes only that two different classifiers respond differently to different perturbations. That observation is unsurprising and does not support a general conclusion about which classifier properties determine vulnerability to particular forms of model misspecification."*

**Response.** Accepted. The carbon-rich classifier is reduced to Appendix B, one paragraph, and is used for the one conclusion the reviewer allows: one classifier's error classes do not stand in for another's, so each classifier needs its own measurement. No claim about which classifier properties determine vulnerability is made.

**Where.** Appendix B; Section 4, Limitations, last sentence.

### B9

*"The manuscript also devotes considerable attention to the failure of confidence-based rejection, distance-based novelty detection, and conformal prediction under distribution shift. These results are quantitatively documented, but their conceptual interpretation is already well established. Predictive confidence can remain high for out-of-distribution observations, distance-based novelty scores need not identify classification errors, and conformal coverage guarantees generally depend on assumptions about the relationship between calibration and test distributions."*

**Response.** Accepted. The decline-rule results are compressed to a few sentences inside Section 3.6, presented as the pattern reported for image classifiers under dataset shift (Ovadia et al. 2019) in a physical setting, scored by the selective-credit protocol of Jaeger et al. (2023), and they enter the rest of the paper only as the existing rules the diagnostic is compared with (Sections 2.3, 2.7 and 3.6). The manuscript also states that the rules' thresholds are quantiles of the clean test scores, so their clean decline rates are fixed by construction (Section 2.3).

**Where.** Section 2.3, last paragraph; Section 3.6, paragraph 2; Section 4.

### B10

*"Applying these existing methods to simulated Ariel spectra may provide useful benchmarking information, but the manuscript does not develop a new uncertainty measure, establish a new failure theorem, or identify a broadly transferable relationship between physical misspecification and confidence calibration."*

**Response.** The revision does not claim a new uncertainty measure or a theorem. What it adds about these methods is a result in the mission's currency with its reason: on clean spectra the confidence rules allocate a retrieval budget worse than a random one, and on a mixed population they find none of the hazed planets (0 to 1 % of them in the 20 % they rank most suspect), because they send the budget to the labels the classifier is unsure about, which are the undecidable ones where the retrieval cannot help either, while the planets the classifier is confidently wrong about are found only by a shift detector (Section 3.6, Tables 9 and C2). The transferable statement, made in the Discussion (Section 4, "Why confidence rules fail and when a shift flag helps"), is that a shift flag has value only when routed to a method that models the shift, and that routed to the standard method it is worse than no flag.

**Where.** Section 3.6; Tables 9 and C2; Section 4.

### B11

*"An additional concern is the reliance on aggregate classification accuracy as the principal measure of scientific utility. Ariel's Tier-1 reconnaissance observations are intended to inform decisions about which planets warrant deeper characterization. The consequences of incorrectly excluding a scientifically valuable planet may be substantially different from those of unnecessarily selecting a planet for additional observation. Yet the manuscript does not formulate a mission-level decision problem that incorporates these asymmetric consequences, observational costs, target priorities, or the number of follow-up opportunities available. Indeed, the reported haze experiments show that overall classification accuracy can obscure a near-total collapse in sensitivity to particular molecules. The more scientifically relevant quantity may therefore be the number and characteristics of valuable targets lost under a constrained selection strategy, rather than the average percentage of correctly classified simulated spectra."*

**Response.** Adopted as the primary metric. The selection simulation (Section 2.7) gives the 955 POP-I planets 60 % Tier-2 slots (573), ranked by the mean present-probability of the three probabilistic learners, and scores every strategy by the number of planets carrying the molecule that are not selected; accuracy and the recall on present labels are reported beside it, never alone. Tables 9, 10 and C2, the abstract and the conclusion are in targets lost, with draw-to-draw spreads where the simulation has them and the effect stated as a fraction of the Tier-2 queue. The asymmetry of consequences is handled in the one way the data allow: at a fixed slot count, slots wasted equal targets lost plus a constant, so the ranking of strategies does not depend on the relative weight of the two errors (Table C1, row C6), and Section 4 states that the ranking by a molecule's probability is a proxy for a selection the mission will make by theme and signal-to-noise, and that observing costs and follow-up opportunities are not modelled. The collapse in sensitivity the reviewer notes is now reported per molecule throughout, as recall on present labels and on hazed targets (Sections 3.4 and 3.6).

**Where.** Section 2.7; Section 3.6; Tables 9, 10 and C2; Section 4; Abstract.

### B12

*"The author's independent reconstruction of the original classifier also warrants recognition, particularly because the original trained models and complete implementation were unavailable. Reproducing the published accuracies across 48 combinations provides reasonable evidence that the reconstructed system captures much of the original design's behavior. However, agreement in classification accuracy does not establish equivalence of learned decision boundaries, feature dependencies, or responses to out-of-distribution spectra. Different implementations may achieve similar in-distribution accuracies while exhibiting substantially different robustness profiles. Therefore, the demonstrated weaknesses should be attributed primarily to the reconstructed implementation and tested design assumptions, rather than treated as experimentally verified properties of the unavailable original classifiers."*

**Response.** Accepted throughout, not only in the limitations. Section 2.1 defines "the published design as re-implemented" as this reading of the recipe with the deviations of Section 2.2, and the text uses that phrase wherever a weakness is claimed; the abstract and Section 1 say the classifiers were re-implemented from the published description; Section 3.1 says the agreement is consistent with following the published design and says nothing about the original trained models; the limitations in Section 4 repeat that the results concern the design as re-implemented.

**Where.** Abstract; Section 1; Section 2.1, last paragraph; Section 2.2; Section 3.1; Section 4.

### B13

*"Overall, the manuscript is methodologically competent and contains useful diagnostic information, particularly its identification of the three optical photometric inputs as a source of haze-related classification failure. The study also benefits from reproducible code, systematic perturbation testing, and explicit acknowledgment of several limitations. These strengths, however, do not resolve the central issue of novelty. Most of the manuscript applies established robustness-testing methodologies to an additional simulated classification problem and obtains results that are broadly consistent with existing understanding of distribution shift, incomplete training distributions, and confidence miscalibration. Its most specific finding concerns a vulnerability of one reconstructed classifier architecture, while its broadest conclusion—that each simulator-trained classifier should be individually tested before operational deployment—is already a standard principle of model validation."*

**Response.** The revision's broadest conclusion is no longer that each classifier should be tested. It is that a classifier's errors on a survey come in two classes that the mission must treat differently, that under the consortium's own prior most of this classifier's errors are of the class no method can fix, that the departures and the real spectra sort cleanly into the two classes, and that the existing fallback fails on the recoverable class unless it models the departure (Section 4). The reproducible code and the pre-registration the reviewer notes are extended to every new experiment: each expectation was committed to the public repository before its run, and all 31 outcomes, including the ones not met and the three never run, are in Table C1.

**Where.** Sections 4 and 5; Table C1; Data and Code Availability.

### B14

*"I therefore do not recommend publication in its present form. The manuscript would be more appropriately characterized as a reproducibility and robustness benchmark than as a study establishing a new scientific principle. To justify reconsideration as an original research contribution, the author would need to move beyond documenting the limits of simulated classifiers and establish a consequential, non-obvious result about the propagation of simulator misspecification into Ariel's scientific decision-making or population-level inference. The existing experiments may provide a useful foundation for such work, but the present conclusions do not yet demonstrate that advance."*

**Response.** The revision was built to this sentence. The population-level result is the prevalence of undecidable labels and of irrecoverable errors under the consortium's prior (Sections 3.3 and 3.4). The decision-level result is the propagation of each departure, and of the real spectra's departures from the prior, into Tier-2 targets lost at a fixed budget, for the classifier, for the existing rules and for the retrieval that is meant to be the stronger method (Sections 3.5 and 3.6), with the sign of the effect, a correct flag with the wrong destination is worse than no flag, stated as the part a pipeline design has to get right (Section 4). The non-obvious parts, in the sense that the pre-registered expectations did not anticipate them or that the existing method is contradicted, are that the retrieval which is the oracle on clean spectra is worse than the classifier under haze and worse than no retrieval when a budget is routed to it, that the cheap identifiability test is wrong in scale by an order of magnitude, and that the real warm giants lie outside the simulator population in a quantity that no input-space rule sees. The benchmark of the original note remains in the paper as Section 3.2 because it located the mechanism the rest of the paper tests.

**Where.** Sections 3.3 to 3.7; Sections 4 and 5.
