### A2. External validation on WASP-39 b

*"He recommends that if you externally validate your results, the manuscript would then generate enough knowledge to become publishable as an original paper. He is of the opinion that you pursue this avenue, since you have already put in considerable work. He suggests using WASP-39 b, which is an exoplanet included in Ariel's planned target program, and it has already been observed by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). However, Ariel and JWST are separate space telescopes. WASP-39 b is not part of the Ariel instrument or system; it is a planetary target that Ariel can observe. This makes WASP-39 b particularly relevant to the manuscript because the same planetary atmosphere can be studied using existing JWST observations and simulated Ariel measurements. The author could compare the classifier's predictions against JWST-derived atmospheric properties, **provided the observed spectra are made compatible with the classifier's input requirements** with adequate justification of the compatibility procedure. That would provide an independent observational test of the simulator-trained classifier, rather than another sensitivity test using entirely simulated spectra."*

**Response.** Done. I converted the JWST benchmark spectrum of WASP-39 b (the four Early Release Science modes re-reduced uniformly by Carter & May et al. 2024, extended to 7.8 µm with the MIRI LRS spectrum of Powell et al. 2024) to the classifier's 104-bin Tier-3 input, and I justify the conversion in three ways.

The procedure (Section 2.6): JWST products are transit depths, the quantity the classifier was trained on, so no conversion of quantity is needed. Each Tier-3 bin takes the inverse-variance-weighted mean of the JWST points inside it, partial overlaps weighted by the overlap fraction. Modes are aligned through their overlaps onto NIRISS order 1, which for WASP-39 b recovers the PRISM offset of −177 ppm that Carter & May report from their joint fit. On the panel the reference is the mode with the most points, and the two G395H detectors share one offset. Bins with no JWST data are filled flat at the spectrum's measured mean and flagged, and the number of filled bins is reported with every verdict (one of 104 for WASP-39 b). Per-spectrum normalisation then removes any global offset, as in training.

The justification (Section 2.6) has three parts. (a) A round trip on a spectrum whose answer is known: the benchmark's own best-fit model binned directly onto the 104 bins, against the same model sampled at each JWST mode's wavelength grid with the per-point errors and pushed through the conversion. The two differ by 22 ppm rms and 104 ppm at worst, with no verdict changed. (b) A sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations (the individual reductions of each mode, two NIRISS pipelines, fitted and fixed limb darkening, offsets on and off, model and flat fill, with and without MIRI, PRISM alone, and the Na, K, SO₂ and CO bins masked singly and together), following Roy-Perez et al. (2026), who showed that WASP-39 b's retrieved abundances move by more than an order of magnitude with the reduction. (c) The noise regimes: JWST precision as delivered and Ariel Tier-1 noise (the payload model's per-bin noise at the planet's Tier-1 transit count, added as 300 Gaussian draws for WASP-39 b and 500 for the panel), with the Tier-3 count run for WASP-39 b only.

The outcome (Section 3.4, "WASP-39 b"): at JWST precision three of four learners call H₂O present and all four call CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at the 10⁻⁴ threshold, which agrees with the published composition on the two scored labels (H₂O present and CH₄ absent; CO₂ and NH₃ are unscored). Those two labels are thin: both were set after the verdicts, the H₂O label is a judgement call between papers, and the CH₄ label rests on a limit from equilibrium models (Section 2.6). The MLP calls H₂O absent with a score of 0.00 in 23 of 24 configurations and on the benchmark's own model binned directly with no conversion, so the miss is not the conversion's, and the clear twin is found by all four learners, so it is not the composition either. Under Ariel Tier-1 noise at WASP-39 b's one transit, H₂O is reported in 58 % of draws with all inputs and 86 % without the three optical points. Under the training prior at the Tier-1 σ, a retrieval of the real spectrum decides CH₄ absent and gives H₂O a p_true of 0.0006, although three of the four learners call H₂O present (Section 3.4; Table C1, row B6).

Because one planet is a weak external test, I converted twenty-two further planets with public JWST transmission spectra, in 25 reductions, by the same procedure, in three batches, with labels from the published retrievals and ambiguous labels left unscored. The manuscript states when each batch's labels were committed relative to its verdicts (Section 2.6), and every label's published value, error and source is tabulated in the repository (`results/jwst_panel/label_sources.csv`). That table came from an audit of all 104 cells after the verdicts. Two K2-18 b H₂O labels, committed as absent, rested on limits above the 10⁻⁴ threshold, so I withdrew them, which leaves 64 scored labels and takes two right labels from every design's score. Of the 64, 56 follow the rule as written, one departs from it (TOI-270 d's NH₃ in the Felix reduction) and seven are judgement calls. Two unscored cells meet the rule for an absent label (HD 209458 b's CO₂ and WASP-52 b's NH₃). Apart from the two withdrawn labels, the labels stand as committed, and every score is also reported under the set that follows the rule throughout (65 labels) and the set that also removes every judgement call (59 labels) (Section 2.6; Appendix E).

An audit of the data path after the independent test found errors, and I corrected them in every result and for every design (Sections 2.6 and 2.10). The two G395H detectors had been given separate offsets, which on TOI-270 d reversed the 3.3 µm CH₄ contrast. Half-widths in the WASP-107 b files were read as full widths, which discarded 13 measured bins. An instrument profile width in the TOI-270 d files was read as a bin width. HIP 67522 b carried a catalogue mass of 186 Earth masses, where the measured value is 13.8. The test's failure prompted this audit, so it is post hoc, and the manuscript says so: the corrected panel is a development set for everything designed after it (Section 4, "Limitations of the real panel and the routing method"). Each outcome in Table C1 is given as read when its expectation was tested, with the corrected value beside it where the corrections changed it.

Over the 64 labels of the panel, with a split counted as half, the re-implemented classifier scores 43.5 at JWST precision, right on 36 of 38 absent labels and 3 of 26 present ones, and the AIRS-only variant 50.0; at Tier-1 noise they score 39.6 and 41.4, against 38 for calling every label absent. The conversion's validation holds at near-complete coverage, and at the panel's partial coverage the control is each planet's clear twin through the same mask. That control shows that the mask itself misleads the re-implemented classifier on eleven present labels (Section 3.4, "What the coverage mask does"), and every design is scored separately on the 47 labels that pass it (Table E3). On the fifteen reductions with measured data both below 1.1 µm and above 1.95 µm the three input variants score 26.5, 29.5 and 32.0 of 38. Most of the gain from dropping the optical points lies in the first batch, and the manuscript gives each batch's score. The eight methane reductions are five planets, and the manuscript reports the result per planet as well as per reduction (Table 8). The expectations for the WASP-39 b test and the panel, and their outcomes, are rows A1 to P6 of Table C1. Twelve further planets, found by a systematic search and labelled blind, were scored once as an independent test (Sections 2.7 and 3.5; Tables 3 and 10). The classifier called all 24 of their labels absent. I also describe the new design of Section 3.7 on these real spectra (Table 15). It is right on WASP-39 b's two scored labels at JWST precision and on them in 96 and 100 % of the Tier-1 draws, but every label had been seen before it was frozen, so this is description and not confirmation.

### A3. Identifiability

*"Then, two main advances beyond the WASP-39 b validation would be required: First, establish identifiability. Determine whether physically distinct atmospheric compositions can produce indistinguishable Ariel spectra, and quantify how frequently such ambiguity occurs under explicitly defined atmospheric populations. This would identify a fundamental limit on molecular classification rather than merely another sensitivity to simulation parameters."*

**Response.** Done, on the whole test population, with two instruments (Section 2.4). The first is a Δχ² test: for each planet and each molecule, the best-fitting atmosphere of the opposite label, with the molecule held at the 10⁻⁴ boundary and the population's nuisance parameters free, scored against the true spectrum at the planet's Tier-1 per-bin noise (eq1). The second is a nested-sampling retrieval of every one of the 955 test planets with the same free quantities, which gives for every label the posterior probability of the true class, the P-statistic of Bocchieri et al. (2023) evaluated for the true class. The two agree in rank (Spearman 0.80 to 0.89), and the retrieval calibrates the test's scale, which differs by an order of magnitude from the conventional one (the posterior crosses 0.95 at Δχ² of 0.45 to 0.98, not at 3.84).

The population is explicitly defined: the planets and abundances of POP-I at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, retrieved under POP-III's prior, with the payload noise model of Section 2.2. Under it (Section 3.2; Table 4), 32 % of CH₄ labels, 54 % of H₂O, 44 % of CO₂ and 32 % of NH₃ are not decided at the 0.95 level, 35 to 62 % when decidability is defined by a Bayes factor against the prior's odds. A fifth of the labels lie within half a decade of the 10⁻⁴ threshold and nine in ten of those are undecidable, so the fractions away from the threshold are 18, 44, 33 and 19 %; that row is in Table 4. Only 17 % of planets have all four labels decidable and 3 % have none. Retrieved under the population's own abundance prior, the undecidable fraction rises by five points on a 200-planet subsample. Three properties of the retrieval are stated with the result (Section 2.4): the posteriors are of the noise-free spectrum; the abundance prior gives absent labels a head start of 0.71; and the forward model is the training model with the radius fixed, so "undecidable" means not decided at the 0.95 level under this prior and this model. On a random 200 planets retrieved on the classifier's own noisy realisation (Section 3.2, "Noisy realisations"), the undecidable fractions move by at most 6 points and 5 to 12 % of labels change class each way. The retrieval's own accuracy, 92 to 94 %, is the ceiling the spectra allow under this prior, and the classifier sits 9 to 16 points below it. This is the limit the editor asked for, stated as a prevalence under two named populations and not as a physical limit (Section 4, "Scope"); Section 3.4 measures how far the real planets sit from that population. The second population is 200 Mission Candidate Sample planets with FastChem equilibrium chemistry and clear atmospheres, retrieved under the same prior: 19 % of CH₄ labels, 24 % of H₂O, 14 % of CO₂ and 7 % of NH₃ are not decided (intervals 14-25, 18-29, 10-19 and 4-11), and 56 % of the classifier's errors fall on them, against 88 % for POP-I on the same planets by the same vote. There the matched ensemble makes 21.1 errors on labels the spectrum settles against the classifier's 43.3 over ten noise draws, and 58.1 against 56.0 on those it does not, with balanced credit above the classifier's for every gas; its training population shares FastChem with this test, which flatters it, and false-present reaches 25 % for H₂O (Section 3.7). The prevalence therefore depends strongly on the population's chemistry, which I report as a result, and the alternative prior moves it by five points on the subsample (Section 3.2; Section 4, "Scope"). Freeing the radius on 200 planets moves it by at most two points (Section 4). The new design leaves undecidable labels undecided: its errors on them fall, from 562 to 490 on clean spectra, because it leans the right way more often, and they stay above the roughly 250 a retrieval makes on the same labels.

**Where.** Section 2.4; Section 3.2; Section 3.7, "The two error classes again"; Tables 4 and 16; Figure 3a; Table C1 (rows B1 to B7); Section 4.

### A4. A remedy with measurable value

*"Second, establish a remedy with measurable value. Develop and evaluate a diagnostic that recognizes when the classifier is likely to be unreliable, and demonstrate that its use improves molecular classification or target selection compared with existing methods."*

**Response.** Addressed in three parts that answer separate questions. The remedy I put forward is a new design, the matched ensemble, which gains on two held-out model grids and comes with its own confidence as an indicator of unreliability (Sections 2.10 and 3.7; Tables 13 to 16; Figure 6). A first method, which routes spectra by two flags, gained labels on the panel it was built on and not on 12 new planets, and I report it as that (Sections 2.7 and 3.5; Tables 10 and E3 to E5). Separately, a retrieval budget routed by a flag is measured in Tier-2 targets lost at a fixed 60 % selection against the classifier alone, the five decline rules and a retrieval of every planet (Section 3.6; Table 12).

The routing method (Sections 2.7 and 3.5) needs the Mission Candidate Sample's bulk parameters and the spectrum, with no retrieval and no published abundance, and it assumes an H₂/He scale height and a correct catalogue mass. Its amplitude flag is the catalogue's five-scale-height modulation over the spectrum's noise-corrected dispersion, with thresholds read from the simulated population through each reduction's own coverage. Its shape flag fires when the optical-excess score or the input disagreement exceeds its clean threshold. A planet above the amplitude threshold goes to a design retrained with aerosols, a planet below the lower cut has its verdict withheld, and on every other planet a cell the shape flag marks goes to the AIRS-only variant. The flags therefore recognise two failures the real spectra show and send each to its own remedy. With HIP 67522 b's mass corrected, its amplitude ratio lies inside the cuts, no flag fires on it and its false CO₂ stands. The lower cut then fires on no reduction, so nothing on the panel is withheld.

With a split or withheld verdict counted as half, it scores 52.0 of 64 labels at JWST precision against 43.5 for the re-implemented classifier, a difference of 8.5 with a descriptive 95 % bootstrap interval over planets of 2.5 to 14.1. Six labels go from wrong to right and none from right to wrong, and methane is found on six of the eight reductions against none. At Tier-1 noise the gain is 2.3 (0.9 to 3.8), and methane is found on about one of the eight. Its cost in simulation is 4.8 points of clean accuracy, and in the selection it loses as many CH₄ targets as the re-implemented classifier on clean spectra (20), 11 fewer under haze, and 16 more H₂O targets on clean spectra.

The manuscript states the limits with equal weight (Section 4, "What the routing method and the matched ensemble claim"). On the development panel the method beats each of the five existing rules used as routers (45.0 to 47.0), but it cannot be separated from two simpler fixes, the AIRS-only variant used everywhere (50.0; difference 2.0, −1.0 to 5.6) and the haze+deck-retrained design used everywhere (50.5; difference 1.5, −2.9 to 5.3). In simulation the retrained designs do better, in accuracy and in the selection. About two fifths of the gain at JWST precision lies on labels the coverage mask hides. The flag and its thresholds were built after the panel was known (Table C3). I then froze the method and applied it to 12 new planets, where it gained nothing (16.0 of 24 for both, Table 10), so the improvement on the development panel did not carry over, and three of the existing rules used as routers scored above it there (16.5 to 17.5). The AIRS-only input's methane recovery did repeat. The method does not address the undecidable class: it neither recognises nor withholds such labels (Section 3.5, first paragraph).

The matched ensemble (Section 2.10) answers three faults the audit measured in the published population and recipe: abundances drawn independently, hard votes that set a decision threshold nobody chose, and missing bins filled flat. Each reduction gets its own five multilayer perceptrons, trained only on the bins it measures, at its own errors, on a population with correlated chemistry and a broad one, and each molecule's threshold is set on rows held out from training. It needs the catalogue's bulk parameters and the reduction's mask and errors, with no retrieval and no published composition. I committed the candidates and the selection rule before any third-round score, chose on the rainout grid alone, froze the design and scored it once on a grid from the development grid's code and on a photochemical grid from another code family, all read through the JWST coverage masks.

On the held-out grids (Table 13; Figure 6) it scores above every other design at both precisions. On the local-condensation grid its balanced credit is 0.711 at JWST precision and 0.636 at Tier-1 noise, against 0.574 and 0.531 for the routing method, gains of 0.137 (95 % interval 0.110 to 0.156) and 0.105 (0.089 to 0.119). On the photochemical grid it is 0.773 and 0.700, against 0.658 and 0.567, gains of 0.115 (0.089 to 0.136) and 0.133 (0.120 to 0.144). The gain holds for each of the four gases at both precisions on both grids, every interval excluding zero. The re-implemented classifier, the AIRS-only input and the haze+deck-retrained design score 0.524 to 0.682 on these grids. A nested-sampling retrieval with this study's four-gas model is scored on a random 90 photochemical-grid spectra (77 finished at JWST precision in the first run): the ensemble scores above it by 0.197 to 0.236 at JWST precision and 0.095 to 0.126 at Tier-1 noise (Section 3.7, "Against a full retrieval"), a comparison with that retrieval, which likely misfits the grid's chemistry, not with richer ones. The five existing rules are scored on them at the same coverage (Section 3.7, "Against the existing rules").

The counterfactual on the photochemical grid tests what the verdict reads (Table 14). Where a gas truly exceeds 10⁻⁴, it is called present on 62.7 % of full spectra and on 11.4 % of spectra with its opacity removed and its chemistry unchanged, so 82 % of the present calls flip to absent (own-absorption difference 0.513, interval 0.473 to 0.553). The design therefore mostly reads the molecule's own absorption. About a fifth of its present calls, and a quarter for H₂O and CO₂, rest on other gases.

At Ariel's coverage, on POP-I at Tier-1 noise (Table 16), its accuracy is 84.2 % on clean spectra and 80.5 % under the column-filling haze, against 81.2 and 63.5 % for the re-implemented classifier and 80.2 and 73.6 % for the haze+deck-retrained design. Over ten noise draws at 60 % slots it loses 16.2 CH₄ and 30.9 H₂O targets on clean spectra, against 15.4 and 47.3, so it has no methane gain there, and 15.8 and 47.3 under haze, against 35.1 and 89.7. Through all 104 bins on the two held-out grids at Tier-1 noise it gains 0.116 and 0.122 over the re-implemented classifier.

On the real labels (Table 15), seen beforehand but used for no design choice, it scores 63.4 of 88 at Tier-1 noise against 57.9 for the routing method, a gain of 5.6 (from unrounded scores; 0.7 to 10.7). The gain is in present labels, 17.3 of 34 against 7.2, and it costs absent ones, 46.2 of 54 against 50.7. At JWST precision, on the 41 labels outside the panel the routing method was built on, it scores 35.0 against 29.5, a gain of 5.5 (0.5 to 10.5), a split I drew after seeing the scores because the panel is where that method was developed. Of these, the 12 new planets give 19.0 of 24 against 16.0, and that batch motivated the per-reduction design of the first round, so it is not an independent test of this line of work. Over all 105 labels it scores 84.0 against 81.5 (+2.5; −6.5 to 11.5) and against 83.0 for the haze+deck-retrained design. Methane is found on six of the eight reductions at JWST precision and on 4.7 on average at Tier-1 noise, where every other design finds at most 1.7.

The diagnostic is the design's own probability (Section 3.7, "Recognising its own unreliable verdicts" and "Against the existing rules"). It ranks labels at AUROC 0.75 to 0.79 on the local-condensation grid, 0.87 to 0.91 on the photochemical grid and 0.89 on the 105 real labels. The measured gain against existing methods is in the design and its confidence together: the confidence rules that fail on the classifier work on this design, and its kept labels score far higher (0.768 against 0.614 on the photochemical grid at JWST precision, averaged over gases), almost entirely through the better learner. Under aerosols and spots at Ariel's coverage the margin ranks errors at AUROC 0.70 to 0.78 (the classifier 0.64 to 0.78), and it falls to 0.62 to 0.67 under extra noise and muting, below the classifier. Declining alone is a smaller matter. With labels kept within each gas, declining the 30 % nearest a probability of 0.5 adds 0.007 to 0.045 to balanced credit on the three grids, against 0.006 to 0.033 for the best existing rule on the re-implemented classifier, separated from zero in two of six cases, so I do not claim that the decline rule alone beats the existing rules. A pooled reading would show 0.075 to 0.117, and I report why that overstates it. Stratifying the rule, keeping the most confident 70 % of present and of absent calls separately, adds 0.044 to 0.078 and beats the classifier's best stratified rule by 0.034 to 0.055, with the interval above zero in five of six cases. That result is post hoc on the grids and does not carry to the real labels (−0.034 at JWST precision, −0.038 at Tier-1 noise, intervals including zero), so it is a lead for the prospective test. In selection, declining loses targets for both designs, so its value is a withheld verdict. The diagnostic with a measured gain over existing methods in selection is the flag routing a retrieval budget (below), in simulation and for one departure.

Its limits are stated in Section 4 ("What the routing method and the matched ensemble claim"). The real labels had been seen, so they cannot serve as a blind test. Over all 105 at JWST precision it is not separated from the routing method or the haze+deck-retrained design, and on the routing method's own panel it scores 49.0 against 52.0 of 64 (−3.0; −10.0 to 4.5), because it calls more absent labels present: over all labels at that precision absent credit falls from 59.0 to 54.0 of 64 while present credit rises from 22.5 to 30.0 of 41, a fall above the three labels the expectation allowed. The false-present rate reached 17.3 % on the local-condensation grid at both precisions, above the 15 % limit I committed and the 10 % the thresholds were set for, and 34 % for CH₄ on that grid, and at a prevalence of 10 % only 0.22 to 0.46 of its present calls would be right. A present verdict is therefore a reason to look again and not a detection. It loses more CH₄ targets than the re-implemented classifier under the confined haze (25.9 against 17.5) and the high deck (33.5 against 28.0). A population muted threefold still costs it 49.9 CH₄ and 74.4 H₂O targets, against 54.9 and 94.0. NH₃ stays weak (0.550 on the photochemical grid at JWST precision), and the real JWST score moves from 83 to 86 of 105 across seeds. The grids are models: the local-condensation grid shares its code with the rainout grid on which the design was chosen, so only the photochemical grid is another code family, and that grid has no aerosols. It does not settle undecidable labels. The next step is a prospective test. The rules are committed, so the design can be applied unchanged to each new public spectrum, with its verdict recorded before that spectrum's published retrieval is scored. That test would turn the description on real spectra into confirmation.

The retrieval-budget answer has three parts. First (Section 3.6, "A retrieval budget on clean spectra"; Table C2), on clean, noise-free simulated spectra the retrieval of every planet is the Bayes-optimal selector, as a correctly specified retrieval must be: ranking by its present-posterior loses 0 of 359 CH₄-bearing targets, against 20 for the classifier, at 365 core-hours. No cheap rule routes a smaller budget much better than a random one, because the confidence rules send it to the undecidable labels, where the retrieval cannot help either. On the 200-planet subsample the retrieval of the classifier's own noisy realisation loses 1 of 74 CH₄ targets, the noise-free one 0 and the classifier 1, and 2, 1 and 12 of 80 H₂O targets.

Second (Section 3.6, "The clear-model retrieval under haze"; Table 11; Figure 3c), the clear-model retrieval shares the classifier's failure: run with the training forward model on the hazed spectra of all 954 planets, it calls almost every present label absent and is confidently wrong on 99 % of present labels. With the haze density as one free parameter it recovers 222 of the 224 present methane labels the clean spectra decide, a best case that the manuscript labels as such because the truth lies inside that family; a power-law aerosol, which does not contain the truth, recovers most of the loss on the 117 planets run, and its pinned slope and lower evidence show the wrong family. The clear-model retrieval's evidence does give the failure away, and the manuscript reports that as a gate.

Third (Section 3.6, "A budget routed by a shift flag"; Table 12; Figure 5), the diagnostic: the optical-excess score, chosen before the runs because Section 3.3 had located the haze failure in those inputs. On a mixed population in which a fifth of the planets are hazed, routing the 20 % of planets it ranks most suspect to the free-haze retrieval loses 13 ± 2 of 359 CH₄ targets. The classifier alone loses 29 ± 4, a random budget 20 ± 4, the margin and learner-spread rules 20 to 22, the clear-model retrieval of every planet read by its posterior 69, and a haze oracle that knows which planets are hazed 12 ± 2. The Mahalanobis rule, used as a router, loses the same number within noise (13 ± 2). The score's advantage over the distance rules is therefore its recall and selectivity on the hazed planets, not the selection, and the improvement comes from routing to a retrieval that models the departure. The same budget routed to the clear-model retrieval loses 71 ± 8 targets, against 29 with no retrieval. Table 12 gives the same results with 10 and 50 % of the planets hazed. The expectations for this test were committed after a pilot on the 124 planets whose fixed-density hazed posteriors existed first, and before any population result existed (Section 2.8; Table C1, rows M1 to M5).

What the diagnostic does not do is also stated. It does not beat a clear-model retrieval of every planet re-run with the haze density free wherever its evidence fails, which loses no methane target at about 520 core-hours against 127 for the budget. It does not help the irrecoverable class: the classifier's own confidence recognises that class only in part (AUROC 0.71 to 0.77 for three molecules, 0.6 for water), a flag trained on the retrieval does better only for water, and even perfect recognition would recover four of 359 methane targets at a 20 % budget. At the weaker density of 2 × 10⁶ m⁻³ the flag's sensitivity falls from 98 to 60 % and the routing without a retrieval recovers little of the loss, which moves to H₂O (Section 3.6, "The weaker haze").

### A5. The two classes of error

*"The most interesting possible outcome is that you discover two distinct classes of error: 1. Recoverable computational errors: The spectrum contains sufficient molecular information, but the classifier interprets it incorrectly because of its training assumptions. 2. Irrecoverable information limitations: Different atmospheric compositions produce observations that cannot be distinguished at the instrument's precision, making a definitive molecular classification unjustified. The manuscript already provides preliminary evidence for the first category through its haze-retraining experiments. You has not systematically investigated the second. Distinguishing these two categories, quantifying their prevalence, and developing a method to recognize them would be a much stronger scientific contribution than the present sensitivity analysis. It would also produce a result with implications beyond Ariel, potentially extending to other simulator-trained scientific inference systems."*

**Response.** This became the paper's spine. The definition (Section 2.5): an error is recoverable if the posterior probability of the true class on the same spectrum exceeds 0.95, undecidable if it lies between 0.05 and 0.95, and misled if below 0.05; under a departure the departure-aware Δχ² at the calibrated threshold is used. Because the posterior is of the noise-free spectrum, the recoverable shares are upper bounds, and the gap is measured on the 200-planet subsample (Section 3.2, "Noisy realisations": 18.5 % of the learners' errors recoverable by the noise-free posterior, 14.9 % decidable by the realisation's). The split of a departure's errors into those shared with the clean run and those introduced was made after the runs and is labelled as such.

Prevalence (Section 3.3; Table 7; Figure 3): about four fifths of the classifier's errors on clean spectra fall on labels not settled at the 0.95 level. That split depends on the level: the posterior leans to the right answer on 74 % of those errors at 0.5 (Section 3.2). Haze and unocculted spots leave the labels decidable when their strength is known, and 57 to 87 % of the errors they introduce are recoverable. A high cloud deck removes information on absent labels (51 to 82 % of its introduced errors recoverable). Threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction and leaves 10 to 56 % recoverable. The clearest demonstration is a pair of retrievals on the same 954 hazed spectra (Section 3.6; Figure 3c): with the training forward model the retrieval is confidently wrong on 99 % of present labels; with the haze density added as one free parameter it recovers the density on every planet and 222 of the 224 CH₄ labels the clean spectra decide, while keeping 75 % of them decidable, which is the price of fitting the strength rather than knowing it.

Recognition (Section 3.6, "Recognising undecidable labels" and "The existing rules under the shift"): the shift detectors find hazed planets, not recoverable errors, and no flag recognises the undecidable labels better than the learner spread, except for H₂O, where a flag trained on the retrieval does better (Section 3.6). The irrecoverable class is marked in part by the classifier's own confidence (AUROC 0.59 to 0.77). Recognising it is worth a withheld verdict rather than a retrieval, and the routing method does not attempt it (Section 3.5). The matched ensemble's confidence was measured against its errors and not against the two classes (Section 3.7, "Recognising its own unreliable verdicts").

On real data (Section 3.4): the methane misses on the five sub-Neptunes are recoverable in the operational sense that another input reading the same spectrum finds the label; the transplants show that the inputs below 1.95 µm gate the verdicts in general, so they do not locate a methane signal. Under the posterior definition with the training prior at Tier-1 precision, however, one of the classifier's 16 wrong labels is recoverable: 6 are undecidable and 9 misled, and the prior reads muted real spectra as low abundance (Table 9). Ten of the twelve real giants are muted 2.3 to 5.3 times against their clear twins, in every bootstrap resample beyond what the population can produce at their bulk parameters and published compositions (Figure 4), though not at lower abundances, so amplitude is degenerate with abundance within the prior. The scaled-twin test shows that uniform muting costs labels at Tier-1 noise and not at JWST precision, and Appendix E carries a muted population into selection.

The new design gives the classes a further test (Section 3.7, "The two error classes again"; Table 16). Classed by the clean-spectrum posterior of Section 2.5, under the column-filling haze the re-implemented classifier makes 675 recoverable errors and 719 on undecidable labels, the haze+deck-retrained design 360 and 648, and the matched ensemble 202 and 544. On clean spectra the re-implemented classifier makes 154 and 562, and the matched ensemble 114 and 490. Under every departure most of the errors it removes are recoverable (59 to 73 %), and on clean spectra most lie on undecidable labels (72 of 112): a better learner leans the right way more often on an unsettled label, but it does not settle it. These counts class each label by the clean-spectrum posterior, so they ignore information a departure removes; the 372 errors counted as recoverable under threefold noise lie on labels decided at the nominal noise. The class split therefore survives the change of design in that sense (Table C1, row W1, met in part).

The implication beyond Ariel has its own paragraph (Section 4, "Beyond Ariel"). The split can be measured by running the expensive method on the population the cheap one is tested on, and only where that method's model is right: here it worked on simulated spectra, whereas on hazed and real spectra the retrieval shared the misspecification and could not class the errors. The procedure, and not any one classifier's numbers, is what could transfer. I have run it on this classifier and its successor only: no decidability was measured for the second classifier of Appendix B.

**Where.** Title; Abstract; Section 1; Sections 2.5 and 2.10; Sections 3.3 to 3.7; Section 4; Tables 7, 9 and 16; Figures 3 and 4; Table C1 (rows B4 and W1).

### B3

### B4

*"However, the manuscript contains one result that deserves closer consideration than the broader robustness analysis. ### B5

*"Nevertheless, even this finding requires a stronger interpretation to qualify as a novel scientific contribution. The observation that a classifier trained without chromatic atmospheric haze may assign inappropriate significance to haze-sensitive optical measurements is physically intelligible from the construction of the training data. The author has demonstrated the magnitude of the vulnerability for a particular implementation, but has not established a new physical mechanism, a general mathematical principle governing the failure, or a previously unrecognized limitation of atmospheric spectroscopy. **Response.** The revision does not claim a new physical mechanism or a mathematical principle. The finding is interpreted in three ways it was not before. It is classed: the retrieval shows that haze leaves the labels decidable when its strength is known, so the haze errors can be repaired, whereas the errors noise introduces mostly cannot (Section 3.3, Table 7). It is tested on real planets: at WASP-39 b's Tier-1 noise H₂O is reported in 58 % of draws with the optical points and 86 % without them, and with all inputs the five methane-bearing sub-Neptunes are found on none of eight reductions (absent on six, split on two), and with the AIRS channels alone on seven of eight, and the optical points alone do not explain this (Section 3.4, Table 8). ### B6

*"A more consequential issue concerns the distinction between robustness within a simulation framework and predictive validity for actual Ariel observations. As the author acknowledges in Section 3.7, all evaluated spectra are simulated, including those assigned to known Ariel targets. This distinction is fundamental. A classifier that performs poorly under a particular simulated haze prescription is not necessarily equally unreliable under the actual distribution of atmospheric hazes encountered by Ariel. Conversely, a classifier that performs well under the tested perturbations cannot be presumed reliable against physical processes absent from all the simulations. The study quantifies sensitivity to selected modeling choices but does not independently establish the relationship between simulated robustness and real-world classification reliability."*

**Response.** This is the point the external test answers (Part A2 above), and the answer is specific rather than general. On WASP-39 b, which lies inside the training distribution by every input-space rule, the classifier is mostly right, and its one wrong learner is not wrong because of the conversion, the composition or a grey deck. On the real planets that carry the molecule, the methane sub-Neptunes, it finds none of eight reductions, with six absent and two split. The clear twins through the same coverage and noise are found by a majority of learners in 63 to 100 % of draws on six of the seven reductions with a twin, so those misses are not the noise; on the seventh reduction the twin is never found, so that miss is the coverage. The coverage mask alone misleads the re-implemented classifier on eleven present labels of the panel. Ten of the twelve real giants with published abundances are muted beyond the simulator population's reach at their bulk parameters, which measures directly, in one quantity, how the real distribution differs from the training prior (Section 3.4, "Muting"; Figure 4; Table D1), with bootstrap intervals on every muting value. Designs are scored at Tier-1 noise on the same real spectra, and the relation the reviewer asks about is stated: across seven designs, simulated accuracy on clean spectra ranks them opposite to the real panel at JWST precision (Spearman −0.50), under haze with it (+0.64), under twofold noise against it (−0.29), and the other cases say little. On the 12 new planets the clean ranking runs with the score (+0.34), so these descriptive values do not show that the clean benchmark picks the wrong design (Appendix E). A retrieval under the training prior reads the real spectra as absent (Table 9), and new planets were scored once with every rule frozen (Table 10). A second kind of test, independent of the real labels, is the grid from the development grid's code and the photochemical grid from another code family on which the matched ensemble was scored once (Table 13).
