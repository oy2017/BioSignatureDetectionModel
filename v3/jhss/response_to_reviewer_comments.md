### A2. External validation on WASP-39 b

*"He recommends that if you externally validate your results, the manuscript would then generate enough knowledge to become publishable as an original paper. He is of the opinion that you pursue this avenue, since you have already put in considerable work. He suggests using WASP-39 b, which is an exoplanet included in Ariel's planned target program, and it has already been observed by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). However, Ariel and JWST are separate space telescopes. WASP-39 b is not part of the Ariel instrument or system; it is a planetary target that Ariel can observe. This makes WASP-39 b particularly relevant to the manuscript because the same planetary atmosphere can be studied using existing JWST observations and simulated Ariel measurements. The author could compare the classifier's predictions against JWST-derived atmospheric properties, **provided the observed spectra are made compatible with the classifier's input requirements** with adequate justification of the compatibility procedure. That would provide an independent observational test of the simulator-trained classifier, rather than another sensitivity test using entirely simulated spectra."*

**Response.** Done. I converted the JWST benchmark spectrum of WASP-39 b (Carter & May et al. 2024; Powell et al. 2024) to the classifier's 104-bin input and, because one planet is a weak test, twenty-two further planets in 25 reductions.

*Procedure and justification* (Section 2.6; Appendix E). JWST gives transit depths, the quantity the classifier was trained on. Each Tier-3 bin takes the inverse-variance-weighted mean of the points inside it; modes are aligned through their overlaps (recovering the −177 ppm PRISM offset that Carter & May fit); empty bins are filled flat and counted with every verdict; normalisation removes any global offset, as in training. It is justified by a round trip on the benchmark's best-fit model (22 ppm rms, no verdict changed), a sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations following Roy-Perez et al. (2026), two noise regimes and, at partial coverage, each planet's clear twin read through the same mask.

*Outcome* (Section 3.4). On WASP-39 b at JWST precision three of four learners call H₂O present and all four call CH₄ absent, agreeing with both scored labels, which are thin (set after the verdicts; H₂O a judgement call; CH₄ from equilibrium models). The dissenting learner misses H₂O on the benchmark's model binned directly, so not through the conversion. Under Tier-1 noise H₂O is reported in 58 % of draws (86 % without the optical points), and a retrieval under the training prior gives it a p_true of 0.0006 (Table C1, row B6). On the panel's 64 labels the re-implemented classifier scores 43.5, right on 36 of 38 absent and 3 of 26 present labels, against 38 for calling all absent; the coverage mask alone misleads it on eleven present labels, so designs are also scored on the 47 that pass (Table E3). Most of the gain from dropping the optical points lies in the first batch, and the eight methane reductions are five planets (Table 8). On twelve new planets, labelled blind and scored once, it called all 24 labels absent (Tables 3 and 10).

*Labels and audits.* Section 2.6 states when each batch's labels were committed. An audit of the 104 label cells after the verdicts withdrew two K2-18 b labels, leaving 64, with scores also on two alternative label sets (Appendix E). A data-path audit prompted by the independent test's failure corrected offsets, bin widths and a catalogue mass throughout (Section 2.10); being post hoc, it makes the corrected panel a development set for later designs (Section 4), and Table C1 gives each outcome as first read beside its corrected value. The new design's real-spectrum results (A4) are description, not confirmation.

**Where.** Sections 2.6, 2.7 and 2.10; Tables 2 and 3; Sections 3.4, 3.5 and 3.7; Tables 8, 10, 15 and E3; Table C1 (rows A1 to P6, B6, H1 to H6, K1 to K3 and R4); Figure 4; Appendix E; `results/jwst_panel/label_sources.csv`.

### A3. Identifiability

*"Then, two main advances beyond the WASP-39 b validation would be required: First, establish identifiability. Determine whether physically distinct atmospheric compositions can produce indistinguishable Ariel spectra, and quantify how frequently such ambiguity occurs under explicitly defined atmospheric populations. This would identify a fundamental limit on molecular classification rather than merely another sensitivity to simulation parameters."*

**Response.** Done, on the whole test population, with two instruments (Section 2.4): a Δχ² test of the best-fitting opposite-label atmosphere (eq1), and a nested-sampling retrieval of all 955 test planets giving each label's posterior probability of the true class (the P-statistic of Bocchieri et al. 2023), which calibrates the test (Spearman 0.80 to 0.89).

Under the explicitly defined population (POP-I at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, retrieved under POP-III's prior), physically distinct compositions do produce indistinguishable spectra: 32 % of CH₄, 54 % of H₂O, 44 % of CO₂ and 32 % of NH₃ labels are not decided at the 0.95 level, and only 17 % of planets have all four decidable (Section 3.2; Table 4). The classifier sits 9 to 16 points below the retrieval, whose accuracy is reported as reference-model performance under its prior rather than as a ceiling (Section 2.4). On 200 Mission Candidate Sample planets with equilibrium chemistry and clear atmospheres the fractions are 19, 24, 14 and 7 %, so the prevalence depends strongly on the population's chemistry, which I report as a result.

The limits are stated with it (Section 2.4; Section 4). The posteriors are of the noise-free spectrum (on noisy realisations of 200 planets the fractions move by at most 6 points); the prior gives absent labels a head start of 0.71, and the population's own prior raises the fraction by five points on 200 planets; and the forward model is the training model with the radius fixed (freeing it moves the fractions by at most two points). "Undecidable" therefore means not decided at the 0.95 level under this prior and model, a measure of posterior assurance rather than of unreadability: on the same noisy spectra a reference rule still removes 38 to 48 % of the classifier's error (Section 3.2). The prevalence is one under named populations, not a physical limit (Section 4, "Scope"). The Δχ² test is calibrated to the posterior on clean spectra; its thresholds are low (0.45 to 0.98) because the prior's volume does much of the work, and a check minimising over the full opposite abundance range, not only at the boundary, is reported in Table C1 (row AD8). The errors on these labels are under A5.

**Where.** Section 2.4; Section 3.2; Section 3.7, "The two error classes again"; Tables 4 and 16; Figure 3a; Table C1 (rows B1 to B7); Section 4.

### A4. A remedy with measurable value

*"Second, establish a remedy with measurable value. Develop and evaluate a diagnostic that recognizes when the classifier is likely to be unreliable, and demonstrate that its use improves molecular classification or target selection compared with existing methods."*

**Response.** Addressed in three parts: a new design, the matched ensemble, whose own probability marks its unreliable verdicts (Sections 2.10 and 3.7); a first routing method, which failed its independent test (Sections 2.7 and 3.5); and a flag routing a retrieval budget, measured in Tier-2 targets (Section 3.6).

*The matched ensemble* needs no retrieval or published composition. Chosen on one grid by a rule committed beforehand, then frozen and scored once on two held-out grids through the JWST masks, it reaches balanced credit of 0.711 and 0.773 at JWST precision and 0.636 and 0.700 at Tier-1 noise, gains of 0.105 to 0.137 over the routing method for every gas, every interval excluding zero under a bootstrap over planets and also under one crossed over planets and the reused source models (Table 13; Figure 6). The gain is positive in every temperature, metallicity and C/O bin of the photochemical grid, and it moves by at most 0.017 when the grid's abundance is defined over other pressure windows. A post-hoc comparison with the candidate that came second in the design selection, a four-learner vote on the same broad population, shows that most of this gain lies in what the two share, training on each spectrum's measured bins at its real errors with calibrated thresholds; the network ensemble adds about 0.02 and a lower false-present rate, and at the routing method's false-present rate the ensemble's recall of present labels is higher in all 16 gas-grid-precision cases (Section 3.7, "Where the gain comes from"). On the photochemical grid it also scores above this study's four-gas retrieval, which likely misfits that grid's chemistry, and 82 % of its present calls flip when the molecule's opacity is removed (Table 14). At Ariel's coverage its accuracy under the column-filling haze is 80.5 %, against 63.5 % (Table 16). On real labels, seen beforehand but used for no design choice, it scores 63.4 of 88 against 57.9 for the routing method at Tier-1 noise (0.7 to 10.7) and finds methane on 4.7 of eight reductions, against at most 1.7 for any other design (Table 15).

*As a diagnostic* (Section 3.7), its probability ranks labels at AUROC 0.75 to 0.91 on the held-out grids and 0.89 on the real labels. The gain over existing methods lies in the design and its confidence together, almost entirely through the better learner (kept labels 0.768 against 0.614 for the classifier with its best rule). Declining alone adds 0.007 to 0.045 within each gas, against 0.006 to 0.033 for the classifier's best rule, separated in two of six cases, so I do not claim that it beats the existing rules; a stratified variant chosen after the grids did not carry to the real labels (Appendix C). Under extra noise and muting its error ranking falls below the classifier's, and declining loses targets in selection.

*Its limits* (Section 4). The real labels are no blind test: at JWST precision it leads only outside the routing method's panel (35.0 against 29.5 of 41, a split drawn after the scores), is not separated over all 105 labels, and scores 49.0 against 52.0 on that panel, losing more absent credit than the expectation allowed; the 12 new planets partly motivated its precursor. Its false-present rate reached 17.3 % on one grid, above the 15 % committed, and at a prevalence of 10 % only 0.22 to 0.46 of its present calls would be right, so a present verdict is a reason to look again. At Ariel's coverage it has no methane gain on clean spectra, loses more CH₄ targets under the confined haze and the high deck, and stays costly on a muted population (B14). NH₃ stays weak, the real score moves from 83 to 86 of 105 across seeds, only one held-out grid is from another code family and it has no aerosols, and undecidable labels stay undecided (A5). A prospective test on new public spectra under the committed rules is the next step.

*The routing method* (Sections 2.7 and 3.5; Appendix E) sends flagged spectra to another input or a haze-retrained design, assuming an H₂/He scale height and a correct catalogue mass. On its development panel it scored 52.0 of 64 against 43.5 (descriptive interval 2.5 to 14.1), above the five existing rules used as routers (45.0 to 47.0) but not separable from two simpler fixes; two fifths of that gain lay on labels the coverage mask hides, the flag was built after the panel was known (Table C3), and in simulation it cost 4.8 points of clean accuracy and trailed the retrained designs. On 12 new planets it gained nothing (16.0 of 24 for both; Table 10), and three existing rules used as routers scored above it. It does not address undecidable labels, and HIP 67522 b's false CO₂ stands.

*The retrieval budget* (Section 3.6; Tables 11, 12 and C2; Figure 5). On clean spectra a retrieval of every planet is the Bayes-optimal selector (0 of 359 CH₄ targets lost, against 20, at 365 core-hours), and no cheap rule routes a partial budget much better than a random one. Under haze the clear-model retrieval is confidently wrong on 99 % of present labels, while with the haze density free it recovers 222 of 224 decidable CH₄ labels, a best case since the truth lies in that family. With a fifth of the planets hazed, routing the 20 % the optical-excess score ranks most suspect to the free-haze retrieval loses 13 ± 2 of 359 CH₄ targets, against 29 ± 4 for the classifier alone, 20 ± 4 for a random budget, 20 to 22 for the confidence rules and 12 ± 2 for a haze oracle; the Mahalanobis rule does as well, and routed to the clear-model retrieval the budget loses 71 ± 8. Expectations were committed after a 124-planet pilot (rows M1 to M5). This holds in simulation, for one departure: it does not beat a retrieval of every planet re-run with the haze free where its evidence fails (about 520 core-hours against 127), does not help the irrecoverable class, and at the weaker haze its sensitivity falls from 98 to 60 %.

**Where.** Sections 2.7, 2.8 and 2.10; Sections 3.5, 3.6 and 3.7; Section 4, "What the routing method and the matched ensemble claim"; Tables 10 to 16 and E2 to E5; Figures 5 and 6; Table C1 (rows C1 to M5, F1 to F3, G1 to G5, H1 to H6, I1 to Z4 and AA1 to AA3); Tables C2, C3, D1 and D2; Appendices C and E.

### A5. The two classes of error

*"The most interesting possible outcome is that you discover two distinct classes of error: 1. Recoverable computational errors: The spectrum contains sufficient molecular information, but the classifier interprets it incorrectly because of its training assumptions. 2. Irrecoverable information limitations: Different atmospheric compositions produce observations that cannot be distinguished at the instrument's precision, making a definitive molecular classification unjustified. The manuscript already provides preliminary evidence for the first category through its haze-retraining experiments. You has not systematically investigated the second. Distinguishing these two categories, quantifying their prevalence, and developing a method to recognize them would be a much stronger scientific contribution than the present sensitivity analysis. It would also produce a result with implications beyond Ariel, potentially extending to other simulator-trained scientific inference systems."*

**Response.** This became the paper's spine. An error is recoverable if the posterior probability of the true class on the same spectrum exceeds 0.95, undecidable between 0.05 and 0.95 and misled below 0.05; under a departure the calibrated departure-aware Δχ² is used (Section 2.5). The posterior is of the noise-free spectrum, so recoverable shares are upper bounds (on 200 planets, 18.5 % of errors are recoverable by it and 14.9 % by the noisy realisation's; Section 3.2).

*Prevalence* (Sections 3.2 and 3.3; Table 7; Figure 3). About four fifths of the classifier's clean errors fall on undecidable labels, though the posterior leans the right way on 74 % of them and a reference rule on the same noisy spectra removes 38 to 48 % of the classifier's error, so the class marks a lack of assurance rather than an unreadable label, and the split depends on the 0.95 level; under a departure the classes are likelihood-based diagnostics read against the test's own clean row (Section 2.5); on the equilibrium-chemistry population of A3 the share is 56 %, against 88 % for POP-I. Haze and spots of known strength leave labels decidable (57 to 87 % of their introduced errors recoverable), a high deck removes information on absent labels, and threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction. The split into shared and introduced errors was made after the runs.

*Recognition* (Section 3.6; Appendix C, "Recognising undecidable labels in detail"). Shift detectors find hazed planets, not recoverable errors. The classifier's confidence marks the irrecoverable class only in part (AUROC 0.59 to 0.77), only for H₂O does a flag trained on the retrieval do better, the matched ensemble does about as well as the classifier (Table C1, row Y4), and recognising the class is worth a withheld verdict, not a retrieval.

*The new design* (Section 3.7, "The two error classes again"; Table 16). Under the column-filling haze the re-implemented classifier makes 675 recoverable errors and 719 on undecidable labels, the matched ensemble 202 and 544; on clean spectra 154 and 562 against 114 and 490, above the roughly 250 a retrieval makes. On the equilibrium-chemistry population it halves errors on decidable labels (21.1 against 43.3) and leaves the rest level (58.1 against 56.0), though that test shares FastChem with its training, which flatters it, and its false-present rate there reaches 25 % for H₂O. It leans the right way more often on unsettled labels but does not settle them; these counts ignore information a departure removes (row W1, met in part).

*Real data and beyond Ariel* (Sections 3.4 and 4). The real methane misses are recoverable only operationally (another input finds them; the transplants do not locate a methane signal); under the posterior definition one of 16 wrong real labels is recoverable, 6 undecidable and 9 misled (Table 9), and the giants' muting is degenerate with abundance within the prior (Figure 4). The split can be measured only where the expensive method's model is right, here on simulated spectra but not on hazed or real ones. What could transfer is the procedure, run on this classifier and its successor only; no decidability was measured for the second classifier of Appendix B.

**Where.** Title; Abstract; Section 1; Sections 2.5 and 2.10; Sections 3.2 to 3.7; Section 4; Tables 7, 9 and 16; Figures 3 and 4; Table C1 (rows B4, W1 and Y4); Appendix C.

## Part B. The reviewer's written report

### B1

*"The manuscript presents a technically substantial investigation of the robustness of machine-learning classifiers trained on simulated exoplanet transmission spectra. The author independently reconstructs previously published Ariel molecular classifiers, reproduces their reported accuracies, and subjects them to fifteen departures from the assumptions of the training simulator. The analysis includes atmospheric haze, stellar contamination, additional molecular absorbers, alternative opacity databases, radiative-transfer implementations, instrumental noise, and combinations of these perturbations. The author further investigates confidence calibration, out-of-distribution detection, domain randomization, and the effects of excluding particular spectral inputs. The computational work is extensive, and the independent reproduction of 48 published classification accuracies within 3.7 percentage points is a useful technical accomplishment."*

**Response.** Thank you. The reproduction is kept, shortened, as Section 3.1 and Appendix A, and was regenerated from scratch on a second machine for the revision (agreement to 0.05 points on average).

**Where.** Section 3.1; Appendix A; Figure 1.

### B2

*"Nevertheless, the central question for publication is not whether the work was carefully executed, but whether it establishes a sufficiently novel scientific proposition."*

**Response.** Agreed. The revision's propositions are new measurements rather than the proposition the reviewer rejects in B3; they are listed under B3 and B13 below.

**Where.** Section 1; Section 5.

### B3

*"The manuscript's principal conclusion is that classifiers trained on simulated spectra may perform poorly when confronted with spectra generated under different physical assumptions, that these failures may occur without corresponding reductions in predictive confidence, and that individual classifiers require independent robustness testing. These observations are consistent with the established literature on distribution shift, model misspecification, uncertainty calibration, and simulation-to-reality transfer. Indeed, the manuscript explicitly acknowledges that robustness testing is established practice and cites previous investigations of model misspecification in simulation-based inference and exoplanet atmospheric retrieval. Consequently, the general proposition that simulator-trained classifiers cannot be assumed reliable outside their training distributions is neither new nor particularly surprising. Demonstrating this principle in another application does not, by itself, constitute a substantive scientific advance."*

**Response.** Agreed; the revised paper no longer rests on that proposition but on measurements not available before: (1) under the training prior a third to a half of the labels are not decided at Tier-1 precision, about four fifths of the classifier's errors fall on them, and 38 to 48 % of its error is excess over a reference rule on the same noisy spectra (A3, A5); (2) the departures separate into the two classes (Table 7); (3) a retrieval of every planet is the Bayes-optimal selector on clean spectra yet fails more confidently than the classifier under haze (Table 11); (4) on real spectra a retrieval under the training prior mostly reads present labels as absent, as the classifier does, though the evidence does not separate a shared prior from other causes (Section 3.4); (5) a correct flag routed to a retrieval that does not model the departure loses more targets than no retrieval (Table 12); (6) a routing method's gain did not repeat on new planets, whereas a new design, frozen and scored once on two held-out grids, gains 0.105 to 0.137 of balanced credit over it at false-present rates of 8 to 17 % (A4). Two results were not among the committed expectations and are reported as such (Section 4): the scale of the cheap identifiability test, and the real giants' position outside the simulator population in feature amplitude at their published compositions, while inside it by every bulk parameter and, for seven of the ten, by all three distance rules.

**Where.** Abstract; Section 1, last two paragraphs; Sections 3.2 to 3.7; Section 4.

### B4

*"However, the manuscript contains one result that deserves closer consideration than the broader robustness analysis. In Section 3.3, the author finds that relatively weak atmospheric haze causes the classifiers to miss methane and water even when the corresponding infrared molecular absorption features remain substantially intact. The author attributes this failure to three optical photometric inputs, demonstrating that removing these inputs reduces the haze-associated accuracy loss from 17.7 to 5.6 percentage points, while restricting classification to the AIRS channels reduces the loss to approximately 0.5 percentage points. The additional experiments in which hazy optical points are transplanted into otherwise clean spectra, and clean optical points are restored to hazy spectra, strengthen this attribution. This is more informative than merely demonstrating reduced accuracy under distribution shift: it identifies a specific architectural vulnerability in the published classifier design."*

**Response.** Kept (Section 3.3, around Table 6) as the revision's starting point: the optical-excess score is built on it (Section 2.7), the AIRS-only variant is tested as a remedy on real spectra (Sections 3.4 and 3.5), the identifiability measurement classes the error (Section 3.3), and the failure was re-tested under three confined hazes because the simulated haze depends on the model's top boundary (Section 2.3). The matched ensemble, trained on hazes and decks, keeps all inputs and holds 80.5 % under this haze (A4; Table 16). On real spectra, removing the optical points alone recovers methane on none of the eight reductions, and the inputs below 1.95 µm gate the verdicts in general (Section 3.4, "The methane planets").

**Where.** Section 3.3; Section 3.4; Section 3.7; Tables 6 and 16.

### B5

*"Nevertheless, even this finding requires a stronger interpretation to qualify as a novel scientific contribution. The observation that a classifier trained without chromatic atmospheric haze may assign inappropriate significance to haze-sensitive optical measurements is physically intelligible from the construction of the training data. The author has demonstrated the magnitude of the vulnerability for a particular implementation, but has not established a new physical mechanism, a general mathematical principle governing the failure, or a previously unrecognized limitation of atmospheric spectroscopy. Moreover, the proposed remedies—incorporating haze into training or excluding or down-weighting affected inputs—are familiar approaches to domain randomization and feature selection. The analysis therefore identifies a useful implementation-specific weakness without clearly advancing the underlying science of classification or atmospheric inference."*

**Response.** The revision claims no new physical mechanism or mathematical principle; it interprets the finding in three new ways. It is classed: haze leaves the labels decidable when its strength is known, so its errors can be repaired, whereas those noise introduces mostly cannot (Table 7). It is tested on real planets: at WASP-39 b's Tier-1 noise H₂O is reported in 58 % of draws with the optical points and 86 % without, and the five methane sub-Neptunes are found on none of eight reductions with all inputs and on seven with the AIRS channels alone, which the optical points alone do not explain (Table 8). And it propagates to the existing method, since the missing haze causes the retrieval's failure too (Table 11). The remedies are familiar, as the paper says (Section 4). What it adds is their measured value (A4): routed by flags they gained nothing on new planets, and a retrieval fallback loses more targets than none unless it models the departure.

**Where.** Sections 3.3 to 3.7; Section 4; Tables 7 to 16 and E1 to E5.

### B6

*"A more consequential issue concerns the distinction between robustness within a simulation framework and predictive validity for actual Ariel observations. As the author acknowledges in Section 3.7, all evaluated spectra are simulated, including those assigned to known Ariel targets. The study therefore compares classifiers trained under one set of modeling assumptions with spectra generated under alternative assumptions, rather than independently testing their predictions against observations of real planetary atmospheres. This distinction is fundamental. A classifier that performs poorly under a particular simulated haze prescription is not necessarily equally unreliable under the actual distribution of atmospheric hazes encountered by Ariel. Conversely, a classifier that performs well under the tested perturbations cannot be presumed reliable against physical processes absent from all the simulations. The study quantifies sensitivity to selected modeling choices but does not independently establish the relationship between simulated robustness and real-world classification reliability."*

**Response.** The external test answers this (A2). On WASP-39 b, inside the training distribution by every distance rule, the classifier is mostly right. On the methane sub-Neptunes it finds none of eight reductions, whereas their clear twins through the same coverage are found in 63 to 100 % of draws on six of the seven with a twin, so those misses are not the noise. Ten of the twelve real giants are muted beyond the simulator population's reach at their bulk parameters, measuring in one quantity how the real distribution departs from the training prior (Section 3.4, "Muting"; Figure 4; Table D1). Across seven designs, simulated clean accuracy ranks them opposite to the real panel at JWST precision (Spearman −0.50) but with the 12 new planets (+0.34), so these descriptive values do not show that the clean benchmark picks the wrong design (Appendix E). The matched ensemble's held-out grids are models, and on real labels, all seen beforehand, its lead is separated at Tier-1 noise but not over all labels at JWST precision (Tables 13 and 15), so its real reliability awaits the prospective test of Section 4. Section 4 ("Scope") calls the departures a selected collection, not a representation of Ariel's targets.

**Where.** Sections 2.6 and 2.10; Sections 3.4, 3.5 and 3.7; Section 4; Tables 8, 9, 10, 13, 15, D1 and E3; Figures 4 and 6; Appendix E.

### B7

*"The manuscript occasionally moves beyond this evidentiary boundary by framing the results in terms of whether classifiers can be trusted for Ariel target selection. Trustworthiness is a substantially broader claim than robustness to a finite set of perturbations. It requires some independent basis for believing that the tested departures, their parameter ranges, their prevalence, and their combinations adequately represent the physical conditions encountered in the intended application. Although the author anchors certain haze strengths to published atmospheric retrievals, this does not establish the joint distribution of atmospheric conditions across Ariel's target population. Without such information, the observed accuracy losses cannot be translated directly into expected mission-wide classification errors or incorrect target-selection decisions. The manuscript therefore demonstrates conditional failure under specified simulated conditions, not the operational reliability or unreliability of the proposed classifiers."*

**Response.** Accepted. "Trust" is removed from the title and text; the supplementary directory keeps that name only because it holds the note's results. Every prevalence is reported under a named population; Section 2.4 states that the true joint distribution of conditions among Ariel's targets is unknown, and the muting census (Section 3.4) measures how real spectra sit against the prior. Mixed-population results are given at three stated hazed fractions, 10, 20 and 50 % (Table 12), with no mission-wide error rate drawn from them, and Section 4 states that this population is hazed at one density and particle size and that the haze strengths rest on an order-of-magnitude conversion holding at one pressure level. The model grids test the design and are no census of Ariel's targets.

**Where.** Title; Abstract; Section 2.4; Section 2.8; Sections 3.4, 3.6 and 3.7; Table 12; Section 4.

### B8

*"The second classifier, designed to identify carbon-rich atmospheres, is introduced to demonstrate that robustness profiles are classifier-specific. The author reports that adding HCN and C₂H₂ substantially degrades carbon-rich classification while producing little corresponding degradation in the molecular-presence classifiers. This comparison is potentially interesting, but the two systems differ simultaneously in their prediction targets, training distributions, atmospheric chemistry, machine-learning architectures, and noise assumptions. The author appropriately acknowledges that these differences prevent causal attribution of the contrasting behavior. Consequently, the comparison establishes only that two different classifiers respond differently to different perturbations. That observation is unsurprising and does not support a general conclusion about which classifier properties determine vulnerability to particular forms of model misspecification."*

**Response.** Accepted. The carbon-rich classifier is reduced to Appendix B, one paragraph, and is used for one conclusion: one classifier's departure profile did not predict the other's. No claim about which classifier properties determine vulnerability is made.

**Where.** Appendix B; Section 4, "Scope".

### B9

*"The manuscript also devotes considerable attention to the failure of confidence-based rejection, distance-based novelty detection, and conformal prediction under distribution shift. These results are quantitatively documented, but their conceptual interpretation is already well established. **Response.** Accepted. These results are compressed to a few sentences in Section 3.6 ("The existing rules under the shift") and Table D3, presented as the known loss of calibration under dataset shift (Ovadia et al. 2019) seen in a physical setting, and scored by the selective-risk evaluation of Jaeger et al. (2023). They enter the rest of the paper only as the existing rules the diagnostics and designs are compared with. The manuscript states that the rules' clean decline rates are fixed by construction (Section 2.3) and reports a held-out check (Section 4).

**Where.** Section 2.3, last paragraph; Section 3.6, "The existing rules under the shift"; Table D3; Section 4.

### B10

*"Applying these existing methods to simulated Ariel spectra may provide useful benchmarking information, but the manuscript does not develop a new uncertainty measure, establish a new failure theorem, or identify a broadly transferable relationship between physical misspecification and confidence calibration."*

**Response.** The revision claims no new uncertainty measure or theorem. It adds a result in the mission's terms, with its reason: the confidence rules allocate a retrieval budget no better than a random one and, under haze, find almost none of the hazed targets (3 % at a 20 % budget), because they send the budget to undecidable labels, where a retrieval cannot help. Only a shift flag finds the planets the classifier is confidently wrong about, and it helps only when routed to a method that does not share the failure (Section 3.6; Section 4, "Why confidence rules fail and when a shift flag helps"). The matched ensemble's probability is measured as an indicator of its errors (AUROC 0.64 to 0.72, Table C1, row V1; for H₂O under extra noise and muting 0.49 to 0.56).

### B11

*"An additional concern is the reliance on aggregate classification accuracy as the principal measure of scientific utility. Ariel's Tier-1 reconnaissance observations are intended to inform decisions about which planets warrant deeper characterization. The consequences of incorrectly excluding a scientifically valuable planet may be substantially different from those of unnecessarily selecting a planet for additional observation. Yet the manuscript does not formulate a mission-level decision problem that incorporates these asymmetric consequences, observational costs, target priorities, or the number of follow-up opportunities available. Indeed, the reported haze experiments show that overall classification accuracy can obscure a near-total collapse in sensitivity to particular molecules. The more scientifically relevant quantity may therefore be the number and characteristics of valuable targets lost under a constrained selection strategy, rather than the average percentage of correctly classified simulated spectra."*

**Response.** Adopted as the primary metric of every selection result (Tables 12, 16 and E5). The simulation (Section 2.8) gives 60 % of the 955 POP-I planets Tier-2 slots and counts the planets carrying CH₄ or H₂O that are not selected, for every design, with the slot count varied from 20 to 80 % (Appendix E). The collapse the reviewer notes is reported per molecule, as recall on present labels and hazed targets. The model grids and real labels have no population to select from and are scored in labels (Tables 10, 13, 15 and E3). At a fixed slot count, slots wasted equal targets lost plus a constant, so the ranking of strategies does not depend on the weight given to the two errors (Table C1, row C6); this does not model the asymmetry the reviewer describes. Section 4 states that ranking by probability is a proxy for the mission's selection, and that observing costs, target priorities and follow-up opportunities are not modelled.

**Where.** Section 2.8; Sections 3.5, 3.6 and 3.7; Tables 12, 16, E5 and C2; Section 4; Section 5.

### B13

### B14

*"I therefore do not recommend publication in its present form. The manuscript would be more appropriately characterized as a reproducibility and robustness benchmark than as a study establishing a new scientific principle. **Response.** The revision was built to this sentence.
