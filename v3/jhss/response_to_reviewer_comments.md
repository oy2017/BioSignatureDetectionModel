### A2. External validation on WASP-39 b

*"He recommends that if you externally validate your results, the manuscript would then generate enough knowledge to become publishable as an original paper. He is of the opinion that you pursue this avenue, since you have already put in considerable work. He suggests using WASP-39 b, which is an exoplanet included in Ariel's planned target program, and it has already been observed by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). However, Ariel and JWST are separate space telescopes. WASP-39 b is not part of the Ariel instrument or system; it is a planetary target that Ariel can observe. This makes WASP-39 b particularly relevant to the manuscript because the same planetary atmosphere can be studied using existing JWST observations and simulated Ariel measurements. The author could compare the classifier's predictions against JWST-derived atmospheric properties, **provided the observed spectra are made compatible with the classifier's input requirements** with adequate justification of the compatibility procedure. That would provide an independent observational test of the simulator-trained classifier, rather than another sensitivity test using entirely simulated spectra."*

**Response.** Done. The JWST benchmark spectrum of WASP-39 b (the four Early Release Science modes re-reduced uniformly by Carter & May et al. 2024, extended to 7.8 µm with the MIRI LRS spectrum of Powell et al. 2024) was converted to the classifier's 104-bin Tier-3 input, and the conversion is justified three ways.

The procedure (Section 2.6): JWST products are transit depths, the quantity the classifier was trained on, so no conversion of quantity is needed. Each Tier-3 bin takes the inverse-variance-weighted mean of the JWST points inside it, partial overlaps weighted by the overlap fraction. Modes are aligned through their overlaps onto NIRISS order 1, which for WASP-39 b recovers the PRISM offset of −177 ppm that Carter & May report from their joint fit. Bins with no JWST data are filled flat at the spectrum's mean and flagged, and the number of filled bins is reported with every verdict (one of 104 for WASP-39 b). Per-spectrum normalisation then removes any global offset, as in training.

The justification (Section 2.6) has three parts. (a) A round trip on a spectrum whose answer is known: the benchmark's own best-fit model binned directly onto the 104 bins, against the same model sampled at each JWST mode's wavelength grid with the per-point errors and pushed through the conversion. The two differ by 22 ppm rms and 104 ppm at worst, with no verdict changed. (b) A sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations (the individual reductions of each mode, two NIRISS pipelines, fitted and fixed limb darkening, offsets on and off, model and flat fill, with and without MIRI, PRISM alone, and the Na, K, SO₂ and CO bins masked singly and together), following Roy-Perez et al. (2026), who showed that WASP-39 b's retrieved abundances move by more than an order of magnitude with the reduction. (c) The noise regimes: JWST precision as delivered and Ariel Tier-1 precision (the payload model's per-bin noise at the planet's Tier-1 transit count, added as 300 Gaussian draws for WASP-39 b and 500 for the panel), with the Tier-3 count run for WASP-39 b only.

The outcome (Section 3.4, "WASP-39 b"): at JWST precision three of four learners call H₂O present and all four call CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at the 10⁻⁴ threshold, which agrees with the published composition on both labels the literature decides (H₂O present and CH₄ absent; CO₂ and NH₃ are unscored). The MLP calls H₂O absent with a score of 0.00 in 23 of 24 configurations and on the benchmark's own model binned directly with no conversion, so the miss is not the conversion's, and the clear twin is found by all four learners, so it is not the composition either. Under Ariel Tier-1 noise at WASP-39 b's one transit, H₂O is detected in 58 % of draws with all inputs and 86 % without the three optical points.

Because one planet is a weak external test, twenty-two further planets with public JWST transmission spectra, in 25 reductions, were converted by the same procedure, in three batches, with labels from the published retrievals and ambiguous labels left unscored. The manuscript states when each batch's labels were committed relative to its verdicts (Section 2.6), and every label's published value, error and source is tabulated in the repository (`results/jwst_panel/label_sources.csv`). That table came from an audit of all 104 cells after the verdicts: 56 of the 66 scored labels follow the rule as written, three depart from it, seven are judgement calls, and two unscored cells should have been scored. The frozen labels were kept and the scores are reported under the corrected sets as well (Sections 2.6 and 3.5).

Over the 66 labels of the panel, with a split counted as half, the all-input classifier scores 45.5 at JWST precision, right on 39 of 40 absent labels and 3 of 26 present ones, and the AIRS-only variant 52.5; at Tier-1 noise they score 41.7 and 43.4, against 40 for calling every label absent. The conversion's validation holds at near-complete coverage, and at the panel's partial coverage the control is each planet's clear twin through the same mask. That control shows that the mask itself misleads the all-input design on eleven present labels (Section 3.4, "What the coverage mask does"), and every design is scored separately on the 50 labels that pass it (Table 12). On the fifteen reductions with measured data both below 1.1 µm and above 1.95 µm the three input variants score 28.5, 32.0 and 34.5 of 40. Most of the gain from dropping the optical points lies in the first batch, and the manuscript gives each batch's score. The eight methane reductions are five planets, and the manuscript reports the result per planet as well as per reduction (Table 8). The expectations for the WASP-39 b test and the panel, and their outcomes, are rows A1 to P6 of Table C1. Twelve further planets, found by a systematic search and labelled blind by two readers, were scored once as an independent test (Sections 2.6 and 3.5; Tables 3 and 15). The classifier called all 24 of their labels absent.

**Where.** Section 2.6; Table 2; Section 3.4; Tables 8 and 12; Table C1 (rows A1 to P6); Figure 4; `results/jwst_panel/label_sources.csv`.

### A3. Identifiability

*"Then, two main advances beyond the WASP-39 b validation would be required: First, establish identifiability. Determine whether physically distinct atmospheric compositions can produce indistinguishable Ariel spectra, and quantify how frequently such ambiguity occurs under explicitly defined atmospheric populations. This would identify a fundamental limit on molecular classification rather than merely another sensitivity to simulation parameters."*

**Response.** Done, on the whole test population, with two instruments (Section 2.4). The first is a Δχ² test: for each planet and each molecule, the best-fitting atmosphere of the opposite label, with the molecule held at the 10⁻⁴ boundary and the population's nuisance parameters free, scored against the true spectrum at the planet's Tier-1 per-bin noise (eq1). The second is a nested-sampling retrieval of every one of the 955 test planets with the same free quantities, which gives for every label the posterior probability of the true class, the P-statistic of Bocchieri et al. (2023) evaluated for the true class. The two agree in rank (Spearman 0.80 to 0.89), and the retrieval calibrates the test's scale, which differs by an order of magnitude from the conventional one (the posterior crosses 0.95 at Δχ² of 0.45 to 0.98, not at 3.84).

The population is explicitly defined: the planets and abundances of POP-I at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, retrieved under POP-III's prior, with the payload noise model of Section 2.2. Under it (Section 3.2; Table 4), 32 % of CH₄ labels, 54 % of H₂O, 44 % of CO₂ and 32 % of NH₃ are not decided at the 0.95 level, 35 to 62 % when decidability is defined by a Bayes factor against the prior's odds. A fifth of the labels lie within half a decade of the 10⁻⁴ threshold and nine in ten of those are undecidable, so the fractions away from the threshold are 18, 44, 33 and 19 %; that row is in Table 4. Only 17 % of planets have all four labels decidable and 3 % have none. Retrieved under the population's own abundance prior, the undecidable fraction rises by five points on a 200-planet subsample. Three properties of the retrieval are stated with the result (Section 2.4): the posteriors are of the noise-free spectrum; the abundance prior gives absent labels a head start of 0.71; and the forward model is the training model with the radius fixed, so "undecidable" means not decided at the 0.95 level under this prior and this model. On a random 200 planets retrieved on the classifier's own noisy realisation (Section 3.2, "Noisy realisations"), the undecidable fractions move by at most 6 points and 5 to 12 % of labels change class each way. The retrieval's own accuracy, 92 to 94 %, is the ceiling the spectra allow under this prior, and the classifier sits 9 to 16 points below it. This is the limit the editor asked for, stated as a prevalence under one named population and not as a physical limit (Section 4, "Scope"); Section 3.4 measures how far the real planets sit from that population.

**Where.** Section 2.4; Section 3.2; Table 4; Figure 3a; Table C1 (rows B1 to B7); Section 4.

### A4. A remedy with measurable value

*"Second, establish a remedy with measurable value. Develop and evaluate a diagnostic that recognizes when the classifier is likely to be unreliable, and demonstrate that its use improves molecular classification or target selection compared with existing methods."*

**Response.** Addressed in two forms that answer separate questions, with the limits stated. The first is a routing method that recognises the two failures the real spectra show and sends each to its own remedy, scored in simulation, on the JWST panel at JWST precision and at Tier-1 noise, and in the selection (Section 3.5; Tables 12, 13 and 14). The second is a retrieval budget routed by a flag, with the value measured in Tier-2 targets lost at a fixed 60 % selection against the classifier alone, the five decline rules and a retrieval of every planet (Section 3.6; Table 17).

The routing method (Sections 2.7 and 3.5) needs the Mission Candidate Sample's bulk parameters and the spectrum, with no retrieval and no published abundance, and it assumes an H₂/He scale height and a correct catalogue mass. Its amplitude flag is the catalogue's five-scale-height modulation over the spectrum's noise-corrected dispersion, with thresholds read from the simulated population through each reduction's own coverage. Its shape flag fires when the optical-excess score or the input disagreement exceeds its clean threshold. A planet above the amplitude threshold goes to a design retrained with aerosols, a planet below the lower cut has its verdict withheld, and on every other planet a cell the shape flag marks goes to the AIRS-only variant.

With a split or withheld verdict counted as half, it scores 54.0 of 66 labels at JWST precision against 45.5 for the re-implemented classifier, a difference of 8.5 with a descriptive 95 % bootstrap interval over planets of 2.8 to 14.0. Six labels go from wrong to right and none from right to wrong, and methane is found on five of the eight reductions against none. At Tier-1 noise the gain is 2.7 (1.2 to 4.3), and methane is found on about one of the eight. Its cost in simulation is 4.9 points of clean accuracy, and in the selection it loses as many CH₄ targets as the re-implemented classifier on clean spectra (20), 11 fewer under haze, and 16 more H₂O targets on clean spectra.

The manuscript states the limits with equal weight (Section 4, "What the method claims"). On the development panel it beats each of the five existing rules used as routers (48.5 to 49.5), but it cannot be separated from two simpler fixes, the AIRS-only variant used everywhere (52.5) and a retrained design used everywhere (52.0). In simulation the retrained designs do better, in accuracy and in the selection. About two fifths of the gain at JWST precision lies on labels the coverage mask hides. The flag and its thresholds were built after the panel was known (Table C3). The paper therefore proposes a new method with those limitations. It was then frozen and applied to 12 new planets, where it gained nothing (16.0 of 24 for both, Table 15), so the improvement on the development panel did not carry over, although the methane recovery repeated. It does not address the undecidable class: the method neither recognises nor withholds such labels (Section 3.5, first paragraph).

The retrieval-budget answer has three parts. First (Section 3.6, "A retrieval budget on clean spectra"; Table C2), on clean, noise-free simulated spectra the retrieval of every planet is the Bayes-optimal selector, as a correctly specified retrieval must be: ranking by its present-posterior loses 0 of 359 CH₄-bearing targets, against 20 for the classifier, at 365 core-hours. No cheap rule routes a smaller budget much better than a random one, because the confidence rules send it to the undecidable labels, where the retrieval cannot help either. On the 200-planet subsample the retrieval of the classifier's own noisy realisation loses 1 of 74 CH₄ targets, the noise-free one 0 and the classifier 1, and 2, 1 and 12 of 80 H₂O targets.

Second (Section 3.6, "The clear-model retrieval under haze"; Table 16; Figure 3c), the clear-model retrieval shares the classifier's failure: run with the training forward model on the hazed spectra of all 954 planets, it calls almost every present label absent and is confidently wrong on 99 % of present labels. With the haze density as one free parameter it recovers 222 of the 224 methane labels the clean spectra decide, a best case that the manuscript labels as such because the truth lies inside that family; a power-law aerosol, which does not contain the truth, recovers most of the loss on the 117 planets run, and its pinned slope and lower evidence show the wrong family. The clear-model retrieval's evidence does give the failure away, and the manuscript reports that as a gate.

Third (Section 3.6, "A budget routed by a shift flag"; Table 17; Figure 5), the diagnostic: the optical-excess score, chosen before the runs because Section 3.3 had located the haze failure in those inputs. On a mixed population in which a fifth of the planets are hazed, routing the 20 % of planets it ranks most suspect to the free-haze retrieval loses 13 ± 2 of 359 CH₄ targets. The classifier alone loses 29 ± 4, a random budget 20 ± 4, the margin and ensemble rules 20 to 22, the clear-model retrieval of every planet read by its posterior 69, and a haze oracle that knows which planets are hazed 12 ± 2. The Mahalanobis rule, used as a router, loses the same number within noise (13 ± 2). The score's advantage over the distance rules is therefore its recall and selectivity on the hazed planets, not the selection, and the improvement comes from routing to a retrieval that models the departure. The same budget routed to the clear-model retrieval loses 71 ± 8 targets, against 29 with no retrieval. Table 17 gives the same results with 10 and 50 % of the planets hazed. The expectations for this test were committed after a pilot on the 124 planets whose fixed-density hazed posteriors existed first, and before any population result existed (Section 2.8; Table C1, rows M1 to M5).

What the diagnostic does not do is also stated. It does not beat a clear-model retrieval of every planet re-run with the haze density free wherever its evidence fails, which loses no methane target at about 520 core-hours against 127 for the budget. It does not help the irrecoverable class: the classifier's own confidence recognises that class only in part (AUROC 0.71 to 0.77 for three molecules, 0.6 for water), a flag trained on the retrieval does better only for water, and even perfect recognition would recover four of 359 methane targets at a 20 % budget. At the weaker density of 2 × 10⁶ m⁻³ the flag's sensitivity falls from 98 to 60 % and the routing without a retrieval recovers little of the loss, which moves to H₂O (Section 3.6, "The weaker haze").

**Where.** Sections 2.7 and 2.8; Sections 3.5 and 3.6; Tables 11 to 17; Figure 5; Tables C1 (rows C1 to M5, F1 to F3, G1 to G5), C2, C3, D1 and D2.

### A5. The two classes of error

*"The most interesting possible outcome is that you discover two distinct classes of error: 1. Recoverable computational errors: The spectrum contains sufficient molecular information, but the classifier interprets it incorrectly because of its training assumptions. 2. Irrecoverable information limitations: Different atmospheric compositions produce observations that cannot be distinguished at the instrument's precision, making a definitive molecular classification unjustified. The manuscript already provides preliminary evidence for the first category through its haze-retraining experiments. You has not systematically investigated the second. Distinguishing these two categories, quantifying their prevalence, and developing a method to recognize them would be a much stronger scientific contribution than the present sensitivity analysis. It would also produce a result with implications beyond Ariel, potentially extending to other simulator-trained scientific inference systems."*

**Response.** This became the paper's spine. The definition (Section 2.5): an error is recoverable if the posterior probability of the true class on the same spectrum exceeds 0.95, undecidable if it lies between 0.05 and 0.95, and misled if below 0.05; under a departure the departure-aware Δχ² at the calibrated threshold is used. Because the posterior is of the noise-free spectrum, the recoverable shares are upper bounds, and the gap is measured on the 200-planet subsample (Section 3.2, "Noisy realisations": 18.5 % of the learners' errors recoverable by the noise-free posterior, 14.9 % decidable by the realisation's). The split of a departure's errors into those shared with the clean run and those introduced was made after the runs and is labelled as such.

Prevalence (Section 3.3; Table 7; Figure 3): about four fifths of the classifier's errors on clean spectra fall on labels not settled at the 0.95 level. That split depends on the level: the posterior leans to the right answer on 74 % of those errors at 0.5 (Section 3.2). Haze and unocculted spots leave the labels decidable when their strength is known, and 57 to 87 % of the errors they introduce are recoverable. A high cloud deck removes information on absent labels (51 to 82 % of its introduced errors recoverable). Threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction and leaves 10 to 56 % recoverable. The clearest demonstration is a pair of retrievals on the same 954 hazed spectra (Section 3.6; Figure 3c): with the training forward model the retrieval is confidently wrong on 99 % of present labels; with the haze density added as one free parameter it recovers the density on every planet and 222 of the 224 CH₄ labels the clean spectra decide, while keeping 75 % of them decidable, which is the price of fitting the strength rather than knowing it.

Recognition (Section 3.6, "Recognising the undecidable class" and "The existing rules under the shift"): the shift detectors find hazed planets, not recoverable errors, and no flag separates the two classes better than the ensemble spread (AUROC 0.62 on clean spectra, 0.72 under haze). The irrecoverable class is marked in part by the classifier's own confidence, and a flag trained on the retrieval does better only for water. Recognising it is worth a withheld verdict rather than a retrieval, and the routing method does not attempt it (Section 3.5).

On real data (Section 3.4): the methane misses on the five sub-Neptunes are recoverable in the operational sense that another input reading the same spectrum finds the label; the transplants show that the inputs below 1.95 µm gate the verdicts in general, so they do not locate a methane signal. Under the posterior definition with the training prior at Tier-1 precision, however, none of the classifier's 17 wrong labels is recoverable: 7 are undecidable and 10 misled, because the prior reads muted real spectra as low abundance (Table 9). Ten of the twelve real giants are muted 2.3 to 5.3 times against their clear twins, in every bootstrap resample beyond what the population can produce at their bulk parameters and published compositions (Figure 4), though not at lower abundances, so amplitude is degenerate with abundance within the prior. The scaled-twin test shows that uniform muting costs labels at Tier-1 noise and not at JWST precision, and Section 3.5 carries a muted population into selection. The implication beyond Ariel has its own paragraph (Section 4, "Beyond Ariel") and closes the Conclusion.

**Where.** Title; Abstract; Section 1; Section 2.5; Sections 3.3, 3.4, 3.5 and 3.6; Section 4; Table 7; Figures 3 and 4; Table C1 (row B4).

### B2

*"Nevertheless, the central question for publication is not whether the work was carefully executed, but whether it establishes a sufficiently novel scientific proposition."*

### B3

*"The manuscript's principal conclusion is that classifiers trained on simulated spectra may perform poorly when confronted with spectra generated under different physical assumptions, that these failures may occur without corresponding reductions in predictive confidence, and that individual classifiers require independent robustness testing. These observations are consistent with the established literature on distribution shift, model misspecification, uncertainty calibration, and simulation-to-reality transfer. Indeed, the manuscript explicitly acknowledges that robustness testing is established practice and cites previous investigations of model misspecification in simulation-based inference and exoplanet atmospheric retrieval. Consequently, the general proposition that simulator-trained classifiers cannot be assumed reliable outside their training distributions is neither new nor particularly surprising. Demonstrating this principle in another application does not, by itself, constitute a substantive scientific advance."*

**Response.** Agreed, and the revised paper no longer rests on that proposition. Its principal conclusions are population-level and decision-level measurements that were not available before. (1) Under the training prior, a third to a half of the four molecular labels are not decided at Tier-1 precision, and about four fifths of the classifier's errors fall on those labels, although the posterior leans to the right answer on most (Section 3.2, Table 4; Section 3.3). (2) The departures separate into the two classes, haze and spots leaving the labels decidable when their strength is known and noise removing them (Section 3.3, Table 7). (3) A retrieval of every planet, the method the classifier was proposed to replace, is the Bayes-optimal selector on clean noise-free spectra at 365 core-hours, yet fails more confidently than the classifier under the departure that reproduces their failure mechanism (Section 3.6, Table 16); (4) on real JWST spectra the classifier is right on almost every absent label and misses most present ones, and a retrieval under the same training prior at Tier-1 precision reads them as absent as well, which locates much of the miss in the prior (Section 3.4); (5) a flag routed to a departure-aware retrieval allocates a partial retrieval budget within noise of an oracle, while the same flag routed to the clear-model retrieval loses more targets than no retrieval (Section 3.6, Table 17). Two results were not among the expectations committed before the runs and are reported as such in the Discussion: the scale of the cheap identifiability test, and the real giants' position outside the simulator population in feature amplitude at their published compositions, while inside it by every bulk parameter and, for seven of the ten, by all three distance rules.

**Where.** Abstract; Section 1, last two paragraphs; Sections 3.2 to 3.6; Section 4.

### B4

*"However, the manuscript contains one result that deserves closer consideration than the broader robustness analysis. In Section 3.3, the author finds that relatively weak atmospheric haze causes the classifiers to miss methane and water even when the corresponding infrared molecular absorption features remain substantially intact. The author attributes this failure to three optical photometric inputs, demonstrating that removing these inputs reduces the haze-associated accuracy loss from 17.7 to 5.6 percentage points, while restricting classification to the AIRS channels reduces the loss to approximately 0.5 percentage points. The additional experiments in which hazy optical points are transplanted into otherwise clean spectra, and clean optical points are restored to hazy spectra, strengthen this attribution. This is more informative than merely demonstrating reduced accuracy under distribution shift: it identifies a specific architectural vulnerability in the published classifier design."*

**Response.** This result is kept (Section 3.3, the paragraphs around Table 6) and is the starting point of the revision: the optical-excess score of Section 2.7 is built on it, the AIRS-only variant is a remedy tested on real spectra in Sections 3.4 and 3.5, the retrieval of Section 3.2 is what turns the attribution into a classification of the error, and the failure was re-tested under three confined hazes because the simulated haze proved to depend on the model's top boundary (Sections 2.3 and 3.3). Its limit on real spectra is also stated: removing the optical points alone recovers methane on none of the eight reductions, and the transplants show that the inputs below 1.95 µm gate the verdicts in general (Section 3.4, "The methane planets").

**Where.** Section 3.3; Section 3.4; Table 6.

### B5

*"Nevertheless, even this finding requires a stronger interpretation to qualify as a novel scientific contribution. The observation that a classifier trained without chromatic atmospheric haze may assign inappropriate significance to haze-sensitive optical measurements is physically intelligible from the construction of the training data. The author has demonstrated the magnitude of the vulnerability for a particular implementation, but has not established a new physical mechanism, a general mathematical principle governing the failure, or a previously unrecognized limitation of atmospheric spectroscopy. Moreover, the proposed remedies—incorporating haze into training or excluding or down-weighting affected inputs—are familiar approaches to domain randomization and feature selection. The analysis therefore identifies a useful implementation-specific weakness without clearly advancing the underlying science of classification or atmospheric inference."*

**Response.** The revision does not claim a new physical mechanism or a mathematical principle. The finding is interpreted in three ways it was not before. It is classed: the retrieval shows that haze leaves the labels decidable when its strength is known, so the haze errors can be repaired, whereas the errors noise introduces mostly cannot (Section 3.3, Table 7). It is tested on real planets: at WASP-39 b's Tier-1 noise H₂O is detected in 58 % of draws with the optical points and 86 % without them, and the five methane-bearing sub-Neptunes are missed with all inputs on seven of eight reductions and found with the AIRS channels alone on six of eight, and the optical points alone do not explain this (Section 3.4, Table 8). And it is shown to propagate to the existing method: the missing haze causes both failures, the classifier's being repaired by changing its input and the retrieval's by changing its model (Section 3.6, Table 16). The remedies are familiar, as the reviewer says, and the revision says so (Section 4, "What the method claims"). What it adds is their combination, routed by two flags, and their value measured on real spectra and in targets at a fixed budget (Sections 3.5 and 3.6), including the result that the most familiar remedy, a retrieval fallback, loses more targets than no retrieval unless the retrieval models the departure.

**Where.** Sections 3.3 to 3.6; Section 4; Tables 7 to 17.

### B6

*"A more consequential issue concerns the distinction between robustness within a simulation framework and predictive validity for actual Ariel observations. As the author acknowledges in Section 3.7, all evaluated spectra are simulated, including those assigned to known Ariel targets. The study therefore compares classifiers trained under one set of modeling assumptions with spectra generated under alternative assumptions, rather than independently testing their predictions against observations of real planetary atmospheres. This distinction is fundamental. A classifier that performs poorly under a particular simulated haze prescription is not necessarily equally unreliable under the actual distribution of atmospheric hazes encountered by Ariel. Conversely, a classifier that performs well under the tested perturbations cannot be presumed reliable against physical processes absent from all the simulations. The study quantifies sensitivity to selected modeling choices but does not independently establish the relationship between simulated robustness and real-world classification reliability."*

**Response.** This is the point the external test answers (Part A2 above), and the answer is specific rather than general. On WASP-39 b, which lies inside the training distribution by every input-space rule, the classifier is mostly right, and its one wrong learner is not wrong because of the conversion, the composition or a grey deck. On the real planets that carry the molecule, the methane sub-Neptunes, it misses seven of eight reductions. The clear twins through the same coverage and noise are found by a majority of learners in 63 to 100 % of draws on six of the seven reductions with a twin, so those misses are not the noise; on the seventh reduction the twin is never found, so that miss is the coverage. The coverage mask alone misleads the all-input design on eleven present labels of the panel. Ten of the twelve real giants with published abundances are muted beyond the simulator population's reach at their bulk parameters, which measures directly, in one quantity, how the real distribution differs from the training prior (Section 3.4, "Muting"; Figure 4; Table D1), with bootstrap intervals on every muting value. Designs are scored at Tier-1 noise on the same real spectra, and the relation the reviewer asks about is stated: across seven designs, simulated accuracy on clean spectra ranks them opposite to the real panel (Spearman −0.71), under haze with it (+0.58), and the other cases say little (Section 3.5). A retrieval under the training prior reads the real spectra as absent (Table 9), and new planets were scored once with every rule frozen (Table 15). The paper does not claim that the simulated departures represent Ariel's target distribution; Section 4 ("Scope") says they do not, and Section 3.4 measures the distance where JWST allows it.

**Where.** Section 2.6; Section 3.4; Section 4; Tables 8, 12 and D1; Figure 4.

### B7

*"The manuscript occasionally moves beyond this evidentiary boundary by framing the results in terms of whether classifiers can be trusted for Ariel target selection. Trustworthiness is a substantially broader claim than robustness to a finite set of perturbations. It requires some independent basis for believing that the tested departures, their parameter ranges, their prevalence, and their combinations adequately represent the physical conditions encountered in the intended application. Although the author anchors certain haze strengths to published atmospheric retrievals, this does not establish the joint distribution of atmospheric conditions across Ariel's target population. Without such information, the observed accuracy losses cannot be translated directly into expected mission-wide classification errors or incorrect target-selection decisions. The manuscript therefore demonstrates conditional failure under specified simulated conditions, not the operational reliability or unreliability of the proposed classifiers."*

**Response.** Accepted. "Trust" is removed from the title and the text, and the supplementary directory keeps that name only because it holds the earlier note's results. Every prevalence is reported under a named population (Section 2.4, last paragraph). The mixed-population results are given at three stated hazed fractions, 10, 20 and 50 % (Table 17), and the paper draws no mission-wide error rate from them. Section 2.4 states that the true joint distribution of atmospheric conditions among Ariel's targets is unknown, and the muting census of Section 3.4 measures how the real spectra sit against the prior rather than replacing it. Section 4 states that the mixed population is hazed at one density and particle size, and that the haze strengths were anchored through an order-of-magnitude conversion that holds at one pressure level.

**Where.** Title; Abstract; Section 2.4; Section 2.8; Section 3.4; Section 3.6; Table 17; Section 4.

### B8

*"The second classifier, designed to identify carbon-rich atmospheres, is introduced to demonstrate that robustness profiles are classifier-specific. The author reports that adding HCN and C₂H₂ substantially degrades carbon-rich classification while producing little corresponding degradation in the molecular-presence classifiers. This comparison is potentially interesting, but the two systems differ simultaneously in their prediction targets, training distributions, atmospheric chemistry, machine-learning architectures, and noise assumptions. The author appropriately acknowledges that these differences prevent causal attribution of the contrasting behavior. Consequently, the comparison establishes only that two different classifiers respond differently to different perturbations. That observation is unsurprising and does not support a general conclusion about which classifier properties determine vulnerability to particular forms of model misspecification."*

**Response.** Accepted. The carbon-rich classifier is reduced to Appendix B, one paragraph, and is used for one conclusion: one classifier's departure profile did not predict the other's. No claim about which classifier properties determine vulnerability is made.

**Where.** Appendix B; Section 4, "Beyond Ariel" and "Scope".

### B9

*"The manuscript also devotes considerable attention to the failure of confidence-based rejection, distance-based novelty detection, and conformal prediction under distribution shift. These results are quantitatively documented, but their conceptual interpretation is already well established. Predictive confidence can remain high for out-of-distribution observations, distance-based novelty scores need not identify classification errors, and conformal coverage guarantees generally depend on assumptions about the relationship between calibration and test distributions."*

**Response.** Accepted. The decline-rule results are compressed to a few sentences in Section 3.6 ("The existing rules under the shift") and Table D3, presented as the pattern reported for image classifiers under dataset shift (Ovadia et al. 2019) in a physical setting, scored in the manner of the selective-risk evaluation argued for by Jaeger et al. (2023), and they enter the rest of the paper only as the existing rules the diagnostic is compared with (Sections 2.3, 2.8 and 3.6). The manuscript also states that the rules' thresholds are quantiles of the clean test scores, so their clean decline rates are fixed by construction (Section 2.3), and reports a held-out check (Section 4).

**Where.** Section 2.3, last paragraph; Section 3.6, "The existing rules under the shift"; Table D3; Section 4.

### B10

*"Applying these existing methods to simulated Ariel spectra may provide useful benchmarking information, but the manuscript does not develop a new uncertainty measure, establish a new failure theorem, or identify a broadly transferable relationship between physical misspecification and confidence calibration."*

**Response.** The revision does not claim a new uncertainty measure or a theorem. What it adds about these methods is a result in the mission's terms, with its reason. On clean spectra the confidence rules allocate a retrieval budget no better than a random one, and on a mixed population they find almost none of the hazed targets (3 % at a 20 % budget). They send the budget to the labels the classifier is unsure about, which are the undecidable ones, where the retrieval cannot help either. Only a shift flag finds the planets the classifier is confidently wrong about (Section 3.6, Tables 17 and C2). The statement made in the Discussion ("Why confidence rules fail and when a shift flag helps") is that a shift flag has value when routed to a method that does not share the failure, and that routed to the clear-model retrieval it loses more targets than no flag.

**Where.** Section 3.6; Tables 17 and C2; Section 4.

### B11

*"An additional concern is the reliance on aggregate classification accuracy as the principal measure of scientific utility. Ariel's Tier-1 reconnaissance observations are intended to inform decisions about which planets warrant deeper characterization. The consequences of incorrectly excluding a scientifically valuable planet may be substantially different from those of unnecessarily selecting a planet for additional observation. Yet the manuscript does not formulate a mission-level decision problem that incorporates these asymmetric consequences, observational costs, target priorities, or the number of follow-up opportunities available. Indeed, the reported haze experiments show that overall classification accuracy can obscure a near-total collapse in sensitivity to particular molecules. The more scientifically relevant quantity may therefore be the number and characteristics of valuable targets lost under a constrained selection strategy, rather than the average percentage of correctly classified simulated spectra."*

**Response.** Adopted as a primary metric. The selection simulation (Section 2.8) gives the 955 POP-I planets 60 % Tier-2 slots (573), ranked by the mean present-probability of the three probabilistic learners, and scores every strategy by the number of planets carrying the molecule that are not selected, for CH₄ and H₂O, and for either molecule in the retrieval comparisons. Every design, the routing method included, is scored this way (Table 14), the slot count is varied from 20 to 80 % (Section 3.5), and the abstract reports the clear-model retrieval's result in targets lost. Accuracy and recall on present labels are reported beside it where they say something targets lost does not. The asymmetry of consequences is handled in the one way the data allow: at a fixed slot count, slots wasted equal targets lost plus a constant, so the ranking of strategies does not depend on the relative weight of the two errors (Table C1, row C6). Section 4 states that ranking by a molecule's probability is a proxy for a selection the mission will make by theme and signal-to-noise, and that observing costs, target priorities and follow-up opportunities are not modelled. The collapse in sensitivity the reviewer notes is reported per molecule, as recall on present labels and on hazed targets (Sections 3.3, 3.5 and 3.6).

**Where.** Section 2.8; Sections 3.5 and 3.6; Tables 14, 17 and C2; Section 4; Abstract.

### B12

*"The author's independent reconstruction of the original classifier also warrants recognition, particularly because the original trained models and complete implementation were unavailable. Reproducing the published accuracies across 48 combinations provides reasonable evidence that the reconstructed system captures much of the original design's behavior. However, agreement in classification accuracy does not establish equivalence of learned decision boundaries, feature dependencies, or responses to out-of-distribution spectra. Different implementations may achieve similar in-distribution accuracies while exhibiting substantially different robustness profiles. Therefore, the demonstrated weaknesses should be attributed primarily to the reconstructed implementation and tested design assumptions, rather than treated as experimentally verified properties of the unavailable original classifiers."*

**Response.** Accepted throughout. Section 2.1 defines "the re-implemented classifier" as the recipe as read here with the deviations of Section 2.2, and the text uses that phrase where a weakness is claimed, including Table 12 and the Conclusion; the abstract says the classifiers were re-implemented. The consortium's original trained models were not released, and Section 2.1 says no claim here concerns them. Section 3.1 says the agreement is consistent with following the published recipe and says nothing about the original trained models, and Section 4 ("Scope") repeats that the results concern the design as re-implemented.

**Where.** Abstract; Section 1; Section 2.1, last paragraph; Section 2.2; Section 3.1; Section 4, "Scope".

### B13

*"Overall, the manuscript is methodologically competent and contains useful diagnostic information, particularly its identification of the three optical photometric inputs as a source of haze-related classification failure. The study also benefits from reproducible code, systematic perturbation testing, and explicit acknowledgment of several limitations. These strengths, however, do not resolve the central issue of novelty. Most of the manuscript applies established robustness-testing methodologies to an additional simulated classification problem and obtains results that are broadly consistent with existing understanding of distribution shift, incomplete training distributions, and confidence miscalibration. Its most specific finding concerns a vulnerability of one reconstructed classifier architecture, while its broadest conclusion—that each simulator-trained classifier should be individually tested before operational deployment—is already a standard principle of model validation."*

**Response.** The revision's broadest conclusion is no longer about testing classifiers. It is that under the training prior most of this classifier's errors fall on labels the spectrum does not settle, that the simulated departures sort into two classes, that real spectra expose the training prior itself, which the classifier and a retrieval share, that a routing method's gain on its development panel did not repeat on new planets, and that the existing fallback fails unless it models the departure (Sections 4 and 5). The reproducible code the reviewer notes is extended to every new experiment. Expectations committed before their runs, with all 45 outcomes including the ones not met and the three never run, are in Table C1, and Section 2.9 lists the measurements that had none.

**Where.** Sections 4 and 5; Table C1; Section 2.9; Data and Code Availability.

### B14

*"I therefore do not recommend publication in its present form. The manuscript would be more appropriately characterized as a reproducibility and robustness benchmark than as a study establishing a new scientific principle. To justify reconsideration as an original research contribution, the author would need to move beyond documenting the limits of simulated classifiers and establish a consequential, non-obvious result about the propagation of simulator misspecification into Ariel's scientific decision-making or population-level inference. The existing experiments may provide a useful foundation for such work, but the present conclusions do not yet demonstrate that advance."*

**Response.** The revision was built to this sentence. The population-level result is the prevalence of undecidable labels and of irrecoverable errors under a named prior (Sections 3.2 and 3.3). The decision-level result is the propagation of the simulated departures into Tier-2 targets lost at a fixed budget, for the classifier, for the existing rules and for the retrieval that is meant to be the stronger method (Sections 3.5 and 3.6), with the sign of the effect, a correct flag with the wrong destination loses more targets than no flag, stated in Section 4. The departure measured on real planets is carried into selection through a population muted by three, which costs more targets than the simulated haze and which no design recovers (Section 3.5; Table 14); the real spectra themselves are scored in labels, because the panel has no population to select from (Table 12). The non-obvious parts are that a retrieval under the training prior reads real spectra as absent just as the classifier does, that the real giants' muting is degenerate with low abundance within that prior, and that a budget routed by a correct flag to the Bayes-optimal selector of clean spectra loses more targets than no retrieval. The benchmark of the original note remains in the paper as Section 3.3 because it located the mechanism the rest of the paper tests.

**Where.** Sections 3.2 to 3.6; Sections 4 and 5.
