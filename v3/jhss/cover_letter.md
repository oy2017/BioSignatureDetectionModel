# Cover letter for the resubmission

Manuscript: "Can a simulator-trained classifier for Ariel Tier-1 spectra be trusted?" (JHSS; decision of 17 October 2026: revise and resubmit, after the editor's discussion message of 15 October setting out the additions required).

Resubmitted as an original article under the title "Which molecular labels Ariel Tier-1 spectra can decide: error classes of a re-implemented classifier and a routing method developed on JWST spectra".

## Message to the editor on resubmission

Dear Dr Apte,

Thank you for reading the manuscript and for the discussion with the reviewer. I accepted the suggestions in full and have done the work. The revised manuscript is resubmitted as an original article, and the four requests of your message are each answered with a measurement:

1. **External validation on WASP-39 b.** The JWST benchmark spectrum of WASP-39 b was converted to the classifier's 104-bin input by a documented procedure, validated on a spectrum whose answer is known: the benchmark's own best-fit model, pushed through the conversion, differs from the same model binned directly by 22 ppm rms and changes no verdict. A sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations measures how much each verdict depends on the reduction. At JWST precision the classifier agrees with the published composition on every label the literature decides, although one learner of four misses the water. Because one planet is a weak test, 22 further planets in 25 reductions were converted the same way. Over their 66 labels the re-implemented classifier is right on 39 of 40 absent labels and 3 of 26 present ones, and scores 45.5 with a split counted as half, against 40 for calling every label absent. At Ariel Tier-1 noise it scores 41.7.

2. **Identifiability.** A nested-sampling retrieval of every one of the classifier's 955 test planets gives for every label the posterior probability of the true class, under a named population: POP-I's planets and abundances at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, retrieved under the training prior. At the 0.95 level, a third of the CH₄ and NH₃ labels, 44 % of the CO₂ labels and 54 % of the H₂O labels are not decided at Tier-1 precision, and only 17 % of planets have all four labels decided. The retrieval's own accuracy, 92 to 94 %, is 9 to 16 points above the classifier. The manuscript states that these posteriors are of the noise-free spectrum with the radius fixed, that the prior favours absent labels, and that a fifth of the labels sit next to the 10⁻⁴ threshold, and it measures what each of these changes.

3. **A remedy with measurable value.** I propose a routing method that needs the catalogue's bulk parameters and the spectrum, and no retrieval or published composition. An amplitude flag sends a planet to a design retrained with aerosols, a shape flag sends a cell to the AIRS-only input, and a verdict is withheld where the amplitude leaves the population. On the 26 JWST reductions it gains 8.5 of 66 labels over the re-implemented classifier at JWST precision (descriptive 95 % interval over planets, 2.8 to 14.0) and 2.7 at Tier-1 noise (1.2 to 4.3), and it finds methane on five of eight reductions against none at JWST precision. It costs 4.9 points of clean accuracy in the simulator. I also state its limitations: on that panel it cannot be separated from the AIRS-only input or a retrained design used everywhere, retraining does better in simulation, about two fifths of its gain at JWST precision lies on labels the coverage mask hides, and it was built on this panel. A further batch of planets scored once with the rule frozen is the test that would settle it. A retrieval budget routed by a flag is measured separately in targets lost: with a fifth of the planets hazed, a flag routed to a retrieval with the haze density free matches an oracle (13 against 12 of 359 CH₄ targets), and the same flag routed to the clear-model retrieval loses 71, against 29 with no retrieval.

4. **The two classes of error.** Every classifier error, on clean spectra and under each departure, was assigned to a class by the retrieval or by the calibrated test (recoverable: the spectrum decides the label; irrecoverable: it does not). About four fifths of the classifier's errors on clean spectra are irrecoverable. Haze and unocculted spots leave the labels decidable when their strength is known, and 57 to 87 % of the errors they introduce are recoverable; a high cloud deck removes information on absent labels; threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction, and its added errors are mostly irrecoverable. On the real spectra the recoverable class appears in the operational sense that another input finds the label: the classifier misses methane on seven of eight reductions of five sub-Neptunes, a failure the transplants place below 1.95 µm, and the AIRS-only input finds it on six. No retrieval of the real spectra is reported, so the irrecoverable class was not tested on them. Ten of the twelve real giants with published abundances are also muted beyond the training prior, which costs labels at Tier-1 noise and not at JWST precision. The implications beyond Ariel have their own paragraph in the Discussion.

The reviewer's further points are each answered in the separate response file, which lists every comment of the reviewer and of your discussion message verbatim with how and where the revision addresses it. Expectations for most of the measurements were committed to the public repository before their runs, and all 39 outcomes, including the ones not met and the three never run, are in Appendix C; the manuscript says which measurements had none. Before resubmission the revised manuscript was checked by automated review passes with AI agents, and their corrections are incorporated and described at the top of the response file. The use of AI tools is disclosed in the Acknowledgements. All code and result tables are public at https://github.com/oy2017/BioSignatureDetectionModel.

I would be grateful if the revised manuscript could be considered as an original article.

With thanks,
Owen Yang
