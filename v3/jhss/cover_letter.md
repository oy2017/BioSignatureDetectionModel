# Cover letter for the resubmission

Manuscript: "Can a simulator-trained classifier for Ariel Tier-1 spectra be trusted?" (JHSS; decision of 17 October 2026: revise and resubmit, after the editor's discussion message of 15 October setting out the additions required).

Resubmitted as an original article under the title "Separating recoverable from irrecoverable errors in Ariel Tier-1 molecular classification: a new design tested on model grids and James Webb Space Telescope spectra".

---

## Message to the editor on resubmission

Dear Dr Apte,

Thank you for reading the manuscript and for the discussion with the reviewer. I accepted the suggestions in full and have done the work. The revised manuscript is resubmitted as an original article, and the four requests of your message are each answered with a measurement:

1. **External validation on WASP-39 b.** The JWST benchmark spectrum of WASP-39 b was converted to the classifier's 104-bin input by a documented procedure, validated on a spectrum whose answer is known. The benchmark's own best-fit model, pushed through the conversion, differs from the same model binned directly by 22 ppm rms and changes no verdict. A sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations measures how much each verdict depends on the reduction. At JWST precision the classifier agrees with the published composition on the two scored labels (H₂O present, CH₄ absent), although one learner of four misses the water. Those labels are thin: both were set after the verdicts, the H₂O label is a judgement call between papers, and the CH₄ label rests on a limit from equilibrium models. Because one planet is a weak test, 22 further planets in 25 reductions were converted the same way. Over the 64 labels of the 23 planets the re-implemented classifier is right on 36 of 38 absent labels and 3 of 26 present ones, and scores 43.5 with a split counted as half, against 38 for calling every label absent. At Ariel Tier-1 noise it scores 39.6. An audit after the independent test on 12 new planets (item 3) found errors, which Section 2.10 records and every result corrects.

2. **Identifiability.** A nested-sampling retrieval of every one of the classifier's 955 test planets gives for every label the posterior probability of the true class, under a named population: POP-I's planets and abundances at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, retrieved under the training prior. At the 0.95 level, a third of the CH₄ and NH₃ labels, 44 % of the CO₂ labels and 54 % of the H₂O labels are undecidable at Tier-1 precision, and only 17 % of planets have all four labels decidable. The retrieval's own accuracy, 92 to 94 %, is 9 to 16 points above the classifier, and an undecided label is unsettled rather than uninformative: the retrieval leans to the right answer on 81 to 86 % of them. The manuscript states that these posteriors are of the noise-free spectrum with the radius fixed, that the prior favours absent labels, and that a fifth of the labels sit next to the 10⁻⁴ threshold, and it measures what each of these changes. Under a second population, 200 Mission Candidate Sample planets with FastChem equilibrium chemistry and clear atmospheres, retrieved under the same prior, the undecidable fraction falls to 7-24 % and 56 % of the classifier's errors fall on those labels, so the prevalence depends on the population. There the matched ensemble makes about half the classifier's errors on labels the spectrum settles (21 against 43 over ten noise draws), although its training chemistry shares a code with this test. Freeing the radius moves the POP-I undecidable fraction by at most two points.

3. **A remedy with measurable value.** The remedy I put forward is a new design, the matched ensemble. It trains networks for each spectrum on its measured bins at its real errors, on a population with correlated chemistry, and sets its thresholds on held-out simulations. Frozen and scored once through JWST coverage on a grid from the development grid's code and on a photochemical grid from another code family, it reaches a balanced credit of 0.711 and 0.773 at JWST precision and 0.636 and 0.700 at Tier-1 noise, against 0.574, 0.658, 0.531 and 0.567 for an earlier routing method and above every other design tested, at false-present rates of 8-17 %, and above a nested-sampling retrieval with this study's four-gas model on the photochemical grid. On the aerosol-free photochemical grid 82 % of its present calls on truly present labels vanish when the molecule's opacity is removed. At Ariel's coverage in simulation its accuracy is 84.2 % clean and 80.5 % under a haze in its training, against 81.2 and 63.5 %, and it loses about half as many targets under that haze. On real labels, seen beforehand but used for no design choice, it leads at Tier-1 noise (63.4 against 57.9 of 88; interval 0.7 to 10.7) and, at JWST precision, on labels outside the routing method's panel (35.0 against 29.5 of 41; 0.5 to 10.5), both lower bounds close to zero. Over all 105 JWST labels the lead is 84.0 against 81.5 (−6.5 to 11.5), and a prospective test on new public spectra is the next step. The design's own probability is its diagnostic: rules that fail on the classifier work on it, and its kept labels score far higher (0.768 against 0.614 on the photochemical grid), mostly through the better learner; declining alone adds about what the best existing rule adds to the classifier (0.007 to 0.045 against 0.006 to 0.033). The routing method's two flags send spectra to a design retrained with aerosols or to the AIRS-only variant. It gains 8.5 of 64 labels at JWST precision on the panel it was built on (descriptive 95 % interval 2.5 to 14.1) and 2.3 at Tier-1 noise (0.9 to 3.8), and gained nothing when frozen and applied to 12 new planets (16.0 of 24 for both). A retrieval budget routed by a flag is measured separately in targets lost: with a fifth of the planets hazed, a flag routed to a retrieval with the haze density free matches an oracle (13 against 12 of 359 CH₄ targets), and the same flag routed to the clear-model retrieval loses 71, against 29 with no retrieval.

4. **The two classes of error.** Every classifier error, on clean spectra and under each departure, was assigned to a class by the retrieval or by the calibrated test (recoverable: the spectrum decides the label; irrecoverable: it does not). About four fifths of the classifier's errors on clean spectra fall on labels not settled at the 0.95 level, although the posterior leans to the right answer on most of them. Haze and unocculted spots leave the labels decidable when their strength is known, and 57 to 87 % of the errors they introduce are recoverable by the pooled fitting test (42 to 76 % by class-conditional thresholds, with CH₄ and NH₃ above half and H₂O and CO₂ about half). Threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction, and its added errors are mostly irrecoverable. On the real spectra the recoverable class appears in the operational sense that another input finds the label: the classifier finds methane on none of eight reductions of five sub-Neptunes, and the AIRS-only input finds it on seven, with no false methane on the panel. By the posterior definition under the training prior at Tier-1 precision none of those errors is recoverable, and that prior reads muted real spectra as low abundance. Ten of the twelve real giants with published abundances are also muted beyond the training prior at those compositions, though not at lower abundances. A population so muted would lose more targets than the simulated haze. The published recipe does not recover them, and the matched ensemble recovers only some of the water targets. The class split survives the new design in part: under haze its recoverable errors fall to 202, against 675, while on clean spectra most of the errors it removes lie on undecidable labels, which it leans on more often the right way without settling them. The confidence of the classifier marks those labels only in part (AUROC 0.59 to 0.77). The implications beyond Ariel have their own paragraph in the Discussion.

The reviewer's further points are each answered in the separate response file, which lists every comment of the reviewer and of your discussion message verbatim with how and where the revision addresses it. Expectations for most of the measurements were committed to the public repository before their runs, and all 104 outcomes (51 met, 36 in part, 15 not met, two never run) are in Appendix C; the manuscript says which measurements had none. All code and result tables are public at https://github.com/oy2017/BioSignatureDetectionModel.

I would be grateful if the revised manuscript could be considered as an original article.

With thanks,
Owen Yang
