## Message to the editor on resubmission

Dear Dr Apte,

Thank you for reading the manuscript and for the discussion with the reviewer. I accepted the suggestions in full and have done the work. The revised manuscript is resubmitted as an original article, and the four requests of your message are each answered with a measurement:

1. **External validation on WASP-39 b.** The JWST benchmark spectrum of WASP-39 b (the four Early Release Science modes re-reduced uniformly by Carter & May et al. 2024, extended with the MIRI spectrum of Powell et al. 2024) was converted to the classifier's 104-bin Tier-3 input by a documented procedure, and the procedure was validated on a spectrum whose answer is known: the benchmark's own best-fit model, pushed through the conversion, differs from the same model binned directly by 22 ppm rms and changes no verdict. A 24-configuration sensitivity matrix (each mode's individual reductions, two NIRISS pipelines, offsets on and off, masked lines, with and without MIRI) measures how much each verdict depends on the reduction. At JWST precision the classifier agrees with the published composition on every label the literature decides: three of four learners call H₂O present, all four call CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at the 10⁻⁴ threshold, and CO₂ appears at 10⁻⁵ where its published abundance puts it; at Ariel Tier-1 precision the water is detected in 58 % of realisations. The one wrong learner is wrong for a reason the paper locates in the real spectrum, not in the conversion. Because one planet is a weak test, twenty-one further planets with public JWST spectra were converted the same way, in three batches whose labels were frozen before their verdicts and the later two added after the routing had been designed on the earlier, with the published abundances behind every label tabulated (Section 2.6, Table 2; Section 3.4).

2. **Identifiability.** A nested-sampling retrieval of every one of the classifier's 955 test planets, under the consortium's own test prior (POP-I) at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, gives for every label the posterior probability of the true class. Under that named population, a third of the CH₄ and NH₃ labels, 44 % of the CO₂ labels and 54 % of the H₂O labels cannot be decided at Tier-1 precision (posterior of the true class below 0.95), only 17 % of planets have all four labels decidable, and the retrieval's own accuracy, 92 to 94 %, is the ceiling the spectra allow, 9 to 16 points above the classifier. The manuscript states that these posteriors are of the noise-free spectrum and that the retrieval prior favours absent labels, reports decidability by class and by Bayes factor, and checks the population figures on 200 planets retrieved on the classifier's own noisy realisation (Section 2.4; Section 3.2; Table 3).

3. **A remedy with measurable value.** The remedy is a pipeline computable from the Mission Candidate Sample and the spectrum alone: a design retrained with aerosols beyond the training prior, an amplitude flag that reads the catalogue's scale-height modulation against the spectrum's dispersion, a shape flag that reads the classifier's nested inputs against each other, each sent to its own remedy, and a withheld verdict where the amplitude leaves the population. On 26 JWST reductions with labels frozen before any verdict it scores 52 of 62 against 42 of 59 for the published design, six labels going from wrong to right and none the other way, methane found on five of the eight methane reductions against none, with the gain holding on planets added after both flags were designed; its cost, 4.6 points of clean accuracy in the simulator, is reported beside the gain and explained by the identifiability ceiling (Sections 2.7 and 3.5; Table 8). Its value in target selection is measured as Tier-2 targets lost at a fixed 60 % selection, with its draw-to-draw spread, against the existing methods: the classifier alone, the five decline rules of the original note, and a retrieval of every planet. That result has three parts. On clean spectra, the retrieval of every planet is the selection oracle at 365 core-hours, and no cheap rule allocates a smaller retrieval budget much better than chance. Under haze, the existing retrieval fails more confidently than the classifier, and a correct shift flag routed to it loses more targets than no retrieval. And on a population in which a fifth of the planets are hazed, a one-number score on the three optical inputs, routed to a retrieval with the haze density free, allocates a 20 % retrieval budget within noise of an oracle that knows which planets are hazed and of the existing Mahalanobis rule used the same way, recovering 80 % of the hazed methane targets against 3 % for the classifier alone, 17 % for a random budget and 3 % for the confidence rules; without any retrieval, the same score routed to the AIRS-only variant recovers two thirds of the targets haze adds to the loss, at no retraining, and is never worse than the classifier. The manuscript also states what the diagnostic does not do: the undecidable class is recognised only in part by the classifier's own confidence and no better by anything cheap, and on the real panel the optical score alone fired on two of the eight methane reductions of a 26-reduction panel, while routing by it or by the input-disagreement flag recovers five of them at a measured cost on clean spectra (Sections 2.7 and 3.5; Tables 8 to 11; Figure 5).

4. **The two classes of error.** Every classifier error, on clean spectra and under each departure, was assigned to a class by the retrieval (recoverable: the spectrum decides the label; irrecoverable: it does not). Three quarters of the classifier's errors on clean spectra are irrecoverable. Haze and unocculted spots leave the labels decidable, and 57 to 87 % of the errors they introduce are recoverable; a deep cloud deck removes information on absent labels; threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction and its added errors are mostly irrecoverable. The same two classes appear on the real spectra: the classifier missed methane on all five methane-bearing sub-Neptunes (seven of eight JWST reductions) while the same design restricted to its AIRS spectrometer inputs found it on four of the five (recoverable), and the five real warm giants are muted beyond anything the simulator population can produce at their bulk parameters, in every bootstrap resample (a prior limit no input-space rule can see). A retrieval that models the haze with one free parameter recovers 222 of the 224 methane labels the clean spectra decide; the same retrieval without that parameter is confidently wrong on 99 % of them (Sections 2.5, 3.3, 3.4 and 3.6; Tables 6 and 10; Figures 3 and 4).

The reviewer's further points (the scope of the "trust" claim, the mission-level decision problem, attribution to the re-implementation, and the compression of the second classifier and the decline-rule results) are each answered in the separate response file, which lists every comment of the reviewer and of your discussion message verbatim with how and where the revision addresses it. Every expectation was committed to the public repository before the corresponding run, and all 31 outcomes, including the ones not met and the three never run, are in Appendix C. Before resubmission the revised manuscript was itself reviewed independently by four readers, and their corrections, among them the explicit statement that the retrievals are of noise-free spectra and the measurement of what that changes, are incorporated and listed at the top of the response file. All code, result tables and the conversion of the JWST spectra are public.

I would be grateful if the revised manuscript could be considered as an original article.

With thanks,
Owen Yang
