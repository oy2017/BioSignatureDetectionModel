**Original article**

# Which molecular labels Ariel Tier-1 spectra can decide: error classes of a re-implemented classifier and a routing method tested on independent JWST spectra

Owen Yang<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Henry M. Gunn High School, 780 Arastradero Rd, Palo Alto, CA 94306, USA. Tel.: +1 650 686 7813. E-mail: owenhyang@gmail.com (corresponding author)

---

## Abstract

Machine learning classification has been proposed for sorting the exoplanet atmospheres of Ariel's Tier-1 survey of about 1,000 planets by composition, and target selection on those labels may dismiss a planet for good. Such classifiers are trained and tested on one simulator, so the identifiability of their labels and their behaviour under distribution shift are unknown. This study re-implements the consortium's Tier-1 classifiers, checks them against 48 published accuracies, measures both, and proposes and tests a routing method. An atmospheric retrieval of 955 simulated transmission spectroscopy targets shows that, at the 0.95 level, under the training prior, on noise-free spectra with the radius fixed, a third to a half of the CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃ labels are not settled at Tier-1 precision. About four fifths of the classifier's clean errors fall on them, although the posterior leans to the right answer on most. Among aerosols, haze leaves labels about as decidable when its strength is known and a high deck does not; threefold noise doubles the undecided fraction. On JWST spectra of 23 planets the classifier is right on 39 of 40 absent labels and finds 6.5 of 26 present ones, or 3.0 at Tier-1 noise. A retrieval under the training prior at Tier-1 precision also reads these spectra as absent, deciding none of the 26 present labels correctly. The classifier misses methane on seven of eight reductions of five sub-Neptunes, and the AIRS-only input finds it on six. The routing method uses two flags that need no retrieval to send a spectrum to that input or to a design retrained with aerosols. On the panel it was built on, it gains 8.5 of 66 labels at JWST precision (descriptive 95 % interval 2.8 to 14.0) and 2.7 at Tier-1 noise (1.2 to 4.3), at 4.9 points of clean simulated accuracy. Frozen and applied to 12 new planets, it gains nothing (16.0 of 24 for both): it finds more present labels and loses as many absent ones, although the methane recovery repeats. Under the model misspecification of haze, a budget that a correct flag routes to a clear-model retrieval loses more targets than no retrieval.

**Keywords:** exoplanet atmospheres; transmission spectroscopy; Ariel; JWST; machine learning classification; identifiability; atmospheric retrieval; target selection; distribution shift; model misspecification; aerosols

## 1. Introduction

When a planet transits its star, part of the starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere, and molecules in the atmosphere absorb that light at their characteristic wavelengths. The resulting transmission spectrum shows which molecules are present. Ariel, a European Space Agency mission scheduled for launch in 2029, will measure transmission spectra of about 1,000 exoplanets between 0.5 and 7.8 µm. It will use three broadband photometers below 1.1 µm, which give the three optical points of the classifier's input, and three spectrometer channels (NIRSpec, AIRS-CH0 and AIRS-CH1) at longer wavelengths (1, 2). Each target is observed first at low signal-to-noise in a Tier-1 reconnaissance survey, and a subset is re-observed at higher precision in Tier 2 (1, 3-5); Tier 3 is the mission's highest-resolution binning, which the classifier reads even at Tier-1 noise. Tier-1 spectra are the basis for choosing which planets receive deeper observations; a planet dismissed at Tier 1 may never be observed again.

An atmospheric retrieval fits a forward model of the atmosphere to a spectrum to infer its composition. It requires 10⁵-10⁸ model evaluations per planet (6) and specialist choices of assumptions and priors (7). These requirements make retrievals costly as a first pass over 1,000 planets, although the whole Ariel target list has been retrieved once in simulation (8). With the simple forward model used here, the test population took 365 core-hours (Section 3.6). Machine-learning classifiers, which answer in seconds once trained, are a faster alternative. The Ariel consortium's population study of the Tier-1 survey proposed, as a preliminary assessment, four scikit-learn classifiers that flag the presence of CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃, reaching 64-89 % accuracy on simulated planets (7). Their code and trained models were not released.

Like the models developed for the Ariel Data Challenges (6) and in the wider machine-learning retrieval literature (9-14), these classifiers were trained and tested on spectra from the same simulator. Such a test shows that a classifier has learned its simulator, not how it behaves when real atmospheres differ from it. Real atmospheres do differ: hazes and clouds mute or reshape spectra (15, 16), unocculted star spots imprint false features (17), line lists disagree (18, 19), and training grids omit absorbers that real atmospheres contain. Misspecification of this kind biases simulation-based inference in general (20-23). JWST transmission spectra of warm giants and sub-Neptunes now carry SO₂, hazes, patchy clouds and metallicities of tens to hundreds of times solar (24-44), none of which a classifier trained on clear hydrogen-helium atmospheres with grey clouds has seen. Simulator-trained retrieval models have been checked against real Hubble and JWST spectra (9, 13, 45-47). On WASP-39 b the retrieved abundances move by more than an order of magnitude with the data reduction alone (48). One amortised retrieval (a neural network trained once and then applied to any spectrum) collapsed on the real WASP-39 b spectrum until the planetary radius was added to its latent parameters (49). A web search on 30 October 2026, including arXiv and the main journals, found no molecule-presence classifier proposed for Ariel that has been checked against a real spectrum.

A classifier's error on a spectrum is one of two things. Either the information was in the spectrum and the classifier missed it, which retraining, a different input or a fallback method can fix. Or the spectrum did not settle the label at that precision, under the population it is drawn from, although the posterior may still lean to the right answer. This study calls the two classes recoverable and irrecoverable. Whether a label is decidable depends on the spectrum: the test is whether an atmosphere with the opposite label fits the spectrum within its noise. The share of a classifier's errors in each class, and the rules that find them, depend on the classifier. The consortium study tested this for one planet, with a retrieval that gave a 48 % probability of CH₄ above 10⁻⁴ at Tier-1 precision (7). Bocchieri et al. (50) then ran retrievals on about 300 simulated Tier-1 targets and defined a P-statistic, the posterior probability that a molecule is present. This is the quantity this study calls decidability. Radica et al. (5) measured it for three benchmark planets across the tiers. Neither measured it on the test population of a classifier, against that classifier's errors, or under departures from the simulator. The classifier's own confidence cannot stand in for the measurement, because confidence does not survive a distribution shift, a result known from image classification (51) that Sections 3.3 and 3.6 reproduce under haze. The alternative a classifier offers is to decline the spectra it is unsure of, or that look unlike its training set; five such rules from the failure-detection literature are scored alongside (Section 2.3).

This study follows a classifier's errors from the spectrum to the target list. It first asks how often physically distinct compositions give indistinguishable Ariel spectra under an explicitly defined population (Sections 2.4 and 3.2), and which of the classifier's errors could therefore be repaired, under the simulator and under each departure from it (Sections 2.5 and 3.3). It then tests the classifier on real spectra, once the observed JWST spectra are made compatible with its inputs by a justified and validated procedure, and measures how those spectra differ from the training population (Sections 2.6 and 3.4). From those differences it builds a routing method, measures it at JWST precision, at Tier-1 noise and in target selection, and tests it, frozen, on planets it was not built on (Sections 2.7 and 3.5). Finally it asks whether the existing methods, a retrieval of every planet and the standard decline rules, share the classifier's failure, and what a flag buys at a fixed retrieval budget (Sections 2.8 and 3.6). The classifiers were first re-implemented and checked against all 48 published accuracies (Section 3.1). Expectations for the identifiability measurements, the error classes, the external test, the diagnostics, the mixed-population test, the per-reduction flag, the confined hazes and the independent test were committed to the repository before their runs. The outcomes, including those not met, are reported (Section 4, Appendix C). The muting census (a comparison of each real spectrum's feature size with a clear model twin), the retrained designs and the routers (rules that send a flagged spectrum to another input or design) had none.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 The published design and its re-implementation

The re-implementation followed Sections 2.2 and 2.5 of the consortium study (7) element by element (Table A1). Isothermal atmospheres with random temperatures, abundances of CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃, and grey cloud decks were generated for every planet of the Ariel Mission Candidate Sample (52), four times for training (POP-III) and once for testing (POP-I). Counts differ slightly between the analyses below because a few spectra failed to render or to bin under one condition or another. Of the 977 planets of the Mission Candidate Sample, 955 have a clean test spectrum (one with no departure from the training simulator), and these are the test population below. The exceptions are the reproduction of Section 3.1 and the mixed-population test of Section 2.8, which use the 954 of them that also have a hazed spectrum. Spectra were binned at Tier-3 resolution, scattered with Tier-1 noise and normalised per spectrum. A molecule was labelled present if its abundance exceeded 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴ or 10⁻³, and four scikit-learn classifiers at default settings (53) were trained for each molecule and threshold.

The study did not state whether the classifier input included the three optical points. It stated that the classifiers learn "from their spectral shape over the whole wavelength range sampled by Ariel" and "gather information from all the spectral data points" (7), and its figures of observed Tier-1 spectra show those points, so they were included. Four terms are used throughout. A *learner* is one of the four scikit-learn models (k-nearest neighbours, KNN; multilayer perceptron, MLP; random forest, RFC; support-vector classifier, SVC). The *classifier* is their average in simulation and their majority on a real spectrum. The *design* is the recipe, which can be retrained on another population or given another input. The *re-implemented classifier* is this recipe, with the deviations of Section 2.2, on all 104 inputs and trained on POP-III. The consortium's original trained models were not released, and no claim here concerns them.

### 2.2 Forward model, noise and deviations

Spectra were computed with TauREx 3 (54) at R ≈ 1,000 and binned by integration: isothermal atmospheres of 100 layers with constant abundances, molecular absorption and Rayleigh scattering, a grey cloud deck and, where stated, a Mie haze. They were first rendered through the MultiREx wrapper (55); the released code calls TauREx directly and reproduces them bit for bit. TauREx 3.3.2 reads the pressure grid of Exo-Transmit opacity tables as bar, whereas Exo-Transmit treats it as Pa; the pressure grid was divided by 10⁵ when the tables were loaded. The inconsistency was reported to the TauREx developers and has since been corrected in their development branch (56). With the correction, TauREx spectra of 1,804 test atmospheres agreed better with Exo-Transmit's own code (median shape correlation 0.9986, against 0.986).

Per-bin noise came from a payload model built with ExoSim 2 (57). The model takes the one-hour noise-to-signal ratio for the host's effective temperature, scales it to the host's distance and radius and to the transit duration, and divides it by the square root of the number of transits. Its one free parameter was calibrated so that a signal-to-noise ratio of 7 on the five-scale-height atmospheric signal (the transit-depth change across five atmospheric scale heights) reproduced the Mission Candidate Sample's Tier-3 transit counts. The model then predicted these counts with a standard deviation of 0.07 dex (a dex is a factor of ten) over 929 targets. For the three example planets in Figure 1 of the consortium study (HD 209458 b, GJ 1214 b and WASP-79 b), its median AIRS noise is 73, 421 and 227 ppm per bin. These values match the published noise bands in size, as read from that figure. The photometric and NIRSpec channels were not checked against published bands, which the limitations in Section 4 note.

Three deviations from the study's recipe remain. (i) Opacities came from Exo-Transmit's tables (18, 58); substituting ExoMol cross sections (19) for H₂O, CH₄, CO₂ and CO cost the classifier 2.9 percentage points (Section 3.3), which bounds the effect of this choice. (ii) Collision-induced absorption was omitted. With the HITRAN tables (59) it changes the largest bin of a spectrum by a median of 1 ppm on 200 random test planets, by 49 ppm at the 90th percentile and by 500 ppm at worst (`cia_effect.py`). With it in the test spectra alone the classifier loses 0.1 points. With both populations re-rendered and the design refitted, the 48 accuracies move by a mean absolute 0.4 points and at most 1.9, and they come no closer to the published values (mean absolute difference 1.6 against 1.4). (iii) Noise came from the payload model instead of ArielRad (60), which is not public. An earlier reading of the publication, with spectra binned to the seven Tier-1 points, the atmosphere truncated at 1 Pa and the noise set exactly at the Tier-1 requirement, missed the published accuracies by 10 points on average and by up to 38. The comparison with the published values therefore discriminates between readings of the recipe.

### 2.3 The departures and the stress test

For each departure (Table 1), the same test planets were re-rendered or modified, so every loss was a within-planet difference under the same noise draw. Haze followed the Lee et al. prescription (61) with particles of 0.1 µm radius filling the column. TauREx applies the density given here as one particle number density in every layer, from the model's base to its top at 10⁻⁴ Pa. Because that density does not fall with height, the haze's mixing ratio rises with height, its optical depth accumulates up to the top boundary, and the size of its optical excess (the depth it adds at the optical points) depends on where the boundary sits. On three test planets, moving the top by two decades changes the excess by up to a factor of two (`haze_top_test.py`). Retrievals of ten observed hot Jupiters expressed haze opacity at 0.35 µm as a multiple, *a*, of the H₂ Rayleigh opacity (62) and found log₁₀ a = 2.1-5.7 (16). At 100 Pa the densities 3 × 10⁷, 2.4 × 10⁸ and 10¹⁰ m⁻³ used here correspond to log₁₀ a ≈ 2.4-2.7, 3.3-3.6 and 4.9-5.2. A constant density makes *a* rise with height, by two decades for every two decades of pressure, so the correspondence holds at that level only. To test whether the results depend on this form, the test population was also rendered under three confined hazes, with the same planets and decks. The first has the same particles and density, below 1 Pa only (optical excess a median of 409 ppm, against 1,094 ppm for the column-filling haze). The other two have a power-law opacity at constant mixing ratio, slope 4 and log₁₀ a ≈ 3.1 and 4.1 at 0.35 µm at every height (373 and 546 ppm; `haze_confined.py`). Spots used PHOENIX spectra from the STScI atlas (63) at a spot-to-photosphere temperature ratio of 0.85, close to the 0.86 of Rackham et al. (17), for every host type, an M-dwarf contrast that differs for FGK hosts (64).

Three input variants of the design were trained on the same spectra: all 104 bins (the input of the re-implemented classifier), the 101 bins without the three optical points (the "no-optical variant"), and the AIRS bins above 1.95 µm alone (the "AIRS-only variant"). Recall is the share of present labels a design reports as present. Two transplant experiments located the haze failure: the three hazed optical points were copied into otherwise clean spectra, and the clean points were restored in otherwise hazed spectra. Table 1 lists every level run; Table 5 reports those that matter for what follows, chosen after the runs, and the rest are in the repository. The stress test, in the manner of a corruption benchmark (65), measured three quantities. The loss is the clean accuracy minus the accuracy under the departure. The irreducible loss is the clean accuracy minus the accuracy of the design retrained with the departure present. The third is the loss of the design trained on a randomised grid in which each planet independently carried haze, spots and extra noise (66).

Five decline rules of the kind used for failure detection were scored alongside. Two, the confidence rules, read the classifier's confidence: the probability margin ("margin") and the spread of the four learners' probabilities ("ensemble"; 67). Three, the distance rules, read the distance of a spectrum from the training set: the Mahalanobis distance (68), the mean distance to the 10 nearest training spectra ("k-NN distance"; 69) and the principal-component reconstruction error ("PCA"). Each rule's threshold is the 90th percentile of its score on the clean test spectra themselves, so that it declines 10 % of them. A rule's clean decline rate is therefore fixed by construction. A held-out check (thresholds set on a random half of the test population and applied to the other half, 20 splits) is reported in Section 4. A rule's selective credit (70, 71) is the accuracy of the planets it accepts under a departure, minus the accuracy the same rule reaches on clean spectra at the same coverage. Split-conformal coverage is reported (72, 73) is reported alongside: the fraction of planets whose conformal prediction set contains the true label, calibrated to 90 % on clean spectra. These five rules are the "existing rules" of Section 3.6.

### 2.4 Identifiability: which labels a spectrum decides

For planet *i* with true parameters θ and molecule *X*, the label "X > 10⁻⁴" is decidable at precision σ if no atmosphere with the opposite label, drawn from the stated population, fits the noise-free spectrum within the noise. The cheap form of this test is

Δχ²ᵢ(X) = min over θ′ with X = 10⁻⁴ and over δ of Σ_b [(S_b(θ) − S_b(θ′) − δ) / σ_b]² (eq1)

where S_b(θ) is the model transit depth in bin *b* for parameters θ, σ_b the planet's Tier-1 noise in that bin and δ an additive depth offset. The minimum runs over the nuisance parameters of the population (temperature over 0.7-1.05 times the equilibrium temperature T_eq, grey cloud top over 5 × 10² to 10⁶ Pa, the other three abundances over 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻²). *X* is held at the 10⁻⁴ boundary. This is the hardest counterfactual (the opposite-label atmosphere closest to the truth), because band depth is monotone in abundance. The sum runs over the 104 Tier-3 bins. The minimum was found by Nelder-Mead from the true parameters and two random restarts from the prior, keeping the smallest value (median 569 forward calls per planet-molecule; 955 planets, four molecules, 207 core-hours). A missed minimum inflates Δχ² and overstates decidability, the opposite sign to the bias discussed next. Under a departure the test is rerun with the departure's physics in the counterfactual model as well, with its strength held at the true value. It then asks whether the label is decidable given a correct model of the departure, which is knowledge a mission would not have. Section 3.6 measures the price of fitting the strength for haze. White noise at *c* times σ needs no run, since Δχ² scales as 1/*c*².

The test represents the opposite class by its single best-fitting point, which over-credits that class, so its scale was calibrated rather than assumed. A nested-sampling retrieval (74, 75; the nestle implementation, multi-ellipsoid sampler, 100 live points, at most 20,000 likelihood calls) was run on every one of the 955 test planets, with the same free quantities, the four abundances over 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻² and uniform priors on the population's ranges. It gave for every label the posterior probability of the true class, p_true, which is the P-statistic of Bocchieri et al. (50) evaluated for the true class (median 3,961 calls and 19.9 min per planet; 365 core-hours). A label is decidable at level *q* if p_true > *q*, with *q* = 0.95 throughout; the Δχ² at which p_true crosses 0.95 was read off per molecule and used as the test's threshold. The looser level 0.84, the one-sided one-sigma level, is reported as a sensitivity; it was in the plan and the calibration script before any retrieval ran, but no expectation was recorded for it. The retrieval's own accuracy, the fraction of labels with p_true > 0.5, is the accuracy ceiling the spectra allow under its prior.

Three properties of this retrieval shape every number that rests on it. First, unless stated, it is run on the noise-free spectrum with the Tier-1 σ in the likelihood only: p_true is the posterior of that one noise-free realisation, not an average over realisations, whereas the classifier reads one noisy realisation. To measure the difference, a fixed random 200 of the 955 planets were also retrieved on the classifier's own noisy realisation (Section 3.2). Second, the abundance prior is POP-III's, 10⁻⁹-10⁻², which is wider than POP-I's. Under it an uninformative spectrum already gives p_true = 0.71 for an absent label and 0.29 for a present one. An absent label then reaches 0.95 on a Bayes factor of 8 against the opposite class, and a present label needs 47. Prevalences are therefore reported for present and absent labels separately. They are reported once more with decidability defined by a Bayes factor above 19 relative to the prior odds, the prior-free counterpart of 0.95. The 200-planet subsample was retrieved a third time under POP-I's own prior, 10⁻⁷-10⁻², to measure the prior's effect directly (Section 3.2). Third, the retrieval's forward model is the training model itself, with the planetary radius fixed and a small depth offset free. "Undecidable" therefore means not decided at the 0.95 level under this prior and this forward model: a chemically informed model or a narrower prior could decide some of these labels, and a free radius could make others undecidable. The population is explicit: the planets and abundances of POP-I at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, retrieved under POP-III's prior. The true joint distribution of atmospheric conditions among Ariel's targets is unknown, so every prevalence reported here is a prevalence under this named prior, and Section 3.4 measures how the real spectra sit against it.

### 2.5 The two error classes

Each classifier error on a spectrum was assigned a class from the same spectrum's decidability. On clean spectra the posterior is the definition. An error is recoverable if p_true > 0.95 (a retrieval of the noise-free spectrum gets the label right). A label is undecidable if 0.05 ≤ p_true ≤ 0.95 and misled if p_true < 0.05. An error is irrecoverable if its label is undecidable or misled. Misled labels are one in 3,820 on clean spectra (Section 3.2), so in practice irrecoverable errors are errors on undecidable labels. Under a departure the departure-aware Δχ² at the calibrated threshold is used. Because the posterior is of the noise-free spectrum, an error the classifier makes through an unlucky noise draw on a decidable label is counted as recoverable although a retrieval of that realisation may not recover it. The recoverable shares are therefore upper bounds, and Section 3.2 measures the gap on the 200-planet subsample. The prevalence of each class is the fraction of the classifier's errors in it; the expectation recorded before the runs was that haze errors would be mostly recoverable and noise errors mostly irrecoverable (Appendix C). After the runs, the errors under a departure were further split into those the learner also makes on the clean spectrum (shared) and those the departure introduces, a split that is reported as the post-hoc refinement it is.

### 2.6 Real JWST spectra on the Ariel grid

The primary external input is the WASP-39 b benchmark of Carter & May et al. (24). It consists of the four JWST Early Release Science modes (26-29) re-reduced uniformly with fitted inter-mode offsets. It covers 0.52-5.3 µm and was extended to 7.8 µm with the MIRI LRS spectrum of Powell et al. (25). Twenty-two further planets with public JWST transmission spectra, in 25 reductions (a reduction is one published processing of the raw observations), formed a panel (Table 2). Ten planets in thirteen reductions joined WASP-39 b in a first batch. Nine more came in a second batch, after the first batch's verdicts (the classifier's calls on each label) were known and the shape flag of Section 2.7 had been designed. Three more came in a third batch, after the muting-census routing of Section 3.5 had been designed and scored on the first two. The panel is therefore a test of those elements on their later batches and of nothing designed after it; Table C3 lists what was fixed before what.

Labels came from the published free-chemistry retrievals at the 10⁻⁴ threshold: present where the 1σ lower bound lies above it, absent where the upper bound or the quoted upper limit lies below it. Where a paper's abundance straddled 10⁻⁴, or the literature disagreed, the label was marked ambiguous and not scored. Their timing differs by batch. They were committed before the verdicts for the first batch (15 October 2026) and the third (29 October). The second batch's labels were committed with its verdicts on 29 October, so the repository does not show their order. WASP-39 b's scored labels were set on 29 October, after its verdicts; its H₂O and CH₄ labels match the expectations of 16 October. Its H₂O label rests on the free retrieval of Deka et al. (76), log₁₀ −2.08 ± 0.09 with patchy clouds and −2.64 ± 0.08 with Mie aerosols. Its CH₄ label rests on the 3σ limit of 5 × 10⁻⁶ from the equilibrium models of Rustamkulov et al. (29), since no free retrieval prints one. Three labels that rest on detections at or below about 3σ are kept but marked tentative: the CH₄ and CO₂ of GJ 1214 b (2.0 and 2.4σ alone, 3.3σ jointly) and the CH₄ of TOI-732 c (3.2σ). The first batch's labels came from a survey of the literature made before this rule was written down. K2-18 b's H₂O, frozen as absent in its three reductions, rests on published limits near 10⁻³, above the threshold. Every design calls it absent except the haze-retrained design on the Hu reduction (split), so it changes only that design's score, by half a label. A split verdict (two learners each way) is neither right nor wrong, and the scoring of Section 2.7 gives it half credit.

All 104 label cells of the panel were audited against their sources after the verdicts were known (`results/jwst_panel/label_sources.csv`). Of the 66 scored labels, 56 follow the rule as written. Three depart from it: K2-18 b's H₂O in two reductions, as above, and TOI-270 d's NH₃ in the Felix reduction, where the reduction's own retrieval straddles the threshold. Seven are judgement calls between papers on the same data, among them WASP-17 b's and WASP-39 b's H₂O. Of the 38 unscored cells, two meet the rule for an absent label (HD 209458 b's CO₂ and WASP-52 b's NH₃), and one is a judgement call. The frozen labels were kept, because changing them after the verdicts would be post hoc. Every score is also reported under the corrected set (65 labels) and under the set that also removes every judgement call (59 labels; `label_sensitivity.py`, Section 3.5).

The first three batches were not a systematic sample. They were the planets whose public reductions and free-chemistry constraints came to hand in three rounds of searching (`jwst_validation_brief.md`), and the methane planets were sought because methane had been reported. Six rocky planets with flat spectra (TOI-836 b, TOI-776 b and c, GJ 357 b, LTT 1445 A b and GJ 1132 b) were fetched and dropped because they fix no label; HAT-P-1 b, LTT 9779 b and WASP-69 b had no public reduction when searched for, and GJ 3090 b had no limit that held across its retrievals. "Right" means agreement with these published retrievals, which are themselves model-dependent.

Each step of the conversion is a stated choice with a measured consequence. JWST products are transit depths, the quantity the classifier was trained on, so no conversion of quantity was needed. Each of the 104 Tier-3 bins took the inverse-variance-weighted mean of the JWST points inside it, partial overlaps weighted by the overlap fraction. Modes were aligned through their overlaps onto a reference mode. For WASP-39 b the reference was NIRISS order 1, which gave PRISM −177 ppm, the offset Carter & May report from their joint fit. For the panel it was the mode with the most points. Modes with no overlap, such as the two G395H detectors, keep their published offset. Bins with no data were filled flat at the spectrum's measured mean and flagged, and the number of filled bins is reported with every verdict (Table 2). Fourteen of the 26 reductions have at least 40 filled bins, and eleven have no measured optical point. Per-spectrum normalisation then removed any global offset; it runs over all 104 bins, filled ones included, and the mask control below tests what that does. Two noise regimes were run on the panel: JWST precision as delivered, and Ariel Tier-1. For the latter, the payload model's per-bin σ at the planet's Tier-1 transit count was added as 500 Gaussian draws (300 for WASP-39 b) on top of the JWST noise already in the points. A detection frequency is the share of draws, averaged over the four learners, in which a learner reports the molecule; where a majority of learners is meant, the text says so. For WASP-39 b the Tier-3 transit count was also run for context. No stellar-contamination correction was applied beyond what each source paper applied. Seven hosts are M dwarfs, among them the hosts of all five methane planets, and the limitations in Section 4 state the consequence.

The procedure was validated on a spectrum whose answer is known. The benchmark's own best-fit model of WASP-39 b was binned directly onto the 104 bins, and separately sampled at the JWST wavelength grids of each mode with the per-point JWST errors and pushed through the conversion. The two differed by 22 ppm rms, 104 ppm at worst in a MIRI bin, and no verdict changed on the noise-free spectra. This validates the conversion at near-complete coverage. At the partial coverage of the panel, the control is each planet's clear twin pushed through the same coverage mask (Section 3.4). A sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations measured how much the verdict depends on the reduction. The configurations were the individual reductions of each mode, two NIRISS pipelines, fitted and fixed limb darkening, offsets on and off, model and flat fill, with and without MIRI, PRISM alone, and the bins of Na, K, SO₂ and CO masked singly and together. Roy-Perez et al. (48) showed that retrieved abundances depend on the reduction, so this dependence was measured and not assumed.

A population-level comparison, the muting census, accompanies the verdicts. For each real planet a clear H₂/He twin (a model spectrum with no clouds or haze) was rendered at the published abundances and bulk parameters (Table 2). The planet's muting is

M = s_twin / (s_real² − ε²)^½ (eq2)

where s_twin and s_real are the dispersions of the twin and the real spectrum over the measured bins and ε is the root mean square of the real per-bin errors. M is 1 for a spectrum as featured as its clear twin and larger when it is more muted. Its uncertainty comes from 2,000 resamples of the real depths with their errors. The most muted atmosphere the population can draw at those bulk parameters (the coldest allowed temperature with the highest allowed deck) says whether the real muting lies inside the simulator's prior, and the same muting was computed for every planet of POP-I against its own clear twin.

Three controls use the same twins (`panel_mechanism.py`, `panel_control.py`). In the mask control the re-implemented classifier reads the twin through the reduction's own mask, scattered 200 times with the real per-bin errors. A label passes if the classifier gets it right in at least half of the draws, so that the mask alone cannot be blamed for a miss. In the transplants one block of the real spectrum is replaced by the twin's, shifted to the real mean over the measured AIRS bins. The block is the three optical points, Ariel's NIRSpec channel, both of these, only the filled bins below 1.95 µm, only the measured bins below 1.95 µm, or the AIRS bins. In the scaled-twin test the twin is scaled about its mean so that its dispersion over the measured bins equals the real noise-corrected one. It is then read twice: with the real errors, and with those errors plus the Tier-1 noise. Because the input is normalised per spectrum, scaling alone changes only the ratio of feature to noise. These twins share the training physics, so a twin found where the real spectrum is missed cannot separate aerosols from forward-model error, unmodelled absorbers or limb asymmetry. The real spectra were also retrieved (`panel_retrieval.py`) with the sampler of Section 2.4 on each converted spectrum, the filled bins given no weight, with the Tier-1 σ in the likelihood as for the simulated spectra, with the clear model and with the haze density free. The radius stays at the catalogue value, and the depth offset is free over ±2 % because the real spectra sit up to 1.1 % from the depth at that radius.

### 2.7 The routing method: flags, remedies and how they were scored

The routing method has two flags, two remedies and a withheld verdict. It needs the bulk parameters of the Mission Candidate Sample (the "catalogue" below) and the spectrum, and no retrieval or published composition. It does assume an H₂/He scale height and a correct catalogue mass.

**The remedies.** The first remedy is the design retrained on a population extended in aerosols. Three training sets were built from POP-III. In the haze set a third of the training planets carry a column-filling Lee-Mie haze with density log-uniform over 10⁶-3 × 10⁸ m⁻³. The haze+deck set adds, on a further sixth of the planets, a grey deck at 5-500 Pa, up to two decades above the prior's 5 × 10² Pa limit. The union set adds at fit time stellar spots at 10 or 20 % coverage on a sixth of the planets and doubled or tripled noise on another sixth, drawn independently. It tests whether covering every named departure adds anything over the two aerosol departures. The designs trained on them are the haze-retrained, haze+deck-retrained and union-retrained designs, each fitted exactly as the re-implemented classifier with the same binning, noise draw and normalisation; the method uses the haze-retrained one. These sets differ from the per-departure retraining of Section 2.3, which trains on the exact departure and measures the irreducible loss, and from the randomised grid: those two are measurements, and these are remedies. The second remedy is the AIRS-only variant (Section 2.3), used only where a flag fires.

**The shape flag.** Four candidate diagnostics were chosen before the runs: (a) the input disagreement between the three variants of the design; (b) the optical-excess score; (c) band-metric consistency, the disagreement between a learner's probability and the flag from the consortium's band metric, which compares the mean depth in a molecule's bands with that in a normalisation band, in units of their dispersions (7); and (d) predicted decidability, a regressor on bulk features trained on the Δχ² of Section 2.4. For a molecule and a learner with present-probabilities p_all, p_no and p_AIRS from the three variants, the input disagreement is

D = max(|p_all − p_no|, |p_all − p_AIRS|) (eq3)

and the optical-excess score, with z the normalised spectrum, is

E = |(mean z over the three optical points − mean z over Ariel's NIRSpec channel) − μ| / s (eq4)

where μ and s are the mean and standard deviation of that difference on the clean training set. Each has a clean threshold, its 90th percentile on the clean test spectra. The shape flag fires on a cell (a molecule and a learner) when D or E exceeds its threshold. Both are used because on the real panel they fire on different reductions (Section 3.5). D is also split by level of the nested inputs, D₁ = |p_all − p_no| and D₂ = |p_no − p_AIRS|, each at its own clean threshold. This split tests whether the disagreement locates the departure in the inputs that differ between the two variants (`nested_router.py`). Each candidate was scored by selective credit as in Section 2.3 and by its area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC; 0.5 is chance) for separating the two error classes. Used as a router, it was also scored by the accuracy and recall (the share of present labels recovered) of the classifier when flagged planets are re-scored by the AIRS-only variant. The optical-excess score was designed from the haze mechanism of Section 3.3 and is evaluated on the same planets under the same haze. The real panel and the confined hazes are its only tests on data it was not built from.

**The amplitude flag.** The flag reads the mismatch the real giants showed (Section 3.4). It is the ratio

R = A₅ / (s² − ε²)^½ (eq5)

where A₅ is the five-scale-height modulation, computed from the catalogue's mass, radius and equilibrium temperature with an H₂/He mean molecular weight, as in the payload noise model (Section 2.2), and the denominator is the noise-corrected dispersion of the measured bins. The muting census of Section 2.6 is its reference: the census reads the same dispersion against a clear twin at the published composition, which a pipeline does not have. Two cuts are read from POP-I through the same bins. The upper threshold is the largest ratio of any clear POP-I planet, so a planet above it is more muted, per scale height, than any clear simulated planet read the same way. The lower cut is the first percentile of POP-I with its own decks, so a planet below it is more featured than 99 % of the population. A spectrum with no dispersion above its errors has no ratio and takes no flag. In simulation all 104 bins are measured and the thresholds are 5.25 and 0.47. A first form of the flag used full-bin thresholds, and its statistic was chosen after its values on the panel had been seen. The per-reduction rule and its sensitivities were committed to the repository before they were computed (Appendix C, rows F1-F3), but after all three batches were known, so the panel is not an independent test of the flag.

**The rule.** The amplitude flag is applied first: a planet above the upper threshold goes to the haze-retrained design, and a planet below the lower cut has its verdict withheld. On every other planet, a cell the shape flag marks takes the AIRS-only variant, and any other cell the re-implemented classifier. The verdict is the majority of the four learners.

**How the designs were scored.** Every design is scored on three tiers of evidence. In simulation: mean accuracy over the four learners and four molecules on POP-I clean and under each departure, from one evaluation of all designs on the same test cases and noise draw (`pipeline_sim.py`), a withheld label counting one half. On the real panel: all 66 labels the literature decides, with one point for a right verdict, none for a wrong one and a half for a split or withheld one, so that no design gains by leaving labels unscored. Each difference has a 95 % bootstrap interval over the 23 planets, with reductions of one planet resampled together (`pipeline_table.py`). This is done at JWST precision and at Tier-1 noise, where each of 500 draws is scored and every flag is recomputed from the noisy spectrum, with the JWST and Tier-1 noise removed from the dispersion in (eq5) (`panel_tier1.py`). The intervals are descriptive, since about twenty router variants were compared on this panel before the rule was fixed. In the selection: every design in the simulation of Section 2.8, at 20, 40, 60 and 80 % Tier-2 slots. The five existing rules are also scored as routers to the AIRS-only variant on all three tiers, added after an external reading of the draft. So is the amplitude flag with the AIRS-only variant on every other planet. A muted case carries the departure measured on real planets into simulation: every feature scaled about the spectrum's mean by a third, about the median muting of the muted giants, with the noise unscaled.

### 2.8 Retrieval fallbacks and the selection simulation

Two fallback retrievals are defined on the hazed spectra. The retrieval of Section 2.4 run unchanged on a hazed spectrum is the "clear-model retrieval", the existing method applied to a spectrum under a departure. On a clean spectrum the same retrieval is the "clean retrieval". The "free-haze retrieval" adds the haze to the forward model as an eighth free parameter, its number density log-uniform over 10³ to 10¹⁰ m⁻³. The lower bound is haze-free to below a part per million. This retrieval is the fallback a pipeline could run on any spectrum without knowing whether it is hazed. Both were run on all 954 hazed spectra.

The mission-level measure is a selection simulation. The 955 POP-I planets receive 60 % Tier-2 slots, 573 (4), ranked by the mean present-probability of the three learners that give one (KNN, MLP and RFC; the one-vs-one SVC does not). The score, "targets lost", is the number of planets carrying the molecule that are not selected, for CH₄, for H₂O and for either ("either-molecule targets"). Ranking by a molecule's probability is a proxy for a selection the mission will make by science theme and signal-to-noise (4). Strategies compared at the same budget: the classifier alone; the classifier with each existing rule (declined planets sent to the back of the queue); the classifier routed by each candidate to the AIRS-only variant; the band metric alone; and a retrieval of every planet, ranking by its present-posterior, with its cost stated. A budget variant re-scores by the retrieval only the fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect, for *f* from 10 to 70 %. Each rule is compared with a random budget (200 draws), with the measured Δχ² of Section 2.4 used as an ordering, which starts from the true parameters and is therefore not attainable, and with the retrieval's own undecidability, the unattainable best ordering. The single-departure simulations use one noise draw. The retrievals are of noise-free spectra and the classifier reads a noisy one, a difference Section 3.6 measures on the 200-planet subsample.

The mixed-population test applies the same budget where the retrieval shares the classifier's failure. Each of the 954 planets with a hazed spectrum is hazed with probability *h* (0.1, 0.2 or 0.5; 20 draws). The fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect (10 to 50 %) is re-scored by the free-haze retrieval, and the rest keep the classifier. The selection is the 60 % cut above. For comparison, the clear-model retrieval is run in the free-haze retrieval's place. The orderings are random, the five existing rules, the optical-excess score, the input disagreement, band-metric consistency, and a haze oracle that knows which planets are hazed and nothing else. The fixed strategies are the classifier alone, the optical-excess score routed to the AIRS-only variant without any retrieval, the clear-model retrieval of every planet, and that retrieval re-run as the free-haze retrieval wherever its evidence fails. The metrics are targets lost at the selection, with their spread over the draws, the pipeline's accuracy, and its recall on the hazed targets ("hazed recall") and on the clean targets. The haze is at 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³, which the optical-excess score flags on every hazed planet (Section 3.5), so the haze oracle is a haze detector at a density where detection saturates. A second point of the dose-response was run at 2 × 10⁶ m⁻³, the lowest density at which the stress test measured a large loss (12.1 points in Table 5, against 1.4 at 2 × 10⁵ m⁻³). The population was re-rendered at that density and the test repeated. The no-retrieval strategies and the detection of the hazed planets by each rule are reported. Expectations for this test were committed after a pilot of the same test on the 124 planets whose fixed-density hazed posteriors existed first, and before any population result existed (Appendix C). The clean planets a budget reaches are scored by the clean retrieval of Section 2.4, the correctly specified model for them; the free-haze retrieval of clean spectra exists so far for 27 planets.

The classifier sits 9-16 points below this ceiling even inside its simulator. The retrieval scores 92-94 % on every molecule on the noise-free spectra, 97-98 % on absent labels, where the prior already supplies 0.71, and 82-88 % on present labels (Table 4). Among the classifier's clean errors only 19-25 % fall on labels the spectrum decides; three quarters to four fifths fall on undecidable labels, and effectively none on a label the spectrum decides the other way. That split depends on the 0.95 level: the decided share is 38 % at 0.84, and at 0.5, where the posterior merely leans to the right answer, 74 %. An undecidable label is therefore unsettled, not uninformative. The retrieval is right on 81-86 % of undecidable labels, against a majority class of 53-60 %, and only 15-19 % of them lie within 0.1 of the prior's 0.29. On decidable labels the learners score 91-98 % for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and 76-93 % for H₂O; on undecidable ones 58-70 %, a few points above the majority class. Recall of present labels is 82-84 % (H₂O 65 %) where the label is decidable and 35-49 % (H₂O 27 %) where it is not. The classifier's default on an uninformative spectrum is "absent", which is the prior, and Section 3.4 finds that default on real spectra.

**Noisy realisations.** On the 200 random planets retrieved on the noisy spectrum the classifier reads, the population figures held, but individual labels moved. The decidable fractions changed by at most 6 points, but 5-11 % of labels lost decidability under the draw and 5-12 % gained it, and the correlation of p_true between the two runs was 0.67-0.79. The retrieval's accuracy on the realisation was 1.5-6 points lower. Labels with p_true below 0.05, of which the noise-free population has one in 3,820, appeared on 8 of 800. On data, a calibrated posterior is confidently wrong about 1 % of the time. Of the four learners' 590 errors on these realisations, 18.5 % are recoverable by the noise-free posterior and 14.9 % are decidable by the noisy one. The noisy retrieval gets 60 % of them right at p ≥ 0.5 (the noise-free one 73 %), and 71 % of those right answers are present labels. The recoverable share defined by the noise-free posterior is therefore an upper bound that overstates by about a fifth of itself on clean spectra; the hazed-spectrum runs on the same realisations are in Section 3.6.

**The Δχ² test used under departures.** Section 3.3 needs a test that can be rerun with a departure in the counterfactual model, so the Δχ² test of Section 2.4 was calibrated against this posterior. The two agreed in rank: Spearman correlation between Δχ² and logit p_true of 0.86 (CH₄), 0.80 (H₂O), 0.88 (CO₂) and 0.89 (NH₃) over the 955 planets. The test's scale, however, differed by an order of magnitude from the conventional one. The posterior crosses 0.95 at Δχ² = 0.45 (CH₄), 0.98 (H₂O), 0.90 (CO₂) and 0.56 (NH₃), not at 3.84, the χ² value for one parameter at 95 %, or 9, the 3σ value. The reason is that the test represents the opposite class by its single best-fitting point, whereas the retrieval integrates over that class's volume. A label is therefore decidable when the best opposite-class fit sits only about one σ away in total χ². This is a statement about the prior's volume as much as about the evidence. At the conventional 3σ the test would call 78-88 % of labels undecidable, the value the calibration replaces. The calibrated thresholds are used everywhere below. The test is a check on the retrieval and not a substitute for it: at 207 core-hours against the retrieval's 365 on this population, it is not the cheap method it was meant to be.

That is information the atmosphere removes, as noise is information the instrument removes.

### 3.4 Real JWST spectra: what the classifier finds and where it fails

**WASP-39 b.** At JWST precision with all 104 inputs, three of the four learners called H₂O present and all four called CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at 10⁻⁴. This agrees with the published composition (24, 25, 30, 76) on every label the literature decides. CO₂ at 10⁻⁵, where its published abundance of about 10⁻⁴·⁵ puts it, was present for two to three learners. NH₃ was absent in all 24 configurations of the sensitivity matrix and with each of three MIRI reductions. The consortium's own band metric agreed with the majority. The MLP called H₂O absent with a score of 0.00. It did so in 23 of the 24 configurations and on the benchmark's own best-fit model binned directly onto the 104 bins with no conversion, so the conversion did not cause the miss. The composition or a grey deck did not cause it either: the clear H₂/He twin at the published abundances is called H₂O-present by all four learners (MLP score 1.00), as are twins with grey decks at pressures down to 10 Pa. Under Ariel Tier-1 noise at WASP-39 b's one transit, H₂O is detected in 58 % of draws with all inputs and 86 % without the three optical points, and the clear twin in 100 %.

**The panel.** Over all 26 reductions the literature decides 66 labels, 40 absent and 26 present, so calling every label absent would score 40. At JWST precision the re-implemented classifier was right on 39 of the absent labels and 3 of the present ones (the H₂O of WASP-39 b, WASP-17 b and WASP-94A b). It was wrong on 17 and split on 7, for a score of 45.5 with a split counted as half. The no-optical variant scored 50.0, and the AIRS-only variant 52.5, reproducing 14 present labels. On the fifteen reductions with measured data both below 1.1 µm and above 1.95 µm the three scores are 28.5, 32.0 and 34.5 of 40. By batch they are 25.5, 28.0 and 31.5 of 40 on the first, 14.5, 16.0 and 16.0 of 19 on the second, and 5.5, 6.0 and 5.0 of 7 on the third. Most of the gain from dropping the optical points therefore lies in the first batch, which holds six of the eight methane reductions. At Tier-1 noise the three scores fall to 41.7, 43.1 and 43.4 (Table 12). The re-implemented classifier finds 3.0 of the 26 present labels on average over the draws, and its score is 1.7 labels above calling every label absent.

**What the coverage mask does.** Bins with no data are filled flat, and that alone misleads the re-implemented classifier. Of the 24 present labels with a clear twin, it misses 11 on the twin itself, read through the reduction's own mask at the real errors; the AIRS-only variant misses 4 (Section 2.6). These eleven labels fail the mask control: the H₂O of WASP-107 b, HAT-P-26 b, GJ 3470 b, WASP-166 b, WASP-80 b and WASP-94A b, the CO₂ of TOI-270 d, TOI-1231 b and WASP-166 b, and the CH₄ and CO₂ of TOI-270 d in the Holmberg reduction. No absent label fails. A miss on these labels cannot be attributed to the real atmosphere, although with full coverage the same twins of the planets with AIRS-range data only are found by all four learners. These labels stay in the count because a pipeline would not know the cause. Every design is also scored on the 50 labels that pass, 37 absent and 13 present (Table 12). Table 10 sorts the 23 present labels the classifier does not find: the twin is missed too on ten, found with the AIRS-only learner also finding the label on eight, found with that learner not finding it on three, and two have no twin. Two further cases deserve a note. GJ 9827 d's H₂O is absent with all inputs and with the AIRS-only variant, and split with the no-optical variant, although its twin through the same NIRISS mask is found in 99 % of draws. The reason is that the real spectrum shows no dispersion above its errors in 63 % of resamples. HIP 67522 b's CO₂ is called present by every learner, every input and every training set, for a reason the muting census gives below. No NH₃ false positive occurred on any panel planet with all inputs. With the AIRS-only variant, WASP-52 b's NH₃ is called present where its retrieval reports no evidence but sets no limit, so the cell is ambiguous and not scored.

**The methane planets.** With all inputs the classifier called CH₄ absent on seven of the eight reductions of TOI-270 d, K2-18 b, TOI-1231 b, GJ 1214 b and TOI-732 c, and split on the eighth (Table 8). Under Tier-1 noise the detection frequencies are 4-22 %, and a majority of learners reports methane in at most 2 % of draws. The published methane detections are at 4.2-10.8σ on TOI-270 d and K2-18 b (31, 32, 34, 35, 77), with a log Bayes factor of 55-70 on TOI-1231 b (36), and at or below about 3σ on the last two (83, 84). Counted per planet, by majority over reductions, the classifier missed four and split on TOI-1231 b. The AIRS-only variant found CH₄ on six of eight reductions and on four of the five planets. It missed GJ 1214 b, whose detection is the weakest of the set and whose retrieved atmosphere is CO₂-dominated, a composition the H₂/He grid does not contain. The eight reductions are five planets, so the comparison has an effective sample of five. It is reported as a pattern, not a rate.

Two tests bear on these misses. The clear twins, through the same mask at the real per-bin errors, are found by a majority of learners in 63-100 % of draws on six of the seven reductions with a twin. Those misses are therefore not caused by the noise or by the classifier's skill inside its simulator. The exception is the Holmberg reduction of TOI-270 d, with 63 filled bins, whose twin is never found, so the coverage causes its miss. The transplants show how strongly the verdict depends on the inputs below 1.95 µm, and they do not locate a methane signal there. Replacing every real bin below 1.95 µm with the twin's brings all seven reductions to a majority of learners, and replacing the AIRS bins brings three. But the same transplant also brings 10 of 37 absent labels to a majority and splits seven more, where the real spectra put one at a majority. A methane-free twin's bins still restore methane on three of the seven, and the optical points alone bring 14 absent labels to a majority. These bins therefore gate the all-input design's verdicts in general. Removing the optical points recovers methane on none of the eight reductions, no methane reduction has all three of them measured, two have none, and the optical-excess score fires on two. **Muting.** Twelve giants and Neptune-mass planets on the panel have a full published abundance set and a usable catalogue mass ("the twelve giants" below). They are HAT-P-26 b, WASP-107 b, WASP-39 b, GJ 3470 b and WASP-166 b from the first batch; WASP-17 b, WASP-80 b, HD 209458 b, WASP-96 b and WASP-52 b from the second; WASP-94A b and HAT-P-12 b from the third. HD 189733 b, HAT-P-18 b and HIP 67522 b, which do not meet that test, appear in Figure 4 and do not change the count. Ten of the twelve are muted by a factor of 2.3 to 5.3 against their clear twins (Figure 4; 68 % intervals 0.1-1.2 wide). In every resample they lie 1.7 to 2.7 times beyond the most muted H₂/He atmosphere the population allows at their mass, radius, irradiation and published composition. The same holds over the AIRS bins alone, so the muting is in the molecular bands and not only in a haze slope. The statement depends on the published composition. At the same bulk parameters with a deck at 5 × 10² Pa, H₂O at 10⁻⁴ and the other gases at 10⁻⁷, the population reaches the real dispersion on nine of the ten, and at H₂O 10⁻⁵ on all ten. Within the prior, amplitude is degenerate with abundance: a muted real spectrum looks like a cloudy low-abundance atmosphere, so "absent" is what the prior would answer. WASP-39 b is the worked example. Its real dispersion over the measured bins is 324 ppm against 1,190 ppm for the clear twin, a muting of 3.7 (68 % interval 3.67-3.79). The most muted atmosphere the population can draw at its bulk parameters reaches 2.0. Yet it is inside the training distribution by every distance rule (k-NN distance 6.2 against the decline threshold 11.25), because those rules work on normalised spectra and normalisation removes amplitude. The two giants inside the prior are the two least muted, WASP-96 b (1.5, 1.4-1.6; the clearest water feature of the set, 88) and WASP-52 b (2.3, 2.2-2.4, against a prior maximum of 2.4).

The population's own clouds mute its median planet by 1 %, its 99th percentile by a factor of 2.5 and its most muted planet by 6.3, at the prior's 5 × 10² Pa limit (Figure 4b). The simulator can therefore reach the giants' muting, but in about one planet in a hundred or fewer (0.1-1.2 %) and only near that limit, whereas ten of the twelve giants sit there. Reaching it in numbers needs what the population does not contain: metallicities of tens to hundreds of times solar, hazes or patchy clouds, which is what their JWST retrievals report. The four H₂-dominated methane planets are muted by only 0.7-2.0, inside the population's range, although three of their seven reductions would need a deck above the prior's limit at their own bulk parameters (Table 8). The giants' real spectra carry smaller features, relative to their continua, than the clear-grid training spectra, and the classifier's verdict on real spectra is mostly "absent", which is also its default on an uninformative simulated spectrum (Section 3.2).

The scaled-twin test separates scale from shape. Normalisation removes pure scaling, so at JWST precision a twin scaled to the real dispersion is found as often as the unscaled twin. On the 23 present labels whose real spectrum has measurable dispersion, the counts are 11 against 12 with all inputs, and 19 against 19 with the AIRS-only variant. Scale is therefore not what the learners miss at that precision; the real spectra differ from their twins in shape as well as in size. At Tier-1 noise scale does cost labels, because the noise does not scale with the features: 4 against 8 of the 23 with all inputs, and 5 against 14 with the AIRS-only variant. Uniform muting therefore lowers what Tier-1 can find on a real planet, whatever the input, while the shape differences cost labels at JWST precision, five of which aerosol training recovers (Section 3.5).

The third batch showed the opposite case. HIP 67522 b is a 17 Myr Jupiter-radius planet of 14 Earth masses (93). Its scale height is over ten times what the catalogue's mass gives the simulator, so a trace abundance produces a feature the classifier reads as a 10⁻⁴ one. It is nine times more featured than its clear twin at the catalogue's parameters, and its CO₂ is called present although the published abundance is 10⁻⁵·⁶.

### 3.5 A routing method

The misses of Section 3.4 that another input or another training population can find are recoverable in that operational sense, and the method addresses only those. A spectrum that does not decide a label stays undecided: the method neither recognises nor withholds such labels, and Section 3.6 measures what recognising them is worth. The method's rule is stated in Section 2.7. Its elements are measured one at a time below, then together on the real panel (Table 12), in simulation (Table 13) and in the selection (Table 14).

**The remedies.** The muting census places eleven reductions beyond the prior, ten of them in more than 95 % of resamples. Sent only to these eleven, the haze+deck-retrained design changes five scored labels from split or absent to present: the CO₂ of HAT-P-26 b and GJ 3470 b, the H₂O and CO₂ of WASP-166 b and the H₂O of WASP-80 b. On those planets' 30 scored labels the score rises from 23.0 (21 right, 5 wrong, 4 split) to 26.5 (26, 3, 1), and nothing else that is scored changes. The haze-retrained design also calls HAT-P-12 b's water present (27.0; 27, 3, 0). Among the methane reductions (Table 11), three would need a high deck at the twin's temperature (K2-18 b in both reanalyses and TOI-1231 b, decks at 47-238 Pa). These are the three on which retraining moved the verdict, two to present and one to split under each training set. Retraining did not move the four that need no such deck, apart from a split on the Madhusudhan K2-18 b reduction under the haze+deck set. It also did not move GJ 1214 b, which has no twin to place. Used on every planet, the haze-retrained design raises the re-implemented classifier's panel score from 45.5 of 66 to 50.5, and the haze+deck-retrained design to 52.0. The AIRS-only variant finds six of the eight methane reductions with no new training population, so it remains the cheaper remedy for those planets.

In simulation, the haze set recovers two thirds of the CH₄ loss under the column-filling haze (66 → 79 % against 85 % clean), three quarters of the CO₂ loss, half of the NH₃ loss and two fifths of the H₂O loss. It leaves the noise × 3 column unchanged and costs 1-2 points on clean spectra. Under the confined hazes, which it did not see, it recovers 54, 48 and 38 % of the re-implemented classifier's loss, and the haze+deck set 61, 59 and 46 %. Covering further departures adds nothing: the union-retrained design is within 0.3 points of the haze+deck-retrained one on every simulated case and scores the same 52.0 on the panel. The per-spectrum normalisation largely removes the multiplicative spot factor, and the noise is irreducible at fixed precision. Classed by the clean-spectrum posterior, retraining's gain is the removal of recoverable errors down to the undecidable floor. Under the column-filling haze, the haze+deck-retrained design removes 315 of the re-implemented classifier's 675 recoverable errors (mean over the four learners, summed over molecules) and 71 of its 719 undecidable ones. On clean spectra it adds 31 recoverable errors, and under twofold noise it moves nothing. The undecidable count stays at 560-730 in every case for both designs, which is the floor Section 3.2 predicts.

**The shape flag in simulation.** The two scores fire on different departures (Table D2). The optical-excess score flags every planet under the column-filling haze, 76-97 % under the confined hazes and two thirds of the spotted ones at its 10 % clean rate, and almost no clouded or noisier ones. The input disagreement fires on 9 % of clean planets. Split by level of the nested inputs, it locates the departure. D₁ rises most under haze, and D₂ rises where the haze slope reaches Ariel's NIRSpec channel, as for CO₂ and NH₃ under the compound departure (33 and 31 %). Under the high deck and twofold noise neither leaves its clean rate. That localisation does not improve accuracy. A three-level router that sends a cell to the largest input set the two levels agree on costs 5.1 points on clean spectra against 4.6 for the shape flag and is within a point of it under every departure. Routing on D₁ alone, to the no-optical variant, halves the clean cost (2.4 points) and is the best router at the weaker density (76.2 %) but the worst under the compound departure (69.2 against 73.5 %). The input set to fall back on therefore depends on how far the departure reaches.

**The shape flag on the panel.** The two scores flag different misses (Table 11). The optical-excess score does not depend on the molecule. It flags HAT-P-18 b (the spotted host) and two of the three K2-18 b reductions; routed by it alone, the classifier recovers methane on those two reductions and scores 47.5 of 66, against 45.5. The input disagreement fires on the CH₄ cell on five of the eight reductions; routed by it alone, the classifier recovers methane on four and scores 50.5. The shape flag uses both. It recovers methane on five of the eight reductions (a sixth becomes split; the Felix reduction of TOI-270 d and GJ 1214 b stay absent) and scores 51.5. Five labels go from wrong to right and none the other way. Two get worse (HD 189733 b's CO₂, absent to split, and HAT-P-12 b's H₂O, split to absent). Three of the improvements lie outside the methane set (GJ 3470 b's CO₂, WASP-166 b's CO₂ and WASP-96 b's H₂O). On the second batch, which the shape flag had not seen, it routed TOI-732 c's methane from absent to present and WASP-96 b's water from split to present. No label got worse, and the batch rose from 14.5 to 16.0 of 19. On the third it lost HAT-P-12 b's water, 5.5 to 5.0 of 7.

**The amplitude flag.** Read through each reduction's own coverage (Table D1), the ratio exceeds its threshold on twelve of the 26 reductions. Eight of them are among the ten giants the muting census places beyond the prior; the other two giants, HAT-P-26 b and HD 209458 b, fall below their thresholds. The other four are HAT-P-18 b, the census's marginal case (beyond the prior in 82 % of resamples); TOI-1231 b, just above at 5.44 against 5.09; and L 98-59 d and GJ 1214 b. These last two have no clear twin, and their flat spectra (ratios 11 and 63) reflect mean molecular weights far above the H₂/He the flag assumes. HIP 67522 b, at 0.32, lies below its lower cut. The flag therefore tracks the census without being the same measurement. The panel score depends little on its form: with the clear 99th percentile as the threshold (14 reductions flagged) it is 54.0, with the own-deck 99th percentile or the AIRS range alone (6 flagged, four of them giants) 53.0, and with the first full-bin form (17 flagged) 53.5. That also means the panel is a weak test of the form. At Tier-1 noise the flag is itself noise-limited: it fires in more than half of the draws on only six of the twelve (WASP-39 b, WASP-107 b, WASP-17 b, WASP-94A b, HAT-P-12 b and HAT-P-18 b). In simulation the flag changes little. At Tier-1 noise it fires on 6.8 % of clean planets, and 7.9 % show no dispersion above their noise and take no flag. Accuracy moves by 0.1 points, because every simulated departure stays inside the amplitude range the training decks span. Its lower cut has a false alarm: under threefold noise beyond the nominal, the excess dispersion reads as amplitude and a quarter of the planets are withheld.

**The method on the panel.** At JWST precision the method scores 54.0 of 66: 52 right, 10 wrong and 4 split or withheld, with methane on five of the eight reductions (Table 12). Against the re-implemented classifier the difference is 8.5 labels (95 % bootstrap interval over planets, 2.8 to 14.0). Six labels go from wrong to right and none from right to wrong. They are the methane of K2-18 b in two reductions, of TOI-270 d in the Holmberg reduction and of TOI-732 c, and the H₂O and CO₂ of WASP-166 b. Five go from split to right. Two go from wrong to half credit (K2-18 b's CH₄ in the Hu reduction, absent to split, and HIP 67522 b's CO₂, present to withheld). Two get worse (HD 189733 b's CO₂, absent to split, and GJ 1214 b's CO₂, split to absent). The muting census in the flag's place, with the same haze-retrained design and HIP 67522 b withheld, scores 55.0, so the flag gives up one label to a measurement that needs the published composition. The order of the two flags matters on at least three planets. With the shape flag first, the water of WASP-166 b and HAT-P-12 b is called absent and that of WASP-80 b split. With the amplitude flag first, all three are called present. HAT-P-12 b is the most muted of the panel. The input disagreement fires on its water, and the AIRS-only variant, which does not read the 1.4 µm band, turns a split into an absent, whereas the haze-retrained design calls the water present. Without any amplitude flag, the haze+deck-retrained design with the shape flag routed to its own AIRS-only variant scores 51.5 and finds methane on six of eight. It is wrong on 14 labels, among them HD 189733 b's CO₂ called present, GJ 1214 b's CO₂ called absent and HAT-P-12 b's H₂O called absent.

**What the gain consists of.** Of the 8.5 labels, 3.5 lie on labels failing the mask control and 5.5 on the 50 that pass (46.0 against 40.5; interval 1.1 to 9.4), and the labels with no twin lose 0.5. On the fifteen reductions with measured data below 1.1 µm and above 1.95 µm the gain is 7.0 of 40 (2.4 to 11.3). About two fifths of the gain therefore lies on labels the mask itself hides, which Ariel's full coverage would not hide, and the rest survives the control. Nor does the gain rest on doubtful labels. With the three labels that depart from the rule removed and the two missed ones added (65 labels), the method scores 52.0 against 44.5, a gain of 7.5 (1.0 to 13.8). With every judgement call also removed (59 labels) it scores 46.0 against 38.5, the same 7.5 (1.1 to 13.8). All of these are at JWST precision. At Tier-1 noise the method scores 44.4 of 66 against 41.7, a gain of 2.7 (1.2 to 4.3). A majority of learners finds methane on about one of the eight reductions (1.1 on average over the draws) against none. The gain shrinks by two thirds because every design misses most present labels at that noise: of the 26 present labels the designs find 3.0 to 5.8. Under the two corrected label sets the Tier-1 gains are 2.5 (0.9 to 4.3) and 2.2 (0.7 to 3.9).

**Against the existing rules.** Used as routers to the AIRS-only variant, the five existing rules score 48.5-49.5 of 66 at JWST precision, against 51.5 for the shape flag and 54.0 for the method, and 41.9-43.0 at Tier-1 noise, against 43.3 and 44.4 (Table 12). Their simulated accuracy is higher on clean spectra because they rarely fire there, and lower under most departures (Table 13). Behind the amplitude flag, in the shape flag's place, the distance rules score 53.0 and the confidence rules 50.5. The amplitude flag with the AIRS-only variant on every other planet scores 55.0 and 44.6, above the rule. It costs the AIRS-only variant's 7.4 points on clean spectra, and the shape flag's part in the method is to keep the other inputs where nothing is flagged.

**Against the simpler fixes.** The method scored higher than both simpler fixes on its panel, and the lead survives removing any one planet. The lead is small and partly reflects choices made after those planets were seen. At JWST precision the AIRS-only variant used everywhere scores 52.5, a difference of 1.5 (−1.5 to 5.7), and the haze+deck-retrained design used everywhere 52.0, a difference of 2.0 (−2.2 to 5.9). At Tier-1 noise the differences are 1.0 (−0.2 to 2.8) and 0.9 (−0.4 to 2.6). The method and the AIRS-only variant differ on 7 labels, 4 in the method's favour and 3 against, and the 1.5 equals the gains on HAT-P-12 b's water and HIP 67522 b's CO₂, which come from the order of the flags and the lower cut. In simulation the method is 2.6 points above the AIRS-only variant on clean spectra and 1.5 under twofold noise, and 0.4-1.1 below it under each haze, spots and the high deck. It is below the haze+deck-retrained design in every column. On methane at JWST precision the order is AIRS-only six, the method five, the retrained design two. What the method buys over dropping the optical points everywhere is the use of those points wherever they are not flagged, and it also names a failure for each planet, which the fixes do not.

**A muted population.** The departure measured on the real giants can be carried into selection. Because the input is normalised, scaling every feature by 1/M with the noise unscaled is the same, for a fixed design, as noise at M times σ. A population muted by three (Tables 13 and 14) costs the re-implemented classifier 56 CH₄ and 97 H₂O targets, against 20 and 51 on clean spectra and 38 and 78 under the column-filling haze. No design repairs it: all lose 54-61 CH₄ and 93-114 H₂O targets, and accuracy stays at 65-68 %, because the information has gone below the noise. This bounds the cost, since only the giants measured here are muted so far. It says that the departure seen on real planets would cost more targets than the simulated haze, and that retraining or routing would not recover them.

**Simulated robustness against real reliability.** Across the seven designs without existing rules, the ranking by simulated accuracy on clean spectra runs opposite to the ranking on the real panel at JWST precision (Spearman −0.71), the ranking under haze runs with it (+0.58), the ranking under twofold noise against it (−0.50), and the other simulated cases say little (at most 0.14). With seven designs these are descriptive, and they suggest that the clean benchmark would have chosen the wrong design.

**What the later batches show.** Most of the gain over the re-implemented classifier lies in the first batch, on which the flags were built and which holds six of the eight methane reductions (31.5 against 25.5 of 40). The second batch gives 16.0 against 14.5 of 19, and the third 6.5 against 5.5 of 7. Only the shape flag, the retrained designs and the census routing were fixed before a later batch (Table C3). On the second batch the shape flag gains 1.5 of 19 and the haze+deck-retrained design 2.0; on the third the shape flag loses 0.5 of 7 and the census routing changes nothing. The method's gain of one label on the third batch (HIP 67522 b withheld and HAT-P-12 b's water) comes from choices made after those planets were seen. With HIP 67522 b's measured mass of 13.8 Earth masses the ratio would be about 4.3, inside the cuts, no flag would fire, the false CO₂ would stand, and the score would be 53.5.

**The independent test.** Frozen and scored once on the 12 new planets, the method scores 16.0 of 24 at JWST precision, the same as the re-implemented classifier and as calling every label absent, and 15.9 against 16.2 at Tier-1 noise (Table 15). The gain on the development panel did not carry over. The interval on the difference, −3.5 to 4.3, is wide enough to hold no gain and the panel's rate, which would be about three labels on this batch, so the test gives no support for a gain without ruling one out. The re-implemented classifier called all 24 labels absent. The method finds 2.5 of the 8 present labels against none, and loses 2.5 of the 16 absent ones: it calls CH₄ present on HATS-6 b, where the published abundance of 10⁻⁵·⁴ is a detection below the threshold, and H₂O present on TOI-5205 b, whose spectrum the source paper finds heavily contaminated by its star, and it splits WASP-121 b's CO₂. Those verdicts are errors under the label rule, fixed before the run. The methane pattern does repeat. All three methane labels are called absent with all inputs; the AIRS-only variant finds two and splits the third; the method finds TOI-6894 b's and splits the other two. Five of the 8 present labels fail the mask control, and on the 19 labels that pass, the method scores 15.5 against 16.0. Of the six expectations, the method staying within two labels of the simpler fixes was met, and a smaller Tier-1 gain only trivially; a positive gain and at most one label from right to wrong were not met, the amplitude flag fired on two of the nine new giants rather than half, and the methane expectation was met in part (Appendix C). The evidence therefore supports a method whose gain on the panel it was built on did not repeat on new planets, and a methane recovery by the AIRS-only input that did.

### 3.6 The retrieval as fallback, against the existing methods

Retrieval is the method the classifier was proposed to replace, so it is the natural fallback for everything above. Section 3.4 showed that under the training prior it reads real spectra as absent. This section measures in simulation whether it shares the failure under haze, what repairs it, and what a flag buys at a fixed retrieval budget. The routing method needs no retrieval and is not part of these budgets. Unless stated, the haze here is the column-filling one.

**The clear-model retrieval under haze.** The clear-model retrieval shares the classifier's failure on hazed spectra and fails more confidently (Table 16). It did not complete on 18 of the 954 planets, against none of 955 on clean spectra, and on the rest it called almost every present label absent. Recall of present labels fell below 1 % for all four molecules, the posterior of the true class was below 0.05 on 99 % of present labels, and every abundance was driven 3.5-4 dex toward the prior floor. The misfit is visible in the Bayesian evidence Z, whose median log Z fell from −12 on clean spectra to −252. An analyst looking at the fit would see it, but a pipeline that reads only the posterior would not; it is the aerosol misspecification mode known from retrievals of real spectra (16, 108, 109). Used as a gate, the evidence catches nearly every hazed planet: 99.6 % of them fall below the first percentile of the clean evidences. The departure is largest where the classifier's failure was: 18σ per bin in the three optical points, 4.2σ over Ariel's NIRSpec channel and 1.9σ over AIRS (medians over the population, with the offset removed).

The free-haze retrieval, with the haze density as one free parameter, completed on every spectrum and recovered the density on every planet. It recovered 222 of the 224 planets with CH₄ present and decidable on the clean spectrum and kept 75 % of them decidable, which is the price of fitting the departure's strength rather than knowing it. Its accuracy stayed within 5 points of the clean retrieval on every molecule, and its evidence stayed near the clean value. This repair is a best case, since the truth lies inside the one-parameter family: particle size, extinction law and vertical extent are all the truth's. Four planets, among them the super-puff Kepler-79 d, fit badly with the radius held fixed and still carried the right labels but one. Figure 3c shows the two retrievals side by side: the haze removes nothing the labels need, and the clear-model retrieval's near-zero recall on the same spectra comes from the model.

**A repair from the wrong family.** The same hazed spectra were also retrieved with a power-law aerosol opacity with two free parameters: its log cross-section at 1 µm, over −34 to −24, and its slope, over 0 to 10. This family does not contain the Lee-Mie truth (`powerlaw_summary.py`). The run covers 117 of a fixed random 200 planets. It was stopped there to free the machine for other checks, the accuracies having moved by at most 4 points since 90 planets. Its accuracy of 84, 73, 84 and 78 % for CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃ lies between the clear-model retrieval (66, 54, 65 and 56 %) and the free-haze retrieval (98, 83, 96 and 93 %) on the same planets. Its recall of present labels is 84, 60, 68 and 86 %, against none for the clear-model retrieval, and it finds all 26 of these planets with CH₄ present and decidable on the clean spectrum. Labels with p_true below 0.05 fall to 3-8 %, against 32-44 % for the clear-model retrieval and at most 1 % for the free-haze retrieval. The fit shows its wrong family: the slope sits above 9 on 97 % of planets, near the prior's limit, and the median log Z is −31, against −16 for the free-haze retrieval and −264 for the clear-model retrieval. Most of the clear-model retrieval's failure therefore comes from having no aerosol at all, not from having the wrong one, although the wrong one costs 10-15 points of accuracy.

Both results hold on the classifier's own noisy realisation of the 200-planet subsample (Section 2.4). The free-haze retrieval moved as the clean retrieval had. Decidable fractions changed by 0-4 points, 7-10 % of labels lost decidability and 8-11 % gained it, accuracy fell by 3.5-6.5 points, and confidently wrong labels rose from 2 to 12 of 800. Of the classifier's 1,177 errors on that realisation it got 72 % right, against 79 % for the noise-free run. The repair therefore holds on the realisation a pipeline would see and not only on the noise-free spectrum. The clear-model retrieval did not move. Every present label stayed absent with p_true below 10⁻³ on all 196 planets that completed, no label changed decidability, and the posterior of the true class was the same between the two runs to three decimals. The evidence fell a further 47 in median log Z as the fit absorbed noise it could not explain. The noise draw moves the labels of a retrieval whose model is right by a few points and leaves those of a retrieval whose model is wrong where the model put them.

**A retrieval budget on clean spectra.** On clean spectra the retrieval is the selection method, and no cheap rule allocates a partial budget much better than chance. At 60 % Tier-2 slots, ranking by the retrieval's present-posterior loses 0 of 359 CH₄-bearing targets (a truth oracle, which ranks by the true labels, 0; classifier 20), 2 of 364 H₂O targets (truth oracle 0; classifier 51) and 39 either-molecule targets (truth oracle 3; classifier 95). On noise-free spectra under its own forward model a correctly specified retrieval is the Bayes-optimal selector, so this result is expected and serves as the reference. A retrieval of every planet took 365 core-hours for this seven-parameter model matched to the generator, and takes more for richer physics. For H₂O the comparison does not rest on the noise-free spectrum. On the 200-planet subsample at 60 % slots, the retrieval of the classifier's noisy realisation loses 1 of 74 CH₄ targets and 2 of 80 H₂O targets, the noise-free retrieval 0 and 1, and the classifier 1 and 12. When only the fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect is re-scored by the retrieval (Table C2), the k-NN distance and Mahalanobis rules beat a random budget by 2-4 CH₄ targets at *f* = 20-30 %, between one and a half and three times the random draw's standard deviation. No other rule does. The confidence rules are no better than random at any *f*. For H₂O only predicted decidability beats random, by 4-5 targets at *f* = 20-30 %, and every other rule is within noise or worse. The confidence rules send the retrieval to the planets the classifier is unsure about, which are the undecidable labels, where the retrieval cannot help either. The retrieval pays off on the planets the classifier is confidently wrong about, and no rule finds those.

**Recognising undecidable labels.** Recognising these labels is worth a withheld verdict, not a retrieval. Predicting decidability from bulk features reached an AUROC of only 0.61-0.66 for undecidable labels (0.47-0.63 for separating the error classes). The classifier's own margin and ensemble spread recognise the undecidable labels with AUROC 0.71-0.77 for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and 0.59-0.61 for H₂O (the distance rules reach 0.68 for H₂O). A random forest trained out of fold on the spectrum and bulk features, with the retrieval's labels as targets, reaches 0.69-0.74: it ties them on CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and beats them on H₂O (0.74 against 0.59-0.68). The ceiling of any such recogniser is the measured Δχ² ordering, which at a 20 % budget loses 8 CH₄ targets against 12 for a random ordering and 7 for the retrieval's own posterior. Knowing which labels are undecidable is therefore worth about four targets in 359, because at 60 % slots few targets are lost. Declining the 30 % of planets the margin ranks least confident raises the CH₄ accuracy of the mean KNN, MLP and RFC probability on the rest from 86.4 to 94.6 %. This is where recognising undecidable labels pays. The planets with undecidable labels also do not all fit the slots. For CH₄, the 224 planets decided present and the 310 with undecidable labels fit the 573 Tier-2 slots, and ranking by the posterior loses no target. For H₂O they number 156 and 518, 101 more than the slots, and for either molecule 809, 236 more.

**The existing rules under the shift.** Nor do the five rules recognise the haze failure (Table D3). Every rule's selective credit was negative under haze (−17.5 to −25.7 points), spots and the high deck. The margin rule declined 7.4 % of clean planets at the threshold set for 10 % but 0.9 % of hazed ones. The three distance rules separated hazed from clean spectra (AUROC 0.90-0.94) but did not rank errors within them (0.44-0.52), so they act as a population alarm and not as a filter. Conformal coverage fell to 73.7 % under haze. No candidate had positive selective credit under haze or spots either (the best, the input disagreement under haze, −6.8, and the optical-excess score under spots, −4.0). The optical-excess score flags every hazed planet, and declining what it flags empties the hazed population, so selective credit cannot measure a shift flag (a score that detects a departure from the training population). Confidence does not detect failure under a shift, and the shift flags that fire do not say which answers are wrong.

**A budget routed by a shift flag.** Routed to a retrieval that models the departure, a shift flag allocates a 20 % budget within noise of a haze oracle. Routed to the clear-model retrieval it loses more targets than no retrieval (Table 17; Figure 5). With a fifth of the 954 planets hazed, the classifier alone loses 29 of 359 CH₄ targets and finds 3 % of the hazed ones, and a clear-model retrieval of every planet, read by its posterior, loses 69 and finds none. That loss is CH₄'s: for H₂O it loses 70 against the classifier's 73, for either molecule 115 against 124, and with a tenth of the planets hazed it beats the classifier on H₂O (35 against 65) and either molecule (65 against 114) while still losing more CH₄ (33 against 24). Re-scoring the 20 % of planets the optical-excess score ranks most suspect by the free-haze retrieval loses 13 and finds 80 % of the hazed targets, the same within the draw-to-draw noise as an oracle that knows which planets are hazed. A random budget loses 20, and the margin and ensemble rules 20-22, because those rules hold none of the hazed planets: the classifier is confident on them. The 16 targets between the flagged and the unflagged pipeline are 4 % of the CH₄ targets and 3 % of the Tier-2 queue. The distance rules used as routers are also within noise of the haze oracle in targets lost, because they also detect the shift, although less selectively. The top 20 % by the optical-excess score holds 98 % of the hazed and 1 % of the clean planets, and the same share by the Mahalanobis rule holds 73 and 7 %. The score's advantage over them is hazed recall and selectivity, not targets at this budget. Routing the same 20 % to the clear-model retrieval loses 71 CH₄ targets against 29 with no retrieval, 94 H₂O targets against 73 and 141 either-molecule targets against 124, so a flag that correctly detects the shift makes selection worse when its destination is the clear-model retrieval read by its posterior, although that retrieval's evidence flags 99.6 % of those spectra. For H₂O the optical-excess ordering loses 40 against 73 for the classifier alone and 40 for the oracle. At a hazed fraction of 10 % it again matches the oracle, and at 50 % a 20 % budget cannot cover the hazed half, and it reaches the oracle within noise (last two columns of Table 17).

A clear-model retrieval of every planet, re-run as the free-haze retrieval wherever its evidence fails, loses no CH₄ target and two H₂O targets at every hazed fraction, at about 520 core-hours against 127 for the budget. The limit on the routed pipeline is therefore the budget, not the fallback, and with a budget for every planet the evidence gate takes the place of the flag. Of the expectations committed after the pilot (Table C1), three were met in full. The other two were met in part. Two of the three distance rules fell more than 20 points behind the optical-excess score in hazed recall, where the standard deviation of a difference is about 6 points. The score's selectivity was 98 % of hazed planets against the 99 % expected. The fifth expectation holds on the hazed planets, the clean ones being scored by the clean retrieval.

## 4. Discussion

**What the two classes mean for Tier-2 selection.** Under the training prior, a third to a half of the Tier-1 labels are not decided by the spectrum at the 0.95 level, and about four fifths of the classifier's errors in its own simulator sit there. A retrieval of such a spectrum gives an unsettled posterior, so a pipeline that reads either answer as a verdict dismisses planets the data did not settle. The undecidable fraction depends on the population, the precision and the forward model, not on the learner. For water it is half the planets at Tier-1 precision under this prior, which can be read alongside the retrieval-based detection fractions of Bocchieri et al. (50) and the tier reassessment of Radica et al. (5). About a third of it is labels within half a decade of 10⁻⁴, a line set by the label definition and not by the physics.

The measurements point to specific steps for a pipeline. On clean spectra a retrieval of every planet loses almost no target at 60 % slots, at 365 core-hours with this model, and no cheap rule allocates a partial budget much better than chance. Where the retrieval's forward model is wrong in the way haze makes it wrong, the retrieval fails more confidently than the classifier, and its evidence, not its posterior, gives the warning. For the planets a shift flag marks, the destination decides the outcome. For the labels the spectra do not decide, the classifier's own confidence marks some of them (AUROC 0.59-0.77), and no flag separates the two classes of error better than the ensemble spread, except for H₂O, where a flag trained on the retrieval does better, and allocating a retrieval budget to them does not help. Ranked by the posterior, those planets follow the decided-present ones: for methane the two groups fit the 60 % slots, and for water and for either molecule they do not (Section 3.6). The absolute stakes of a flag are modest in this simulation, 16 of 359 methane targets at a 20 % budget, 3 % of the Tier-2 queue.

**Real spectra against the training prior.** The re-implemented classifier is right on 39 of 40 absent labels and finds few present ones, so at Tier-1 noise it scores 1.7 labels above calling every label absent, and on the new planets it called every label absent. Coverage explains part of this: ten of the missed present labels are missed on the clear twin as well, which says nothing about Ariel. The learners' dependence on the inputs below 1.95 µm explains another part: the AIRS-only learner finds methane the all-input learner misses, on the development panel and again on new planets, at the price of some false positives. The largest part may be the prior. The real giants are muted beyond what the training prior allows at their published compositions but not at lower abundances, and a retrieval under that prior at Tier-1 precision reads them, and the methane planets, as absent. The classifier and the retrieval agree because they share the prior, and that shared prior is what real Tier-1 spectra expose. Precision then limits what any input can do. Muting by M lowers the Δχ² of (eq1) by about M² if it scales the features uniformly. Because these planets are bright, their median Δχ² is 15.9 against 1.45 for the population, and a uniform muting leaves about 43 % of their labels undecidable, near the population's 39 %. A training population for real Tier-1 spectra needs the aerosols, metallicities and abundances the JWST retrievals report, and the muting census is a cheap way to check, planet by planet, whether a real spectrum lies where the prior can reach at its published composition. For the sub-Neptunes with the highest mean molecular weights the clear H₂/He twin is itself an approximation, since the population contains no such atmosphere. HIP 67522 b adds the mirror image: the catalogue's mass is why the lower cut fires, and with the measured mass the flag is silent and the false CO₂ stands.

**Why confidence rules fail and when a shift flag helps.** The five existing rules did not recognise the haze failure, because the classifier is confident on hazed spectra. The three distance rules, which do detect the shift, rank errors within it no better than chance. This is the pattern reported for image classifiers under dataset shift (51) in a physical setting. The selective-risk evaluation argued for by Jaeger et al. (70) makes it visible: a rule that declines the whole hazed population has no selective credit, because it accepts no hazed planet. The value of a shift flag is therefore in routing rather than declining, and the destination decides the outcome. Routed to the clear-model retrieval, a flag that correctly detects the shift loses more targets than no flag (71 against 29). Routed to a retrieval that models the departure, the optical-excess score, the Mahalanobis rule and the haze oracle lose the same number of targets within the draw-to-draw noise. The score's advantage over the distance rules is its recall and selectivity on the hazed planets, not the selection. That retrieval-budget use was demonstrated in simulation for one departure the fallback models exactly. On real data the routing without a retrieval recovered the methane planets only once the input disagreement was added to the optical-excess score, at the clean cost Section 3.5 measures.

**What the method claims.** The routing method combines known elements, domain randomisation (66) and feature amplitude in scale heights (110-112), in a way that appears to be new. Web searches on 30 October 2026 covered arXiv and the main journals, with terms combining classifier, out-of-distribution or distribution shift, routing or flag, and exoplanet transmission spectra, JWST or Ariel. They found no published method that routes a simulator-trained atmospheric classifier on real spectra by such flags. The closest work diagnoses the collapse of an amortised retrieval on real WASP-39 b spectra and repairs it with a radius latent (49). The method needs no retrieval and no published composition, and it names a failure for each planet. On the panel it was built on, it scores 8.5 labels above the re-implemented classifier at JWST precision (descriptive interval 2.8 to 14.0) and 2.7 at Tier-1 noise (1.2 to 4.3), and above each existing rule used as a router. Its limitations are these. Frozen and applied to 12 new planets it gained nothing, the plainest fact about it. It cannot be told apart on its panel from the AIRS-only variant or a retrained design. In simulation it costs 4.9 points on clean spectra and stays below the retrained designs, in accuracy and in the selection. About two fifths of its gain at JWST precision lies on labels the coverage mask hides. It does not address undecidable labels. It is therefore a method with a measured gain on development data and no demonstrated gain on new data, and the part of it that repeated is the AIRS-only input's methane recovery.

**Beyond Ariel.** The decomposition does not depend on transmission spectroscopy. Any inference system trained on a simulator and used to make decisions produces errors of two kinds: those the data could have decided and those they could not. The split can be measured by running the expensive method on the population the cheap one is tested on. The measurement gives three things a decision pipeline uses and a classifier cannot supply: the prevalence of undecidable answers under a stated prior, the share of the cheap method's errors that any method could fix, and the test of whether a proposed fallback shares the failure. In this study the fallback, applied under the departure it does not model, lost more CH₄ and H₂O targets than no fallback when a flag sent it the departed spectra. The C/O classifier of Appendix B, trained at the same binning for a different objective, was fragile where this one is robust (omitted absorbers cost it half its accuracy on carbon-rich planets) and robust where this one is fragile (haze cost it 5 points), and the same distance rules that rank errors no better than chance here ranked its errors well (AUROC 0.90-0.91). No decidability was measured for that classifier, so this shows only that one classifier's departure profile did not predict the other's. The procedure, not any one classifier's numbers, is what could transfer.

**Expectations.** Of the 45 expectations committed before their runs (Appendix C), nineteen were met as written, seventeen in part, six not met, and three were never run. The misses include WASP-39 b's water at Tier-1 precision (58 % against the 80 % expected), the undecidable fractions at the 0.95 level for three of four molecules, the selective credit of every candidate, the predicted-decidability regressor, and the method's gain on new planets. Two results were not among the expectations, and each changed the paper. The scale of the Δχ² test differed by an order of magnitude from the posterior, which the calibration was designed to catch. And the real giants lie outside the simulator population in feature amplitude at their published compositions, while inside it in every bulk parameter and, for seven of the ten, by all three distance rules, which produced the amplitude flag and the second remedy. Measurements with no prior expectation are listed in Section 2.9.

**Limitations of the identifiability measure.** Every retrieval-based number in Sections 3.2, 3.3 and 3.6 comes from the noise-free spectrum with the Tier-1 σ in the likelihood. Decidability is therefore that of a spectrum without noise, and the recoverable shares are upper bounds. The 200-planet subsample retrieved on the classifier's realisation bounds the difference on clean spectra (Section 3.2) and for the free-haze and clear-model retrievals of hazed spectra (Section 3.6). The retrieval's abundance prior is wider than the test population's, which gives absent labels a head start of 0.71. The Bayes-factor row of Table 4 removes it, and the retrieval under the population's own prior measures it at five points of prevalence (Section 3.2). The ceiling of 92-94 % includes that default. The decision scale itself is weak: the posterior crosses 0.95 at a Δχ² of only 0.45-0.98, because the prior's volume does much of the work, and a fifth of the labels lie within half a decade of the threshold. The retrieval holds the planetary radius fixed with only a small depth offset free. The radius-abundance-cloud degeneracy (113) is the dominant nuisance of absolute-abundance retrieval, so the undecidable fractions are lower bounds and the ceiling an upper bound under that model. The forward model is the training model throughout, and a retrieval with a free radius and a full aerosol model is what a consortium would run on a flagged planet. The departure-aware error classes for spots and the high deck hold the departure's strength at its true value and rest on the Δχ² test, which on clean spectra credits more errors as recoverable than the posterior does. For haze both measures were run, and the price of fitting the strength was a quarter of the decidable present CH₄ labels and 18 % of all decidable labels. The nested-sampling settings (100 live points, 20,000 calls, a median of 3,961 calls per planet) are modest beside the 10⁵-10⁸ calls of richer retrievals. They carry a Monte Carlo error near the 0.95 cut that no repeat-seed check bounds.

**Limitations of the departures and the simulations.** The haze of the retrievals and of the retrained designs fills the column at one number density up to the model's top boundary. Its size therefore depends on that boundary and on an order-of-magnitude conversion to the observed opacity scale that holds at one pressure level (Section 2.3). The classifier's failure and the flags' firing were re-measured under three confined hazes (Section 3.3), and a power-law aerosol was tried as a wrong-family repair of the column-filling haze (Section 3.6). The retrievals, the error classes and the budget were not re-measured under confined hazes. The column-filling haze at 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³ exceeds 1 % of the gas mass above about 0.05 Pa for 0.1 µm particles of density 1 g cm⁻³ at 1,000 K, so its upper layers are not physical, which is one reason for the confined hazes. The mixed-population test with the retrieval budget hazed the planets at one density and one particle size, with the fallback's forward model correct up to one parameter. At that density the optical-excess score flags every hazed planet, so matching the haze oracle there is the expected outcome. At the weaker density the optical-excess score finds only 60 % of the hazed planets, and the no-retrieval routing recovers little of the loss, which is in H₂O (Section 3.6). This study does not report the budgeted retrieval at that density, mixed particle sizes or the free-haze fallback on spotted spectra, which the score sends to it. The rules' thresholds are quantiles of the clean test scores, so their clean decline rates are fixed by construction. With thresholds set on a random half of the test population and applied to the other half (20 splits), every rule's decline rate under every departure is reproduced within 0.5 points, and the clean rates fall at 7.6-10.7 %. The single-departure selection simulation of Table 14 is one noise draw. Over ten draws its rows move by 3-5 targets and the routing's recovery under haze is about half of the added loss rather than two thirds (Section 3.5). The selection simulation ranks by a molecule's probability, a proxy for a selection the mission will make by theme and signal-to-noise. Observing costs, target priorities and follow-up opportunities are not modelled. The payload noise model was checked against published bands for AIRS only, so the size of the optical points' noise, which the mechanism of Section 3.3 depends on, is unchecked.

**Limitations of the real panel and the method.** No stellar-contamination correction was applied beyond the source papers' own. Seven panel hosts are M dwarfs, among them the hosts of all five methane planets, so a stellar contribution to their short-wavelength shape cannot be excluded. The simulated spot departure shows what 10-20 % coverage would cost, and the shape flag fires on two thirds of the spotted simulated planets. Labels for the real planets come from the literature, the ambiguous ones are not scored, and thirteen of the 104 cells carry a departure from the rule or a judgement call (Section 2.6). Coverage of the Ariel grid by JWST is partial for every panel planet. Fourteen reductions have at least 40 filled bins and eleven have no optical data. The flat fill itself misleads the re-implemented classifier (Section 3.4), and the conversion was validated only at near-complete coverage, with the mask control standing in at partial coverage. The flags' clean thresholds were set at Tier-1 noise and are applied also at JWST precision, where a spectrum carries much less noise than the flags saw in simulation. The eight methane reductions are five planets, and two of the eight labels rest on detections at or below about 3σ. The method was assembled on this panel and about twenty router variants were compared on it before the rule was fixed, so its intervals are descriptive, and half credit for a split or withheld verdict is itself a scoring choice. The muting intervals cover the depth errors only, not the reduction, the mass, the temperature or the published composition behind each twin. Fifteen of the 24 present labels with a twin have published abundances above the training ceiling of 10⁻², so those twins lie outside the prior and those labels may be out of the classifier's reach by construction. The real-panel retrievals use the training prior and a fixed radius, and they are no test of what a richer retrieval could do. The independent batch is small, its present labels number eight, five of them fail the mask control, and many of its absent labels are detections below the threshold, on which a classifier reading real features is scored as wrong. The flag's lower cut has fired on one real planet. The amplitude flag assumes an H₂/He scale height, so high-metallicity or steam atmospheres read as muted for reasons other than aerosols, and it relies on catalogue masses: the candidate sample lists HIP 67522 b at 186 Earth masses, with an uncertainty of −120/+338 and a mass flag it also carries on 13 % of its entries, an order of magnitude above the 13.8 measured (93). The Mission Candidate Sample lists several panel planets with eclipse as the preferred method, so their Tier-1 transit counts are not what the mission would schedule.

**Scope.** The results concern the design as re-implemented, on the stated simulator, under one named population, and the prevalences are limits under that population and not physical limits. A second population with equilibrium chemistry and a metallicity distribution would show how far they move with the prior. The experiments cover a selected collection of departures, and they do not show how far the classifier can be relied on outside it. The second classifier of Appendix B, built for a different objective, showed a nearly opposite departure profile, which limits any generalisation from one classifier to another.

## 5. Conclusion

Under the training prior, at the 0.95 level and on noise-free spectra, a third to a half of the molecular labels are not settled at Tier-1 precision, and about four fifths of the re-implemented classifier's clean errors fall on them, although the posterior leans to the right answer on most. Haze leaves the labels decidable when its strength is known, noise does not, and a high deck is partly each. On JWST spectra the classifier is right on almost every absent label and misses most present ones. Some of those misses are coverage. Some are found by the AIRS-only input, including methane on new planets. The rest of the picture is the prior: real giants are muted beyond it at their published compositions, and a retrieval under it at Tier-1 precision reads the real spectra as absent as well. A population muted as those giants are would cost more targets than the simulated haze, and no design tested here recovers them. The routing method proposed here reads these mismatches with two flags that need no retrieval. It gained 8.5 of 66 labels at JWST precision and 2.7 at Tier-1 noise on the panel it was built on, at 4.9 points of clean simulated accuracy, and it gained nothing when frozen and applied to 12 new planets. The clear-model retrieval shares the haze failure and fails more confidently than the classifier, so a flag's destination decides whether it helps. A selection pipeline for real Tier-1 spectra therefore needs a training prior that reaches the muted atmospheres real planets show, before any routing of its outputs.
