# Recoverable and irrecoverable errors of a simulator-trained classifier for Ariel Tier-1 spectra, with an external test on JWST observations

Owen Yang<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Henry M. Gunn High School, 780 Arastradero Rd, Palo Alto, CA 94306, USA. Tel.: +1 650 686 7813. E-mail: owenhyang@gmail.com (corresponding author)

## Abstract

Machine-learning classifiers have been proposed for sorting the roughly 1,000 planets of Ariel's Tier-1 survey by composition, and a planet they dismiss may never be observed again. Such classifiers are trained and tested on one simulator, so it is not known which of their errors could be fixed. This study separates a classifier's errors into two classes: recoverable, where the spectrum decides the label at Tier-1 precision and the classifier misread it, and irrecoverable, where the spectrum does not decide the label under the stated population, so no method reading it can. The Ariel consortium's Tier-1 molecular classifiers were re-implemented and reproduced the 48 published accuracies within 3.7 points. A nested-sampling retrieval of every one of the 955 test planets measured which labels each spectrum decides. Under the consortium's own test prior, a third to a half of the CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃ labels are undecidable, and three quarters of the classifier's clean errors fall on them. Haze leaves the labels decidable and the errors it introduces are two thirds to seven eighths recoverable; threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction and its errors are mostly not. On JWST spectra of eleven planets converted to the Ariel grid by a validated procedure, the classifier missed methane on all three methane-bearing sub-Neptunes while its AIRS-only variant found it on all three, and five warm giants are muted 3 to 5 times beyond their clear twins, outside the simulator population. A retrieval with the training forward model fails on hazed spectra more confidently than the classifier: a correct shift flag routed to it loses more Tier-2 targets than no retrieval. Routed to a retrieval with the haze density free, the same flag allocates a 20 % budget within noise of an oracle and of the existing Mahalanobis rule, recovering 80 % of the hazed methane targets against 17 % for a random budget and 3 % for confidence rules. The classifier's own confidence recognises the irrecoverable class with AUROC 0.6-0.77, a flag learned from the spectrum does no better, and even perfect recognition would recover 4 of 359 methane targets at a 20 % budget; on the real panel the flag did not fire on the methane misses.

**Keywords:** exoplanet atmospheres; Ariel; JWST; machine learning classification; identifiability; atmospheric retrieval; target selection; domain shift

## 1. Introduction

When a planet transits its star, part of the starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere, and molecules in the atmosphere absorb that light at their characteristic wavelengths. The resulting transmission spectrum shows which molecules are present. Ariel, a European Space Agency mission scheduled for launch in 2029, will measure transmission spectra of about 1,000 exoplanets between 0.5 and 7.8 µm, using three broadband photometers below 1.1 µm and three spectrometer channels (NIRSpec, AIRS-CH0 and AIRS-CH1) at longer wavelengths (1). Each target is observed first at low signal-to-noise in a Tier-1 reconnaissance survey, and a subset is re-observed at higher precision in Tier 2 (1-4). Tier-1 spectra are the basis for choosing which planets receive deeper observations; a planet dismissed at Tier 1 may never be observed again.

Reading a spectrum with an atmospheric retrieval requires 10⁵-10⁸ model evaluations per planet (5) and specialist choices of assumptions and priors (6), which makes retrievals costly as a first pass over 1,000 planets, although the whole Ariel target list has been retrieved once in simulation (67). Machine-learning classifiers, which answer in seconds once trained, are an attractive alternative. The Ariel consortium's population study of the Tier-1 survey proposed, as a preliminary assessment, four scikit-learn classifiers that flag the presence of CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃, reaching 64-89 % accuracy on simulated planets (6). Their code and trained models were not released.

Like the models developed for the Ariel Data Challenges (5) and in the wider machine-learning retrieval literature (7, 8, 19), these classifiers were trained and tested on spectra from the same simulator. Such a test shows that a classifier has learned its simulator, not how it behaves when real atmospheres differ from it, and they do: hazes and clouds mute or reshape spectra (9, 10), unocculted star spots imprint false features (11), line lists disagree (12, 13), training grids omit absorbers that real atmospheres contain, and misspecification of this kind biases simulation-based inference in general (17, 18). JWST transmission spectra of warm giants and sub-Neptunes now carry SO₂, hazes, patchy clouds and metallicities of tens to hundreds of times solar (39-45, 47-60), none of which a classifier trained on clear hydrogen-helium atmospheres with grey clouds has seen. Simulator-trained retrieval models have been checked against real Hubble and JWST spectra (7, 19, 63, 64, 69), and on WASP-39 b the retrieved abundances move by more than an order of magnitude with the data reduction alone (46); no molecule-presence classifier proposed for Ariel has been checked against a real spectrum.

A classifier's error on a spectrum is one of two things. Either the information was in the spectrum and the classifier missed it, which is a property of the classifier and can be fixed by retraining, by a different input or by a fallback method; or the information was not there at that precision under the population the spectrum is drawn from, which is a property of the instrument and the population, and no method reading that spectrum can fix it, so the mission must not act on those spectra as if they were decided. This study calls the two classes recoverable and irrecoverable. Which class an error belongs to is a question about the spectrum, not the classifier, and it is answered by asking whether an atmosphere of the opposite class fits the spectrum within its noise. The consortium study asked it for one planet, a retrieval that gave a 48 % probability of CH₄ above 10⁻⁴ at Tier-1 precision (6). Bocchieri et al. (65) then ran retrievals on about 300 simulated Tier-1 targets and defined a P-statistic, the posterior probability that a molecule is present, which is the quantity this study calls decidability, and Radica et al. (4) measured it for three benchmark planets across the tiers. Neither measured it on the test population of a classifier, against that classifier's errors, or under departures from the simulator. The classifier's own confidence cannot stand in for the measurement, because confidence does not survive a distribution shift, a result known from image classification (66) that Section 3.2 reproduces under haze.

This study answers four questions about the consortium design, in the order a mission would need them. Is the classifier right on real spectra, with the observed JWST spectra made compatible with its inputs by a justified and validated procedure (Sections 2.6 and 3.5)? How often do physically distinct compositions give indistinguishable Ariel spectra under an explicitly defined population (Sections 2.4 and 3.3)? Which of the classifier's errors are recoverable and which are not, under the simulator and under each departure from it (Sections 2.5 and 3.4)? And does a diagnostic that recognises when the classifier is unreliable improve classification or target selection against the existing methods, a retrieval of every planet and the standard decline rules (Sections 2.7 and 3.6)? The classifiers were first re-implemented and checked against all 48 published accuracies (Section 3.1) and stress-tested against the departures of Table 2, which located a confident failure under haze in the three optical photometric inputs (Section 3.2). Expectations for every result were recorded in the repository before the runs, and the outcomes, including those not met, are reported (Section 3.7, Appendix C).

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 The published classifier and its re-implementation

The re-implementation followed Sections 2.2 and 2.5 of the consortium study (6) element by element (Table 1). Isothermal atmospheres with random temperatures, abundances of CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃, and grey cloud decks were generated for every planet of the Ariel candidate list, four times for training (POP-III) and once for testing (POP-I). Counts differ slightly between the analyses below because a few spectra failed to render or to bin under one condition or another: 955 of the 965 planets have a clean test spectrum, and these are the test population everywhere below; 954 of them have a hazed one, and the mixed-population test of Section 2.7 uses those. Spectra were binned at Tier-3 resolution, scattered with Tier-1 noise and normalised per spectrum. A molecule was labelled present if its abundance exceeded 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴ or 10⁻³, and four scikit-learn classifiers at default settings (20) were trained for each molecule and threshold.

Throughout, "the published design as re-implemented" means this reading of the recipe with the deviations of Section 2.2; the original trained models were unavailable, and no claim here concerns them.

### 2.2 Forward model, noise and unavoidable deviations

Spectra were computed with TauREx 3 (22) at R ≈ 1,000 and binned by integration: isothermal atmospheres of 100 layers with constant abundances, molecular absorption and Rayleigh scattering, a grey cloud deck and, where stated, a Mie haze. They were first rendered through the MultiREx wrapper (23); the released code calls TauREx directly and reproduces them bit for bit. TauREx 3.3.2 reads the pressure grid of Exo-Transmit opacity tables as bar, whereas Exo-Transmit treats it as Pa; the pressure grid was divided by 10⁵ when the tables were loaded. The inconsistency was reported to the TauREx developers and has since been corrected in their development branch (24). With the correction, TauREx spectra of 1,804 test atmospheres agreed better with Exo-Transmit's own code (median shape correlation 0.9986, against 0.986).

Per-bin noise came from a payload model built with ExoSim 2 (25): the one-hour noise-to-signal ratio for the host's effective temperature, scaled to the host's distance and radius and to the transit duration, and divided by the square root of the number of transits. Its one free parameter was calibrated so that a signal-to-noise ratio of 7 on the five-scale-height atmospheric signal reproduced the candidate sample's Tier-3 transit counts, which the model then predicted within 0.07 dex. For the three example planets in Figure 1 of the consortium study, its AIRS noise of about 75, 430 and 230 ppm per bin matched the published noise bands in size; the photometric and NIRSpec channels were not checked against published bands, which the limitations in Section 4 note.

Three deviations could not be avoided. (i) Opacities came from Exo-Transmit's tables (12, 21); substituting ExoMol cross sections (13) for H₂O, CH₄, CO₂ and CO cost the classifier 2.9 points (Section 3.2), which bounds the effect of this choice. (ii) Collision-induced absorption was omitted; adding it changed test spectra by at most 3 ppm, against feature amplitudes of 270-1,400 ppm. (iii) Noise came from the payload model instead of ArielRad, which is not public. An earlier reading of the publication, with spectra binned to the seven Tier-1 points, the atmosphere truncated at 1 Pa and the noise set exactly at the Tier-1 requirement, missed the published accuracies by 10 points on average and by up to 38, so the comparison with the published values discriminates between readings of the recipe.

### 2.3 The departures and the stress test

For each departure (Table 2), the same test planets were re-rendered or modified, so every loss was a within-planet difference under the same noise draw. Haze followed the Lee et al. prescription (30) with particles of 0.1 µm radius filling the column, its density passed to TauREx as the Mie particle mixing ratio and quoted here as a number density at 1 mbar. Retrievals of ten observed hot Jupiters expressed haze opacity at 0.35 µm as a multiple of the H₂ Rayleigh opacity and found log₁₀ a = 2.1-5.7 (10); the densities 3 × 10⁷, 2.4 × 10⁸ and 10¹⁰ m⁻³ used here correspond to log₁₀ a ≈ 2.4-2.7, 3.3-3.6 and 4.9-5.2, placing 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³ at the weak end of the observed range. Spots used PHOENIX spectra (31) at a spot-to-photosphere temperature ratio of 0.85 (11) for every host type.

Unless stated, results are for the 10⁻⁴ threshold, averaged over the four classifiers and four molecules; "the classifier" refers to this average, and per-learner values are in the repository. Three input variants of the design were trained on the same spectra: all 104 bins (the published input), the 101 bins without the three photometric points ("no-optical"), and the AIRS bins above 1.95 µm alone ("AIRS-only"). Two transplant experiments located the haze failure: the three hazed photometric points were copied into otherwise clean spectra, and the clean points were restored in otherwise hazed spectra. Table 2 lists every level run; Table 4 reports those that matter for what follows, chosen after the runs, and the rest are in the repository. The stress test, in the manner of a corruption benchmark (15), measured the loss (clean accuracy minus accuracy under the departure), the irreducible loss (clean accuracy minus the accuracy of the design retrained with the departure present), and the loss of the design trained on a randomised grid in which each planet independently carried haze, spots and extra noise (32).

Five decline rules of the kind used for failure detection were scored alongside: the probability margin ("margin"), the spread of the four classifiers' probabilities ("ensemble"; 33), the Mahalanobis distance (34), the mean distance to the 10 nearest training spectra ("k-NN"; 35) and the principal-component reconstruction error ("PCA"). Each rule's threshold is the 90th percentile of its score on the clean test spectra themselves, so that it declines 10 % of them; a rule's clean decline rate is therefore fixed by construction, and a held-out check (thresholds set on a random half of the test population and applied to the other half, 20 splits) is reported in Section 4. A rule is credited only with the accuracy of the planets it accepts above the clean selective baseline at the same coverage (its "selective credit"; 16, 36), with split-conformal coverage (37, 38) alongside. These five rules are the "existing rules" of Section 3.6.

### 2.4 Identifiability: which labels a spectrum decides

For planet *i* with true parameters θ and molecule *X*, the label "X > 10⁻⁴" is decidable at precision σ if no atmosphere with the opposite label, drawn from the stated population, fits the noise-free spectrum within the noise. The cheap form of this test is

Δχ²ᵢ(X) = min over θ′ in the opposite class of Σ_b [(S_b(θ) − S_b(θ′)) / σ_b]²,

where the minimum runs over the nuisance parameters of the population (temperature over 0.7-1.05 T_eq, grey cloud top over 5 × 10² to 10⁶ Pa, the other three abundances over 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻², and an additive depth offset) with *X* held at the 10⁻⁴ boundary, which is the hardest counterfactual because band depth is monotone in abundance. The sum runs over the 104 Tier-3 bins at the planet's Tier-1 per-bin σ. The minimum was found by Nelder-Mead from the true parameters and two random restarts from the prior, keeping the smallest value (median 569 forward calls per planet-molecule; 955 planets, four molecules, 207 core-hours). Under a departure the test is rerun with the departure's physics in the counterfactual model as well, with its strength held at the true value, so that it asks whether the label is decidable given a correct model of the departure, which is knowledge a mission would not have; Section 3.6 measures the price of fitting the strength for haze. White noise at *k* times σ needs no run, since Δχ² scales as 1/*k*².

The test represents the opposite class by its single best-fitting point, which over-credits that class, so its scale was calibrated rather than assumed. A nested-sampling retrieval (61, 62; the nestle implementation, multi-ellipsoid sampler, 100 live points, at most 20,000 likelihood calls) of every one of the 955 test planets, with the same free quantities, the four abundances over 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻² and uniform priors on the population's ranges, gave for every label the posterior probability of the true class, p_true, which is the P-statistic of Bocchieri et al. (65) evaluated for the true class (median 3,961 calls and 19.9 min per planet; 365 core-hours). A label is decidable at level *q* if p_true > *q*, with *q* = 0.95 throughout; the Δχ² at which p_true crosses 0.95 was read off per molecule and used as the test's threshold. The looser level 0.84, the one-sided one-sigma level, is reported as a sensitivity; it was added after the 0.95 results were in. The retrieval's own accuracy, the fraction of labels with p_true > 0.5, is the accuracy ceiling the spectra allow under its prior.

Two properties of this retrieval shape every number that rests on it. First, unless stated, it is run on the noise-free spectrum with the Tier-1 σ in the likelihood only: p_true is then the posterior an analyst would expect on average over noise realisations, and a label decidable by it is decidable in expectation, whereas the classifier reads one noisy realisation. To measure the difference, a fixed random 200 of the 955 planets were also retrieved on the classifier's own noisy realisation (Section 3.3). Second, the abundance prior is POP-III's, 10⁻⁹-10⁻², which is wider than POP-I's: under it an uninformative spectrum already gives p_true = 0.71 for an absent label and 0.29 for a present one, so an absent label reaches 0.95 on a Bayes factor of 8 against the opposite class and a present label needs 47. Prevalences are therefore reported for present and absent labels separately, and once more with decidability defined by a Bayes factor above 19 relative to the prior odds, which is the prior-free counterpart of 0.95. The population is explicit: POP-I, the consortium's test prior, at each planet's Tier-1 transit count. The true joint distribution of atmospheric conditions among Ariel's targets is unknown, so every prevalence reported here is a prevalence under this named prior, and Section 3.5 measures how the real spectra sit against it.

### 2.5 The two error classes

Each classifier error on a spectrum was assigned a class from the same spectrum's decidability. On clean spectra the posterior is the definition: an error is recoverable if p_true > 0.95 (a retrieval of the expected spectrum gets the label right), undecidable if 0.05 ≤ p_true ≤ 0.95, and misled if p_true < 0.05. Under a departure the departure-aware Δχ² at the calibrated threshold is used. Because the posterior is of the noise-free spectrum, an error the classifier makes through an unlucky noise draw on a decidable label is counted as recoverable although a retrieval of that realisation may not recover it; the recoverable shares are therefore upper bounds, and Section 3.3 measures the gap on the 200-planet subsample. The prevalence of each class is the fraction of the classifier's errors in it; the expectation recorded before the runs was that haze errors would be mostly recoverable and noise errors mostly irrecoverable (Appendix C). After the runs, the errors under a departure were further split into those the learner also makes on the clean spectrum (shared) and those the departure introduces, a split that is reported as the post-hoc refinement it is.

Each step of the conversion is a stated choice with a measured consequence. JWST products are transit depths, the quantity the classifier was trained on, so no conversion of quantity was needed. Each of the 104 Tier-3 bins took the inverse-variance-weighted mean of the JWST points inside it, partial overlaps weighted by the overlap fraction. Modes were aligned through their overlaps onto NIRISS order 1; for WASP-39 b this gave PRISM −177 ppm, which is the offset Carter & May report from their joint fit. Bins with no data were filled flat at the spectrum's measured mean and flagged, and the number of filled bins is reported with every verdict (Table 3). Per-spectrum normalisation then removed any global offset. Three noise regimes were run: JWST precision as delivered; Ariel Tier-1, the payload model's per-bin σ at the planet's Tier-1 transit count added as 500-1,000 Gaussian draws on top of the JWST noise already in the points, giving a detection frequency per molecule; and Tier-2 and Tier-3 counts for context. No stellar-contamination correction was applied beyond what each source paper applied; four hosts are M dwarfs, and the limitations in Section 4 state the consequence.

The procedure was validated on a spectrum whose answer is known. The benchmark's own best-fit model of WASP-39 b was binned directly onto the 104 bins, and separately sampled at the JWST wavelength grids of each mode with the per-point JWST errors and pushed through the conversion; the two differed by 22 ppm rms, 104 ppm at worst in a MIRI bin, and no verdict changed noise-free. This validates the conversion at near-complete coverage; at the partial coverage of the panel, the control is each planet's clear twin pushed through the same coverage mask (Section 3.5). A sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations (the individual reductions of each mode, two NIRISS pipelines, fitted and fixed limb darkening, offsets on and off, model and flat fill, with and without MIRI, PRISM alone, and the bins of Na, K, SO₂ and CO masked singly and together) measured how much the verdict depends on the reduction, which Roy-Perez et al. (46) showed must be shown rather than assumed.

Two population-level comparisons accompany the verdicts. For each real planet a clear H₂/He twin was rendered at the published abundances and bulk parameters (Table 3). The twin's dispersion over the measured bins divided by the noise-corrected dispersion of the real spectrum is the planet's muting: 1 for a spectrum as featured as its clear twin, larger when it is more muted. Its uncertainty comes from 2,000 resamples of the real depths with their errors. The most muted atmosphere the population can draw at those bulk parameters (the coldest allowed temperature with the deepest allowed deck) says whether the real muting lies inside the simulator's prior, and the same muting was computed for every planet of POP-I against its own clear twin. Separately, the real panel was re-scored by the design retrained on a population in which a third of the training planets carried a haze of density log-uniform in 10⁶-3 × 10⁸ m⁻³, and in a second set a further sixth carried a grey deck at 5-500 Pa, up to two decades beyond the prior's 5 × 10² Pa limit. This is the test of whether a real error belongs to the recoverable class, not a proposed remedy.

### 2.7 Diagnostics, fallbacks and the selection simulation

Four candidate diagnostics were chosen before the runs to target the two classes: (a) disagreement between the all-input, no-optical and AIRS-only variants of the design; (b) an optical-excess score, the three normalised photometric points against the normalised NIRSpec continuum as a z-score on the clean training set; (c) the consortium's own band metric (6), used as a ranking in its own right; and (d) predicted decidability, a regressor on bulk features trained on the Δχ² of Section 2.4. Each was scored by selective credit as in Section 2.3, by its AUROC for separating the two error classes, and, used as a router, by the accuracy and recall of the classifier when flagged planets are re-scored by the AIRS-only variant. The optical-excess score was designed from the haze mechanism of Section 3.2 and is evaluated on the same planets under the same haze; the real panel is its only test on data it was not built from.

Two fallback retrievals are defined on the hazed spectra. The retrieval of Section 2.4 run unchanged on a hazed spectrum is the "clear-model retrieval", the existing method applied to a departed spectrum. The same retrieval with the haze added to its forward model as an eighth free parameter, its number density log-uniform over 10³ to 10¹⁰ m⁻³, where the lower bound is haze-free to below a part per million, is the "free-haze retrieval", the fallback a pipeline could run on any spectrum without knowing whether it is hazed. Both were run on all 954 hazed spectra.

The mission-level measure is a selection simulation. The 955 POP-I planets receive 60 % Tier-2 slots, 573 (3), ranked by the mean present-probability of the three learners that give one (KNN, MLP and RFC; the one-vs-one SVC does not); the score is the number of planets carrying the molecule that are not selected, for CH₄, for H₂O and for either ("joint targets"). Ranking by a molecule's probability is a proxy for a selection the mission will make by science theme and signal-to-noise (3). Strategies compared at the same budget: the classifier alone; the classifier with each existing rule (declined planets sent to the back of the queue); the classifier routed by each candidate to the AIRS-only variant; the band metric alone; and a retrieval of every planet, ranking by its present-posterior, with its cost stated. A triage variant re-scores by the retrieval only the fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect, for *f* from 10 to 70 %, and compares each rule to a random budget (200 draws), to the measured Δχ² of Section 2.4 used as an ordering, which starts from the true parameters and is therefore not attainable, and to the retrieval's own undecidability, the unattainable best ordering. The single-departure simulations use one noise draw.

The mixed-population test applies the same budget where the retrieval shares the classifier's failure. Each of the 954 planets with a hazed spectrum is hazed with probability *q* (0.1, 0.2 or 0.5; 20 draws); the fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect (10 to 50 %) is re-scored by the free-haze retrieval and the rest keep the classifier; the selection is the 60 % cut above, and the clear-model retrieval is run in the free-haze retrieval's place for comparison. The orderings are random, the five existing rules, the optical-excess score, the input disagreement, and an oracle that knows which planets are hazed and nothing else; the fixed strategies are the classifier alone, the optical score routed to the AIRS-only variant without any retrieval, and either retrieval of every planet. The metrics are targets lost at the selection, with their spread over the draws, the pipeline's accuracy, and its recall on the hazed and on the clean targets. The haze is at 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³, which the optical score flags on every hazed planet (Section 3.2), so the oracle is a haze detector at a density where detection saturates. A second point of the dose-response was run at 2 × 10⁶ m⁻³, the lowest density at which the stress test measured a loss (Table 4), by re-rendering the population at that density and repeating the test; its no-retrieval strategies and the detection of the hazed planets by each rule are reported here, and the budgeted retrieval at that density is run on the 200-planet subsample of Section 2.4 [PENDING: the free-haze retrieval of the subsample hazed at 2 × 10⁶ m⁻³ is running]. Expectations for this test were committed after a pilot of the same test on the 124 planets whose free-haze posteriors existed first, and before the population run (Appendix C). [PENDING: the clean planets are scored by the clean retrieval of Section 2.4 until the free-haze retrieval of the clean population completes.]

### 3.3 Identifiability: a third to a half of the labels are undecidable at Tier-1 precision

The Δχ² test and the retrieval posterior agreed in rank: Spearman correlation between Δχ² and logit p_true of 0.86 (CH₄), 0.80 (H₂O), 0.88 (CO₂) and 0.89 (NH₃) over the 955 planets. The test's scale, however, was wrong by an order of magnitude. The posterior crosses 0.95 at Δχ² of 0.45, 0.98, 0.90 and 0.56, not at the nested-model value 3.84 or the 3σ value 9, because the test represents the opposite class by its single best-fitting point while the retrieval integrates over that class's volume; in plain terms, a label is decidable when the best opposite-class fit sits only about one σ away in total χ², which is a statement about the prior's volume as much as about the evidence. The calibrated thresholds are used everywhere below, and the test is a check on the retrieval rather than a substitute: at 207 core-hours against the retrieval's 365 on this population, it is not the cheap method it was meant to be.

A third of the CH₄ and NH₃ labels, 44 % of the CO₂ labels and 54 % of the H₂O labels are undecidable at the 0.95 level (Table 6); with the prior's head start removed the fractions rise by 3-8 points, and present labels are undecidable more often than absent ones for every molecule. Only 17 % of the planets have all four labels decidable, 3 % have none, and a third have both CH₄ and H₂O decidable. Decidability is set first by the label's distance from the 10⁻⁴ boundary and second by the planet's noise: labels within half a decade of the boundary are undecidable in 89-92 % of cases, labels one to two decades away in 12-36 %, and absent labels two to three decades below the boundary in 1-8 % for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ but 31 % for H₂O, the planets whose noise leaves water unconstrained at any abundance. The one retrieved planet of the consortium study, CH₄ at 10⁻³·¹ with a 48 % posterior at Tier 1 (6), is a typical member of the undecidable class. The water figure is a statement about the survey design: at Tier-1 precision under the consortium's own prior, the primary molecule's presence above 10⁻⁴ is undecided for half the planets.

The retrieval scores 92-94 % on every molecule on the noise-free spectra: 97-98 % on absent labels, where the prior already supplies 0.71, and 82-88 % on present labels. The classifier sits 9-16 points below it, 12 on average, so even inside its simulator it is well short of the information limit. Among the classifier's clean errors, only 19-25 % are on labels the spectrum decides; the remaining three quarters to four fifths are on undecidable labels, and effectively none (0-0.8 %) is on a label the spectrum decides the other way. On decidable labels the learners score 91-98 % for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and 76-93 % for H₂O; on undecidable ones 58-70 %, a few points above the majority class. Recall of present labels is 82-84 % (H₂O 65 %) where the label is decidable and 35-49 % (H₂O 27 %) where it is not: the classifier's default on an uninformative spectrum is "absent", which is the prior, and Section 3.5 meets that default on real spectra.

**The retrieval on the classifier's own realisation.** On the 200 random planets retrieved on the noisy spectrum the classifier reads, the population figures held and the labels under them moved. The decidable fractions changed by at most 5 points (CH₄ 70 → 65 %, H₂O 44 → 49 %, CO₂ 55 → 56 %, NH₃ 67 → 66 %), but 5-11 % of labels lost decidability under the draw and 5-12 % gained it, and the correlation of p_true between the two runs was 0.67-0.79. The retrieval's accuracy on the realisation was 3-6 points lower (CH₄ 92.5, H₂O 84.0, CO₂ 89.5, NH₃ 91.0 %), and labels with p_true below 0.05, of which the noise-free population has one in 3,820, appeared on 8 of 800: on data, a calibrated posterior is confidently wrong about 1 % of the time. Of the four learners' 590 errors on these realisations, 18.5 % are recoverable by the noise-free posterior and 14.9 % are decidable by the noisy one; the noisy retrieval gets 60 % of them right at p ≥ 0.5 (the noise-free one 73 %), most of that from the prior's absent default. The recoverable share defined by the expected posterior is therefore an upper bound that overstates by about a fifth of itself on clean spectra; the hazed-spectrum runs on the same realisations are reported in Section 3.6.

### 3.4 The two error classes separate across the departures

Under haze at 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³, with the haze in the counterfactual model, the labels are about as decidable as on the clean spectra (Figure 3a; Table 7): the haze costs water a little, because its bands sit under the near-infrared slope the haze adds, and makes CO₂ more decidable, because with the density known the slope pins the scale height and removes part of the temperature-abundance degeneracy. Under 20 % unocculted spots, with the spots in the counterfactual at their true coverage and temperature, the decidable fractions move by 0.5 to 2.5 points: the contamination deepens the transit by a few per cent at every wavelength, and a test that knows it loses nothing, which bounds what a test that must fit it would keep. A grey deck at 10² Pa, above the prior's limit of 5 × 10² Pa, is different: with the deck at its true pressure in the counterfactual, the decidable fraction falls to 70 % for CH₄, 28 % for H₂O, 53 % for CO₂ and 63 % for NH₃, almost entirely on absent labels (H₂O absent labels decidable 226 → 93 of 591, present 194 → 173 of 364), because the deck hides the deep atmosphere where a small abundance would have shown, so the floor on an absence rises. That is information the atmosphere removes, as noise is information the instrument removes.

Table 7 is the two-class split measured rather than argued. The errors the haze introduces are four fifths recoverable for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and two thirds for H₂O, and all of its errors 45-75 %, against a clean baseline of 31-50 % by the same test. The haze does not take the methane out of the spectrum; it takes the classifier's recall of decidable present labels from 83 % to 14 %. The spots are the same mechanism at lower amplitude: they cost 8-16 points of accuracy and half to two thirds of that recall while leaving the labels as decidable as before, and the errors they introduce are 57-80 % recoverable. The deck sits between haze and noise: on CH₄ and NH₃, whose bands sit high, its errors are four fifths recoverable, but on H₂O and CO₂ only half, because the deck has made undecidable a third of the H₂O labels and a tenth of the CO₂ labels the clean spectrum could decide, and the classifier's errors on those are the atmosphere's, not its own. ### 3.5 External validation: the same two classes on real JWST spectra

**WASP-39 b.** At JWST precision with all 104 inputs, three of the four learners called H₂O present and all four called CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at 10⁻⁴, which agrees with the published composition (39, 40, 45) on every label the literature decides; CO₂ at 10⁻⁵, where its published abundance of about 10⁻⁴·⁵ puts it, was present for two to three learners; NH₃ was absent in all 24 configurations of the sensitivity matrix and with each of three MIRI reductions; and the consortium's own band metric agreed with the majority. The multilayer perceptron called H₂O absent with a score of 0.00, in 23 of the 24 configurations, and on the benchmark's own best-fit model binned directly onto the 104 bins with no conversion at all, so the miss is not the conversion's. It is the shape of a real hot Saturn: the clear H₂/He twin at the published abundances is called H₂O-present by all four learners (perceptron score 1.00), as are twins with grey decks down to 10 Pa, but the real spectrum's dispersion over the measured bins is 324 ppm against 1,190 ppm for the clear twin, a muting of 3.7 (68 % interval 3.67-3.79), whereas the most muted atmosphere the population can draw at WASP-39 b's bulk parameters reaches 2.0. The real spectrum is nevertheless inside the training distribution by every input-space rule (k-NN distance 6.2 against the decline threshold 11.25), because those rules work on normalised spectra and normalisation removes amplitude. Under Ariel Tier-1 noise at WASP-39 b's one transit, H₂O is detected in 58 % of draws with all inputs and 86 % without the three optical points, while the clear twin is detected in 100 %; the optical points decide the Tier-1 outcome on a real planet, which is Section 3.2's mechanism in a weaker form.

**The panel.** Over the 35 panel labels the literature decides, the all-input classifier was right on 22 (63 %), below the share of absent labels (22 of 35), and reproduced none of the thirteen present labels outright. Without the optical points it was right on 25 of 36, and with the AIRS channels alone on 29 of 37 (78 %); the denominators differ because split verdicts are not scored, and the intersection gives the same ordering. Three of the counted misses are the H₂O labels of the planets with only AIRS coverage (WASP-107 b, HAT-P-26 b, GJ 3470 b), called absent by every variant although H₂O is present at 6-21σ. Their twins through the same coverage mask are also missed and the same twins with full coverage are found by all four learners, so this is a coverage artefact of the flat fill rather than a classifier error; it is kept in the count because a pipeline would not know, and without those three labels the scores are 22 of 32, 25 of 33 and 29 of 34. The fourth AIRS-only planet, HD 189733 b, has an ambiguous H₂O label and was called absent, correctly, on the other three. No NH₃ false positive occurred on any panel planet.

The clear twins of these planets, through the same coverage mask and scattered with the real per-bin JWST errors, are detected in 68-100 % of draws and never flagged, so the misses are not the noise, the coverage or the classifier's in-simulator skill; they are the shape of the real spectra, and the information is in the spectrum because the AIRS-only learner finds it. All three planets have clouds or hazes in their retrievals, and the three optical points carry that shape into the learners. These are recoverable errors on real planets in the operational sense that another learner reading the same spectrum finds the label; no retrieval of the real spectra was run, so the posterior definition of Section 2.5 is not applied to them. The distance rule flags three of six and puts the other three at the 90th percentile of clean distances.

**Muting.** Every warm giant on the panel with a published abundance set (HAT-P-26 b, WASP-107 b, WASP-39 b, GJ 3470 b, WASP-166 b) is muted by a factor of 3.1 to 5.3 against its clear twin (Figure 4; 68 % intervals 0.1-1.2 wide), and by 1.7 to 2.2 times beyond the coldest, cloudiest atmosphere the population allows at its mass, radius and irradiation, in every resample; the same holds over the AIRS bins alone, so the muting is in the molecular bands and not in a haze slope. The population's own clouds mute its median planet by 1 %, its 99th percentile by a factor of 2.5 and its most muted planet by 6.3, at the prior's 5 × 10² Pa limit (Figure 4b). The simulator can therefore reach the giants' muting, but in fewer than one planet in a hundred and only at that limit, whereas five of five real giants sit there, needing what the population does not contain: metallicities of tens to hundreds of times solar, hazes or patchy clouds, which is what their JWST retrievals report. The three methane sub-Neptunes are muted by only 0.7-2.0, inside the population's range, although three of their six reductions need a deck above the prior's limit at their own bulk parameters (Table 8); their misses have the different cause above. Both results say that the real spectra carry smaller features, relative to their continua, than the clear-grid training spectra, which is why the classifier's default on real spectra is "absent".

**The error class of the real misses.** Retraining the design on a population in which a third of the training planets carried haze recovered two thirds of the CH₄ haze loss on the simulated population (66 → 79 % against 85 % clean), three quarters of the CO₂ loss, half of the NH₃ loss and two fifths of the H₂O loss, left the noise × 3 column unchanged, and cost 1-2 points on clean spectra. On the real panel it raised the all-input score from 22 of 35 to 26 of 36, and with the deep decks to 27 of 36. The three methane reductions whose muting lies beyond the simulator's aerosol prior (K2-18 b in both reanalyses and TOI-1231 b, needing decks at 47-238 Pa) are the three on which it moved, two to present and one to split under each training set; the three within the prior (TOI-270 d in both reductions and the primary K2-18 b) it did not, apart from a split on K2-18 b under the deep decks (Table 8). Where the failure on a real planet is the training prior, extending the prior repairs it, planet by planet. The AIRS-only variant finds five of six at no retraining, so routing remains the cheaper remedy; the retraining is the test of the class, not the contribution.

### 3.6 The remedy, against the existing methods

**The existing method shares the recoverable failure, and one free parameter repairs it.** The clear-model retrieval of the hazed spectra (Table 9) failed to complete on 18 of 954 planets, against none of 955 on the clean spectra, and on the rest it called every present label absent: recall of present labels fell below 1 % for all four molecules, the posterior of the true class was below 0.05 on 99 % of present labels, every abundance was driven 3.5-4 dex toward the prior floor, and of the 219 completed planets with CH₄ present and decidable on the clean spectrum it recovered two. The misfit is visible in the evidence, median log Z −252 against −12 on clean spectra, so an analyst looking at the fit would see it, but a pipeline that reads only the posterior would not; it is the aerosol misspecification mode known from retrievals of real spectra (10). The departure is largest where the classifier's was: 18σ per bin in the three optical points, 4.6σ over NIRSpec and 1.8σ over AIRS. With the haze density added as a free parameter, the same retrieval completed on all 954 spectra, recovered the density on every planet (median log₁₀ n 7.47 against 7.48 true), recovered 222 of the 224 planets with CH₄ present and decidable on the clean spectrum and kept 75 % of them decidable, which is the price of fitting the departure's strength rather than knowing it; its accuracy stayed within 5 points of the clean retrieval on every molecule and it fit with the clean evidence. Four planets, among them the super-puff Kepler-79 d, fit badly with the radius held fixed and still carried the right labels but one. Figure 3c shows the two retrievals side by side: the haze removes nothing the labels need, and the clear-model retrieval's zero recall on the same spectra is entirely the model's. The free-haze retrieval repeated on the classifier's own noisy realisation of the 200-planet subsample (Section 2.4) holds: the decidable fractions move by 1-4 points (CH₄ 64.5 to 63.0 %, H₂O 33.5 to 37.5 %), 7-10 % of labels lose decidability and 8-11 % gain it, accuracy drops 3.5-6.5 points, confidently wrong labels rise from 2 to 12 of 800, and of the classifier's 1,177 errors on that realisation the noisy retrieval gets 72 % right against 79 % for the noise-free one, so the repair is of the realisation the pipeline would see and not only of the expectation.

**On clean spectra the retrieval is the selection method, and no cheap rule allocates a partial budget much better than chance.** At 60 % Tier-2 slots, ranking by the retrieval's present-posterior loses 0 of 359 CH₄-bearing targets (oracle 0; classifier 20), 2 of 364 H₂O targets (oracle 0; classifier 51) and 39 joint targets (oracle 3; classifier 95). On noise-free spectra under its own forward model a correctly specified retrieval is the Bayes-optimal selector, so this is the expected result and its value is as the reference: the existing method, a retrieval of every planet at 365 core-hours, two days on a laptop, dominates the classifier and every diagnostic on it, and the question becomes what a diagnostic buys at a fixed retrieval budget, and where the retrieval shares the classifier's failure. When only the fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect is re-scored by the retrieval (Table C2), the k-NN and Mahalanobis distances beat a random budget by 2-4 CH₄ targets at *f* = 20-30 %, between one and a half and three times the random draw's standard deviation, and nothing else does; the confidence-based rules are no better than random at any *f*, and for H₂O every rule is within noise of random or worse. They send the retrieval to the planets the classifier is unsure about, which are the undecidable labels, where the retrieval cannot help either; the retrieval pays off on the planets the classifier is confidently wrong about, and no rule finds those. Predicting decidability from bulk features reached an AUROC of only 0.47-0.63; the classifier's own margin and ensemble spread recognise the undecidable labels with AUROC 0.73-0.77 for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and 0.59-0.61 for H₂O (the distance rules 0.68 for H₂O), a random forest trained out of fold on the spectrum itself with the retrieval's labels reaches 0.69-0.74 and does not beat them, and the ceiling of any such recogniser is the measured Δχ² ordering, which loses 8 CH₄ targets at a 20 % budget against 12 for a random one and 7 for the retrieval's own posterior: knowing which labels are undecidable is worth about four targets in 359, because those are the planets the retrieval cannot help either. Declining the 30 % of planets the margin ranks least confident raises the classifier's CH₄ accuracy on the rest from 86 to 95 %, and that, not selection, is where recognising the class pays, as a verdict withheld. Nor do the five rules recognise the haze failure: every rule's selective credit was negative under haze (−17.5 to −25.8 points), spots and the high deck; the margin rule declined 7.4 % of clean planets at the threshold set for 10 % but 0.9 % of hazy ones; the three distance scores separated hazed from clean spectra (AUROC 0.90-0.94) but did not rank errors within them (0.44-0.52), a population alarm rather than a filter; and conformal coverage fell to 73.7 % under haze. This is the pattern reported for image classifiers under dataset shift (66) in a physical setting: confidence does not detect failure under a shift, and the shift detectors that fire do not say which answers are wrong.

**Routed to a retrieval that models the departure, a shift flag allocates a 20 % budget within noise of an oracle; routed to the existing retrieval it is worse than nothing.** With a fifth of the 954 planets hazed (Table 10; Figure 5), the classifier alone loses 29 ± 4 of 359 CH₄ targets and finds 3 % of the hazed ones; the existing method, a clear-model retrieval of every planet, loses 69 and finds none. Re-scoring the 20 % of planets the optical score ranks most suspect by the free-haze retrieval loses 13 ± 2 and finds 80 ± 4 % of the hazed targets, the same within noise as an oracle that knows which planets are hazed (12 ± 2; 81 %), against 20 ± 4 and 17 % for a random budget and 20-22 and 3 % for the margin and ensemble rules, which hold none of the hazed planets because the classifier is confident on them. The 16 targets between the flagged and the unflagged pipeline are 4 % of the CH₄ targets and 3 % of the Tier-2 queue. The existing distance rules used as routers are also within noise of the oracle in targets lost (Mahalanobis 13 ± 2 with 64 % of hazed targets found; PCA 14 ± 2, 58 %; k-NN 14 ± 2, 54 %), because they also detect the shift, less selectively: the top 20 % by the optical score holds 98 % of the hazed and 1 % of the clean planets, by Mahalanobis 73 and 7 %. The score's advantage over them is hazed recall and selectivity, not targets at this budget. Routing the same 20 % to the existing clear-model retrieval loses 71 ± 8 targets, worse than no retrieval: a flag that correctly detects the shift makes selection worse when its destination is the standard method. For H₂O the optical ordering loses 40 against 73 for the classifier alone and 40 for the oracle; at a hazed fraction of 10 % it again matches the oracle (12 against 12), and at 50 % a 20 % budget cannot cover the hazed half and it reaches the oracle at *f* = 50 % (5 against 5). The free-haze retrieval of every planet loses no CH₄ target and two H₂O targets at every hazed fraction, so the limit on the routed pipeline is the budget, not the fallback. Of the expectations committed after the pilot (Table C1), all were met except that two of the three distance rules fell more than 20 points behind the optical score in hazed recall, where the standard deviation of a difference is about 6 points, and that the score's selectivity was 98 % of hazed planets against the 99 % expected.

### 3.7 Expected and surprising results

Appendix C lists every expectation committed before the runs with its outcome. Of the 31, eleven were met as written, fourteen in part, three not met, and three were never run; the misses include WASP-39 b's water at Tier-1 precision (58 % against the 80 % expected), the undecidable fractions at the 0.95 level for three of four molecules, the selective credit of every candidate, and the predicted-decidability regressor. Two results were not among the expectations. The scale of the Δχ² test was wrong by an order of magnitude against the posterior, which the calibration was designed to catch, and the uncalibrated prevalence first computed (78-88 % undecidable at 3σ) is reported here only as the value the calibration replaced. And the real warm giants turned out to lie outside the simulator population in feature amplitude while inside it in every bulk parameter and by every distance rule, which no expectation anticipated.

## 4. Discussion

**What the two classes mean for Tier-2 selection.** Under the consortium's own test prior, a third to a half of the Tier-1 labels are not decided by the spectrum, and that is where most of the classifier's errors in its own simulator sit. Those planets are not a classifier problem: a retrieval of the same spectrum gives a posterior near the prior, and a pipeline that reads either answer as a verdict dismisses planets the data never judged. The undecidable fraction is a function of the population and the precision, not of the method, and it is the first number a selection pipeline should know; for water, Ariel's primary molecule, it is half the planets at Tier-1 precision under this prior, which should be read alongside the retrieval-based detection fractions of Bocchieri et al. (65) and the tier reassessment of Radica et al. (4). The picture for a pipeline is then specific. On clean spectra, retrieve every planet; the cost is two days, and no cheap rule allocates a partial budget much better than chance. Where the retrieval's forward model is wrong in the way haze makes it wrong, the retrieval fails more confidently than the classifier and its evidence, not its posterior, is the warning; a one-number score on the optical inputs finds the affected planets in simulation, and a retrieval budget sent to them with the haze density free selects as an oracle would, while the same budget sent to the standard retrieval loses more targets than no retrieval. And for the labels the spectra do not decide, the classifier's own confidence marks most of them, no cheap flag marks them better, and no allocation of a retrieval budget to them helps, so those planets should reach Tier 2 as undecided rather than dismissed. The absolute stakes are modest in this simulation, 16 of 359 methane targets at a 20 % budget, 3 % of the Tier-2 queue; the sign of the effect, that a correct flag with the wrong destination is worse than no flag, is what a pipeline design has to get right.

**Real spectra against the training prior.** The external test found the two classes it was looking for, and one thing it was not. The three methane sub-Neptunes are recoverable errors in the operational sense: the information is in the AIRS bands, a learner that reads only those finds it, and the learner that also reads the optical points does not, which is the simulated haze mechanism on real data. The five warm giants are a different finding. Each is muted 3 to 5 times beyond its clear twin and 1.7 to 2.2 times beyond anything the simulator population can draw at its bulk parameters, in every bootstrap resample, while sitting inside the training distribution by every input-space rule, because those rules work on normalised spectra and normalisation removes amplitude. That is a limit of the prior that no normalised-input rule can see. A muting score built the same way for the simulated population, the dispersion of a spectrum's AIRS bins against the lowest the training prior produces at the planet's bulk parameters, flags 8-14 % of the hazed, spotted and clouded spectra at a 10 % clean rate (AUROC 0.43-0.58 against clean spectra), because every simulated departure stays inside the amplitude range the training decks already span; the real giants do not, which is what makes their muting a finding about the prior rather than about the classifier. It is also the reason the classifier's default on real spectra is "absent": the real features are smaller, relative to their continua, than the clear-grid training features, and the operating point is tuned to the latter. For the sub-Neptunes with the highest mean molecular weights the clear H₂/He twin is itself an approximation the simulator cannot improve on, since the population contains no such atmosphere. A training population for real Tier-1 spectra needs the aerosols and metallicities the JWST retrievals report, and the muting census is a cheap way to check, planet by planet, whether a real spectrum lies where the prior can reach.

**Why confidence rules fail and when a shift flag helps.** The five decline rules did not recognise the haze failure because the classifier is confident on hazed spectra, and the three distance rules that do detect the shift rank errors within it no better than chance. This is the pattern reported for image classifiers under dataset shift (66) in a physical setting, and the selective-credit protocol of Jaeger et al. (16) is what makes it visible: a rule that declines the whole hazed population has no credit, because it has nothing left to be right about. The value of a shift flag is therefore not in declining but in routing, and the destination decides everything. Routed to the standard retrieval, a flag that correctly detects the shift is worse than no flag; routed to a retrieval that models the departure, the optical-excess score, the Mahalanobis distance and an oracle that knows which planets are hazed lose the same number of targets within the draw-to-draw noise, and the score's advantage over the existing distance rules is its recall and selectivity on the hazed planets, not the selection. On the real panel the score flagged the spotted host and one K2-18 b reduction and said nothing about the methane misses, which the input-disagreement candidate flagged on four of six. The honest statement of the remedy is that the use was demonstrated, in simulation, for one departure the fallback models exactly, and that the flag which organises it did not fire on the real failures it was built to catch.

**Beyond Ariel.** Nothing in the decomposition is specific to transmission spectroscopy. Any inference system trained on a simulator and used to make decisions produces errors of two kinds, those the data could have decided and those they could not, and the split can be measured by running the expensive method on the population the cheap one is tested on. The measurement gives three things a decision pipeline needs and a classifier cannot supply: the prevalence of undecided answers under a stated prior, the share of the cheap method's errors that any method could fix, and the test of whether a proposed fallback shares the failure. The last is the one this study would single out: the standard fallback, applied under the departure it does not model, was worse than nothing.

**Limitations.** Every retrieval-based number in Sections 3.3, 3.4 and 3.6 is of the noise-free spectrum with the Tier-1 σ in the likelihood, so decidability is decidability in expectation and the recoverable shares are upper bounds; the 200-planet subsample retrieved on the classifier's realisation bounds the difference on clean spectra (Section 3.3) and for the free-haze retrieval of hazed spectra (Section 3.6); the clear-model retrieval of the noisy hazed subsample is running. The retrieval's abundance prior is wider than the test population's, which gives absent labels a head start of 0.71 that the Bayes-factor row of Table 6 removes; the ceiling of 92-94 % includes that default. The retrieval holds the planetary radius fixed with only a small depth offset free, and the radius-abundance-cloud degeneracy (68) is the dominant nuisance of absolute-abundance retrieval, so the undecidable fractions are lower bounds and the ceiling an upper bound under that model; the forward model is the training model throughout, and a retrieval with a free radius and a full aerosol model is what a consortium would run on a flagged planet. The departure-aware error classes for spots and the deep deck hold the departure's strength at its true value and rest on the Δχ² test, which on clean spectra credits more errors as recoverable than the posterior does; for haze both measures were run and the price of fitting the strength was a quarter of the decidable labels. The mixed-population test with the retrieval budget hazed the planets at one density and particle size with the fallback's forward model correct up to one parameter, at a density where the optical score flags every hazed planet, so matching the oracle there is the expected outcome; at the weaker density the flag's sensitivity halves and the no-retrieval routing recovers little of the loss, which is in H₂O (Section 3.6), and the budgeted retrieval there, mixed particle sizes and the free-haze fallback on spotted spectra, which the score sends to it, are not yet reported. The rules' thresholds are quantiles of the clean test scores, so their clean decline rates are fixed by construction; set on a random half of the test population and applied to the other half (20 splits), every rule's decline rate under every departure is reproduced within 0.5 points, and the clean rates fall at 7.6-10.7 %. The single-departure selection simulation of Table 11 is one noise draw; over ten draws its rows move by 3-5 targets and the routing's recovery under haze is about half of the added loss rather than two thirds (Section 3.6). The payload noise model was checked against published bands for AIRS only. No stellar-contamination correction was applied beyond the source papers' own; four panel hosts are M dwarfs, and the simulated spot departure shows what 10-20 % coverage would cost. Labels for the real planets come from the literature, the ambiguous ones are not scored, coverage of the Ariel grid by JWST is partial for every panel planet, and the conversion was validated only at near-complete coverage, with the clear-twin control standing in at partial coverage. The six methane reductions are three planets. Haze strengths were anchored to retrieved hazes through an order-of-magnitude conversion that depends on the assumed pressure level, and the nested-sampling settings (100 live points, 20,000 calls) carry a Monte Carlo error near the 0.95 cut that no repeat-seed check bounds. The selection simulation ranks by a molecule's probability, a proxy for a selection the mission will make by theme and signal-to-noise. The results concern the published design as re-implemented, under one population; a second population with equilibrium chemistry and a metallicity distribution would show how far the prevalences move with the prior, and the second classifier of Appendix B, built for a different objective, showed a nearly opposite departure profile, so one classifier's error classes do not stand in for another's.

## 5. Conclusion

Before a simulator-trained classifier is used to select Ariel targets, the mission should know which of its labels the spectra decide, retrieve every planet where it can afford to, route the planets a shift flag marks to a retrieval that models the departure or, failing that, to an input that does not see it, and send the undecided planets to Tier 2 as undecided rather than dismissed. This study measured each of those quantities for the consortium's Tier-1 design, re-implemented and checked against its published accuracies. A retrieval of every test planet showed that under the consortium's own prior a third to a half of the labels are undecidable at Tier-1 precision and that three quarters of the classifier's clean errors fall on them, so they are irrecoverable at that precision under that prior; haze leaves the labels decidable and its errors mostly recoverable, noise does the opposite, and a deep deck is partly each. On real JWST spectra the same classes appear: the methane sub-Neptunes are recoverable misses that enter through the optical inputs, and the warm giants are muted beyond anything the training prior contains. The existing retrieval shares the haze failure and fails more confidently than the classifier; one free parameter repairs it, and a shift flag routed to the repaired retrieval allocates a partial budget within noise of an oracle, while the same flag routed to the existing retrieval is worse than no flag. The undecidable class is recognised, imperfectly, by the classifier's own confidence and by nothing cheaper or better, and recognising it is worth a withheld verdict rather than a retrieval; the flag did not fire on the real misses. The question a selection pipeline must answer first, which of its answers the data decide, is one the data answer and the classifier cannot, and that holds for any simulator-trained inference system used to make decisions. The procedures and the code that implement them are released with this paper.

## Appendix B. A second classifier

A gradient-boosted classifier (29) for the carbon-to-oxygen ratio (C/O > 1) (14, 26), trained on 18,040 atmospheres with equilibrium chemistry from FastChem (27) at the Tier-3 layout of the mission's radiometric model (28), reached 96.1 % on its own test sets. HCN and C₂H₂, abundant in carbon-rich atmospheres above about 800 K and absent from its training grid as from the Ariel Data Challenge grid (5), lowered its accuracy on carbon-rich planets from 97.6 to 48.9 % while it stayed confident; retraining with the absorbers restored 96.1 %, and distance scores ranked these errors well (AUROC 0.90-0.91). Haze at 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³ cost it only 5.1 points. At the same Tier-3 binning, one classifier was robust to omitted absorbers and fragile to haze, and the other the reverse. The stress test of one Ariel classifier did not predict the other's, so each classifier needs its own measurement of the two error classes.
