**Original article**

# Which molecular labels Ariel Tier-1 spectra can decide: error classes of a re-implemented classifier and a routing method developed on JWST spectra

Owen Yang<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Henry M. Gunn High School, 780 Arastradero Rd, Palo Alto, CA 94306, USA. Tel.: +1 650 686 7813. E-mail: owenhyang@gmail.com (corresponding author)

---

## Abstract

Machine learning classification has been proposed for sorting the exoplanet atmospheres of Ariel's Tier-1 survey of about 1,000 planets by composition, and target selection on those labels may dismiss a planet for good. Such classifiers are trained and tested on one simulator, so the identifiability of their labels and their behaviour under distribution shift are unknown. This study re-implements the consortium's Tier-1 classifiers, checks them against 48 published accuracies, measures both, and proposes a routing method. An atmospheric retrieval of 955 simulated transmission spectroscopy targets shows that, at the 0.95 level, under the training prior, on noise-free spectra with the radius fixed, a third to a half of the CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃ labels are undecided at Tier-1 precision, and about four fifths of the classifier's clean errors fall on them. Aerosols leave labels decidable when their strength is known, and their errors are mostly recoverable; threefold noise doubles the undecided fraction. On JWST spectra of 23 planets the classifier is right on 39 of 40 absent labels and finds 6.5 of 26 present ones, or 3.0 at Tier-1 noise. It misses methane on seven of eight reductions of five sub-Neptunes, a failure in the inputs below 1.95 µm that the AIRS-only input repairs on six. Ten of twelve giants are muted beyond the training prior, which costs labels at Tier-1 noise and not at JWST precision. The method reads each mismatch with a flag that needs no retrieval and sends it to its own remedy. It gains 8.5 of 66 labels at JWST precision (descriptive 95 % interval 2.8 to 14.0) and 2.7 at Tier-1 noise (1.2 to 4.3), at 4.9 points of clean simulated accuracy. It cannot be separated from two simpler fixes and was built on this panel. Under the model misspecification of haze a clear-model retrieval fails more confidently than the classifier, and a budget routed to it loses more targets than no retrieval.

**Keywords:** exoplanet atmospheres; transmission spectroscopy; Ariel; JWST; machine learning classification; identifiability; atmospheric retrieval; target selection; distribution shift; model misspecification; aerosols

## 1. Introduction

When a planet transits its star, part of the starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere, and molecules in the atmosphere absorb that light at their characteristic wavelengths. The resulting transmission spectrum shows which molecules are present. Ariel, a European Space Agency mission scheduled for launch in 2029, will measure transmission spectra of about 1,000 exoplanets between 0.5 and 7.8 µm, using three broadband photometers below 1.1 µm, which give the three optical points of the classifier's input, and three spectrometer channels (NIRSpec, AIRS-CH0 and AIRS-CH1) at longer wavelengths (1, 2). Each target is observed first at low signal-to-noise in a Tier-1 reconnaissance survey, and a subset is re-observed at higher precision in Tier 2 (1, 3-5). Tier-1 spectra are the basis for choosing which planets receive deeper observations; a planet dismissed at Tier 1 may never be observed again.

Reading a spectrum with an atmospheric retrieval requires 10⁵-10⁸ model evaluations per planet (6) and specialist choices of assumptions and priors (7), which makes retrievals costly as a first pass over 1,000 planets, although the whole Ariel target list has been retrieved once in simulation (8), and with the simple forward model used here the test population took 365 core-hours (Section 3.6). Machine-learning classifiers, which answer in seconds once trained, are an attractive alternative. The Ariel consortium's population study of the Tier-1 survey proposed, as a preliminary assessment, four scikit-learn classifiers that flag the presence of CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃, reaching 64-89 % accuracy on simulated planets (7). Their code and trained models were not released.

Like the models developed for the Ariel Data Challenges (6) and in the wider machine-learning retrieval literature (9-14), these classifiers were trained and tested on spectra from the same simulator. Such a test shows that a classifier has learned its simulator, not how it behaves when real atmospheres differ from it, and they do: hazes and clouds mute or reshape spectra (15, 16), unocculted star spots imprint false features (17), line lists disagree (18, 19), training grids omit absorbers that real atmospheres contain, and misspecification of this kind biases simulation-based inference in general (20-23). JWST transmission spectra of warm giants and sub-Neptunes now carry SO₂, hazes, patchy clouds and metallicities of tens to hundreds of times solar (24-44), none of which a classifier trained on clear hydrogen-helium atmospheres with grey clouds has seen. Simulator-trained retrieval models have been checked against real Hubble and JWST spectra (9, 13, 45-47), on WASP-39 b the retrieved abundances move by more than an order of magnitude with the data reduction alone (48), and one amortised retrieval collapsed on the real WASP-39 b spectrum until the planetary radius was added to its latent parameters (49). A web search on 30 October 2026, including arXiv and the main journals, found no molecule-presence classifier proposed for Ariel that has been checked against a real spectrum.

A classifier's error on a spectrum is one of two things. Either the information was in the spectrum and the classifier missed it, which retraining, a different input or a fallback method can fix. Or the information was not there at that precision, under the population the spectrum is drawn from, and a method reading that spectrum can do no better than the population's prior. This study calls the two classes recoverable and irrecoverable. A label's class depends on the spectrum and is answered by asking whether an atmosphere of the opposite class fits the spectrum within its noise; the share of a classifier's errors in each class, and the rules that find them, depend on the classifier. The consortium study asked the first question for one planet, a retrieval that gave a 48 % probability of CH₄ above 10⁻⁴ at Tier-1 precision (7). Bocchieri et al. (50) then ran retrievals on about 300 simulated Tier-1 targets and defined a P-statistic, the posterior probability that a molecule is present, which is the quantity this study calls decidability, and Radica et al. (5) measured it for three benchmark planets across the tiers. Neither measured it on the test population of a classifier, against that classifier's errors, or under departures from the simulator. The classifier's own confidence cannot stand in for the measurement, because confidence does not survive a distribution shift, a result known from image classification (51) that Sections 3.3 and 3.6 reproduce under haze. The alternative a classifier offers is to decline the spectra it is unsure of, or that look unlike its training set; five such rules from the failure-detection literature are scored alongside (Section 2.3).

This study answers five questions about the consortium design, in the order a mission would need them, and proposes a routing method from the answers. First, how often do physically distinct compositions give indistinguishable Ariel spectra under an explicitly defined population (Sections 2.4 and 3.2)? Second, which of the classifier's errors are recoverable and which are not, under the simulator and under each departure from it (Sections 2.5 and 3.3)? Third, is the classifier right on real spectra, once the observed JWST spectra are made compatible with its inputs by a justified and validated procedure, and where it is not, what fails (Sections 2.6 and 3.4)? Fourth, does a method that recognises those failures and routes each to its own remedy improve classification and target selection, at what cost and at which precision (Sections 2.7 and 3.5)? Fifth, do the existing methods, a retrieval of every planet and the standard decline rules, share the classifier's failure, and what does a flag buy at a fixed retrieval budget (Sections 2.8 and 3.6)? The classifiers were first re-implemented and checked against all 48 published accuracies (Section 3.1). Expectations for the identifiability measurements, the error classes, the external test, the diagnostics, the mixed-population test, the per-reduction flag and the confined hazes were committed to the repository before their runs, and the outcomes, including those not met, are reported (Section 4, Appendix C). The muting census, the retrained designs and the routers had none.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 The re-implemented classifier and its re-implementation

The re-implementation followed Sections 2.2 and 2.5 of the consortium study (7) element by element (Table A1). Isothermal atmospheres with random temperatures, abundances of CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃, and grey cloud decks were generated for every planet of the Ariel Mission Candidate Sample (52), four times for training (POP-III) and once for testing (POP-I). Counts differ slightly between the analyses below because a few spectra failed to render or to bin under one condition or another: 955 of the 977 planets of the list have a clean test spectrum, and these are the test population below, except that the reproduction of Section 3.1 and the mixed-population test of Section 2.8 use the 954 of them that also have a hazed spectrum. Spectra were binned at Tier-3 resolution, scattered with Tier-1 noise and normalised per spectrum. A molecule was labelled present if its abundance exceeded 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴ or 10⁻³, and four scikit-learn classifiers at default settings (53) were trained for each molecule and threshold.

The study did not state whether the classifier input included the three optical points. It stated that the classifiers learn "from their spectral shape over the whole wavelength range sampled by Ariel" and "gather information from all the spectral data points" (7), and its figures of observed Tier-1 spectra show those points, so they were included. Four terms are used throughout. A *learner* is one of the four scikit-learn models (k-nearest neighbours, KNN; multilayer perceptron, MLP; random forest, RFC; support-vector classifier, SVC). The *classifier* is their average in simulation and their majority on a real spectrum. The *design* is the recipe, which can be retrained on another population or given another input. The *re-implemented classifier* is this recipe, with the deviations of Section 2.2, on all 104 inputs and trained on POP-III. The consortium's original trained models were not released, and no claim here concerns them.

### 2.2 Forward model, noise and deviations

Spectra were computed with TauREx 3 (54) at R ≈ 1,000 and binned by integration: isothermal atmospheres of 100 layers with constant abundances, molecular absorption and Rayleigh scattering, a grey cloud deck and, where stated, a Mie haze. They were first rendered through the MultiREx wrapper (55); the released code calls TauREx directly and reproduces them bit for bit. TauREx 3.3.2 reads the pressure grid of Exo-Transmit opacity tables as bar, whereas Exo-Transmit treats it as Pa; the pressure grid was divided by 10⁵ when the tables were loaded. The inconsistency was reported to the TauREx developers and has since been corrected in their development branch (56). With the correction, TauREx spectra of 1,804 test atmospheres agreed better with Exo-Transmit's own code (median shape correlation 0.9986, against 0.986).

Per-bin noise came from a payload model built with ExoSim 2 (57): the one-hour noise-to-signal ratio for the host's effective temperature, scaled to the host's distance and radius and to the transit duration, and divided by the square root of the number of transits. Its one free parameter was calibrated so that a signal-to-noise ratio of 7 on the five-scale-height atmospheric signal reproduced the candidate sample's Tier-3 transit counts, which the model then predicted with a standard deviation of 0.07 dex over 929 targets. For the three example planets in Figure 1 of the consortium study (HD 209458 b, GJ 1214 b and WASP-79 b), its median AIRS noise of 73, 421 and 227 ppm per bin matches the published noise bands in size, as read from that figure. The photometric and NIRSpec channels were not checked against published bands, which the limitations in Section 4 note.

Three deviations from the study's recipe remain. (i) Opacities came from Exo-Transmit's tables (18, 58); substituting ExoMol cross sections (19) for H₂O, CH₄, CO₂ and CO cost the classifier 2.9 points (Section 3.3), which bounds the effect of this choice. (ii) Collision-induced absorption was omitted. With the HITRAN tables (59) it changes the largest bin of a spectrum by a median of 1 ppm on 200 random test planets, by 49 ppm at the 90th percentile and by 500 ppm at worst (`cia_effect.py`). With it in the test spectra alone the classifier loses 0.1 points, and with both populations re-rendered and the design refitted the 48 accuracies move by a mean absolute 0.4 points and at most 1.9, no closer to the published values (mean absolute difference 1.6 against 1.4). (iii) Noise came from the payload model instead of ArielRad (60), which is not public. An earlier reading of the publication, with spectra binned to the seven Tier-1 points, the atmosphere truncated at 1 Pa and the noise set exactly at the Tier-1 requirement, missed the published accuracies by 10 points on average and by up to 38, so the comparison with the published values discriminates between readings of the recipe.

### 2.3 The departures and the stress test

For each departure (Table 1), the same test planets were re-rendered or modified, so every loss was a within-planet difference under the same noise draw. Haze followed the Lee et al. prescription (61) with particles of 0.1 µm radius filling the column: TauREx applies the density given here as one particle number density in every layer, from the model's base to its top at 10⁻⁴ Pa. Because that density does not fall with height, the haze's mixing ratio rises with height, its optical depth accumulates up to the top boundary, and the size of its optical excess depends on where the boundary sits: on three test planets, moving the top by two decades changes the excess by up to a factor of two (`haze_top_test.py`). Retrievals of ten observed hot Jupiters expressed haze opacity at 0.35 µm as a multiple of the H₂ Rayleigh opacity (62) and found log₁₀ a = 2.1-5.7 (16). At 100 Pa the densities 3 × 10⁷, 2.4 × 10⁸ and 10¹⁰ m⁻³ used here correspond to log₁₀ a ≈ 2.4-2.7, 3.3-3.6 and 4.9-5.2, but a constant density makes a rise with height, by two decades for every two decades of pressure, so the correspondence holds at that level only. To test whether the results depend on this form, the test population was also rendered under three confined hazes, with the same planets and decks: the same particles and density below 1 Pa only (optical excess a median of 409 ppm, against 1,094 ppm for the column-filling haze), and a power-law opacity at constant mixing ratio, slope 4 and log₁₀ a ≈ 3.1 and 4.1 at 0.35 µm at every height (373 and 546 ppm; `haze_confined.py`). Spots used PHOENIX spectra from the STScI atlas (63) at a spot-to-photosphere temperature ratio of 0.85, close to the 0.86 of Rackham et al. (17), for every host type, an M-dwarf contrast that differs for FGK hosts (64).

Three input variants of the design were trained on the same spectra: all 104 bins (the input of the re-implemented classifier), the 101 bins without the three optical points ("no-optical"), and the AIRS bins above 1.95 µm alone ("AIRS-only"). Two transplant experiments located the haze failure: the three hazed optical points were copied into otherwise clean spectra, and the clean points were restored in otherwise hazed spectra. Table 1 lists every level run; Table 4 reports those that matter for what follows, chosen after the runs, and the rest are in the repository. The stress test, in the manner of a corruption benchmark (65), measured the loss (clean accuracy minus accuracy under the departure), the irreducible loss (clean accuracy minus the accuracy of the design retrained with the departure present), and the loss of the design trained on a randomised grid in which each planet independently carried haze, spots and extra noise (66).

Five decline rules of the kind used for failure detection were scored alongside. Two read the classifier's confidence: the probability margin ("margin") and the spread of the four learners' probabilities ("ensemble"; 67). Three read the distance of a spectrum from the training set: the Mahalanobis distance (68), the mean distance to the 10 nearest training spectra ("k-NN distance"; 69) and the principal-component reconstruction error ("PCA"). Each rule's threshold is the 90th percentile of its score on the clean test spectra themselves, so that it declines 10 % of them; a rule's clean decline rate is therefore fixed by construction, and a held-out check (thresholds set on a random half of the test population and applied to the other half, 20 splits) is reported in Section 3.6. A rule is credited only with the accuracy of the planets it accepts above the clean selective baseline at the same coverage (its "selective credit"; 70, 71), with split-conformal coverage (72, 73) alongside, the fraction of planets whose conformal prediction set contains the true label, calibrated to 90 % on clean spectra. These five rules are the "existing rules" of Section 3.6.

### 2.4 Identifiability: which labels a spectrum decides

For planet *i* with true parameters θ and molecule *X*, the label "X > 10⁻⁴" is decidable at precision σ if no atmosphere with the opposite label, drawn from the stated population, fits the noise-free spectrum within the noise. The cheap form of this test is

Δχ²ᵢ(X) = min over θ′ with X = 10⁻⁴ and over δ of Σ_b [(S_b(θ) − S_b(θ′) − δ) / σ_b]² (eq1)

where S_b(θ) is the model transit depth in bin *b* for parameters θ, σ_b the planet's Tier-1 noise in that bin and δ an additive depth offset. The minimum runs over the nuisance parameters of the population (temperature over 0.7-1.05 T_eq, grey cloud top over 5 × 10² to 10⁶ Pa, the other three abundances over 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻²), with *X* held at the 10⁻⁴ boundary, which is the hardest counterfactual because band depth is monotone in abundance. The sum runs over the 104 Tier-3 bins. The minimum was found by Nelder-Mead from the true parameters and two random restarts from the prior, keeping the smallest value (median 569 forward calls per planet-molecule; 955 planets, four molecules, 207 core-hours); a missed minimum inflates Δχ² and over-states decidability, the opposite sign to the bias discussed next. Under a departure the test is rerun with the departure's physics in the counterfactual model as well, with its strength held at the true value, so that it asks whether the label is decidable given a correct model of the departure, which is knowledge a mission would not have; Section 3.6 measures the price of fitting the strength for haze. White noise at *c* times σ needs no run, since Δχ² scales as 1/*c*².

The test represents the opposite class by its single best-fitting point, which over-credits that class, so its scale was calibrated rather than assumed. A nested-sampling retrieval (74, 75; the nestle implementation, multi-ellipsoid sampler, 100 live points, at most 20,000 likelihood calls) of every one of the 955 test planets, with the same free quantities, the four abundances over 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻² and uniform priors on the population's ranges, gave for every label the posterior probability of the true class, p_true, which is the P-statistic of Bocchieri et al. (50) evaluated for the true class (median 3,961 calls and 19.9 min per planet; 365 core-hours). A label is decidable at level *q* if p_true > *q*, with *q* = 0.95 throughout; the Δχ² at which p_true crosses 0.95 was read off per molecule and used as the test's threshold. The looser level 0.84, the one-sided one-sigma level, is reported as a sensitivity; it was in the plan and the calibration script before any retrieval ran, but no expectation was recorded for it. The retrieval's own accuracy, the fraction of labels with p_true > 0.5, is the accuracy ceiling the spectra allow under its prior.

Three properties of this retrieval shape every number that rests on it. First, unless stated, it is run on the noise-free spectrum with the Tier-1 σ in the likelihood only: p_true is the posterior of that one noise-free realisation, not an average over realisations, whereas the classifier reads one noisy realisation. To measure the difference, a fixed random 200 of the 955 planets were also retrieved on the classifier's own noisy realisation (Section 3.2). Second, the abundance prior is POP-III's, 10⁻⁹-10⁻², which is wider than POP-I's: under it an uninformative spectrum already gives p_true = 0.71 for an absent label and 0.29 for a present one, so an absent label reaches 0.95 on a Bayes factor of 8 against the opposite class and a present label needs 47. Prevalences are therefore reported for present and absent labels separately, and once more with decidability defined by a Bayes factor above 19 relative to the prior odds, which is the prior-free counterpart of 0.95, and the 200-planet subsample was retrieved a third time under POP-I's own prior, 10⁻⁷-10⁻², to measure the prior's hand directly (Section 3.2). Third, the retrieval's forward model is the training model itself, with the planetary radius fixed and a small depth offset free. "Undecidable" therefore means not decided at the 0.95 level under this prior and this forward model: a chemically informed model or a narrower prior could decide some of these labels, and a free radius could undecide others. The population is explicit: the planets and abundances of POP-I at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, retrieved under POP-III's prior. The true joint distribution of atmospheric conditions among Ariel's targets is unknown, so every prevalence reported here is a prevalence under this named prior, and Section 3.4 measures how the real spectra sit against it.

### 2.5 The two error classes

Each classifier error on a spectrum was assigned a class from the same spectrum's decidability. On clean spectra the posterior is the definition: an error is recoverable if p_true > 0.95 (a retrieval of the noise-free spectrum gets the label right), undecidable if 0.05 ≤ p_true ≤ 0.95, and misled if p_true < 0.05. An error is irrecoverable if its label is undecidable or misled; misled labels are one in 3,820 on clean spectra (Section 3.2), so in practice the irrecoverable class is the undecidable one. Under a departure the departure-aware Δχ² at the calibrated threshold is used. Because the posterior is of the noise-free spectrum, an error the classifier makes through an unlucky noise draw on a decidable label is counted as recoverable although a retrieval of that realisation may not recover it; the recoverable shares are therefore upper bounds, and Section 3.2 measures the gap on the 200-planet subsample. The prevalence of each class is the fraction of the classifier's errors in it; the expectation recorded before the runs was that haze errors would be mostly recoverable and noise errors mostly irrecoverable (Appendix C). After the runs, the errors under a departure were further split into those the learner also makes on the clean spectrum (shared) and those the departure introduces, a split that is reported as the post-hoc refinement it is.

### 2.6 Real JWST spectra on the Ariel grid

The primary external input is the WASP-39 b benchmark of Carter & May et al. (24), the four JWST Early Release Science modes (26-29) re-reduced uniformly with fitted inter-mode offsets, 0.52-5.3 µm, extended to 7.8 µm with the MIRI LRS spectrum of Powell et al. (25). Twenty-two further planets with public JWST transmission spectra, in 25 reductions, formed a panel (Table 2): ten planets in thirteen reductions joined WASP-39 b in a first batch, nine more came in a second batch after the first batch's verdicts were known and the shape flag of Section 2.7 had been designed, and three more in a third batch after the muting-census routing of Section 3.5 had been designed and scored on the first two. The panel is therefore a test of those elements on their later batches and of nothing designed after it; Table C3 lists what was fixed before what.

Labels came from the published free-chemistry retrievals at the 10⁻⁴ threshold: present where the 1σ lower bound lies above it, absent where the upper bound or the quoted upper limit lies below it. Where a paper's abundance straddled 10⁻⁴, or the literature disagreed, the label was marked ambiguous and not scored. Their timing differs by batch. They were committed before the verdicts for the first batch (15 October 2026) and the third (29 October). The second batch's labels were committed with its verdicts on 29 October, so the repository does not show their order. WASP-39 b's scored labels were set on 29 October, after its verdicts; its H₂O and CH₄ labels match the expectations of 16 October. Its H₂O label rests on the free retrieval of Deka et al. (76), log₁₀ −2.08 ± 0.09 with patchy clouds and −2.64 ± 0.08 with Mie aerosols, and its CH₄ label on the 3σ limit of 5 × 10⁻⁶ from the equilibrium models of Rustamkulov et al. (29), since no free retrieval prints one. Three labels that rest on detections at or below about 3σ are kept but marked tentative: the CH₄ and CO₂ of GJ 1214 b (2.0 and 2.4σ alone, 3.3σ jointly) and the CH₄ of TOI-732 c (3.2σ). The first batch's labels came from a survey of the literature made before this rule was written down, and K2-18 b's H₂O, frozen as absent in its three reductions, rests on published limits near 10⁻³, above the threshold; every design calls it absent except the haze-retrained design on the Hu reduction (split), so it changes only that design's score, by half a label. A split verdict (two learners each way) is neither right nor wrong, and the scoring of Section 2.7 gives it half credit.

All 104 cells were audited against their sources after the verdicts were known (`results/jwst_panel/label_sources.csv`). Of the 66 scored labels, 56 follow the rule as written. Three depart from it: K2-18 b's H₂O in two reductions, as above, and TOI-270 d's NH₃ in the Felix reduction, where the reduction's own retrieval straddles the threshold. Seven are judgement calls between papers on the same data, among them WASP-17 b's and WASP-39 b's H₂O. Of the 38 unscored cells, two should have been scored absent (HD 209458 b's CO₂ and WASP-52 b's NH₃), and one is a judgement call. The frozen labels were kept, because changing them after the verdicts would be post hoc, and every score is also reported under the corrected set (65 labels) and under the set that also removes every judgement call (59 labels; `label_sensitivity.py`, Section 3.5).

Each step of the conversion is a stated choice with a measured consequence. JWST products are transit depths, the quantity the classifier was trained on, so no conversion of quantity was needed. Each of the 104 Tier-3 bins took the inverse-variance-weighted mean of the JWST points inside it, partial overlaps weighted by the overlap fraction. Modes were aligned through their overlaps onto a reference mode, NIRISS order 1 for WASP-39 b, where this gave PRISM −177 ppm, the offset Carter & May report from their joint fit, and the mode with the most points for the panel; modes with no overlap, such as the two G395H detectors, keep their published offset. Bins with no data were filled flat at the spectrum's measured mean and flagged, and the number of filled bins is reported with every verdict (Table 2). Fourteen of the 26 reductions have at least 40 filled bins, and eleven have no measured optical point. Per-spectrum normalisation then removed any global offset; it runs over all 104 bins, filled ones included, and the mask control below tests what that does. Two noise regimes were run on the panel: JWST precision as delivered, and Ariel Tier-1, the payload model's per-bin σ at the planet's Tier-1 transit count added as 500 Gaussian draws (300 for WASP-39 b) on top of the JWST noise already in the points. A detection frequency is the share of draws, averaged over the four learners, in which a learner reports the molecule; where a majority of learners is meant, the text says so. For WASP-39 b the Tier-3 transit count was also run for context. No stellar-contamination correction was applied beyond what each source paper applied. Seven hosts are M dwarfs, among them the hosts of all five methane planets, and the limitations in Section 4 state the consequence.

The procedure was validated on a spectrum whose answer is known. The benchmark's own best-fit model of WASP-39 b was binned directly onto the 104 bins, and separately sampled at the JWST wavelength grids of each mode with the per-point JWST errors and pushed through the conversion; the two differed by 22 ppm rms, 104 ppm at worst in a MIRI bin, and no verdict changed noise-free. This validates the conversion at near-complete coverage; at the partial coverage of the panel, the control is each planet's clear twin pushed through the same coverage mask (Section 3.4). A sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations (the individual reductions of each mode, two NIRISS pipelines, fitted and fixed limb darkening, offsets on and off, model and flat fill, with and without MIRI, PRISM alone, and the bins of Na, K, SO₂ and CO masked singly and together) measured how much the verdict depends on the reduction, which Roy-Perez et al. (48) showed must be shown rather than assumed.

A population-level comparison accompanies the verdicts, the muting census. For each real planet a clear H₂/He twin was rendered at the published abundances and bulk parameters (Table 2). The planet's muting is

M = s_twin / (s_real² − ε²)^½ (eq2)

where s_twin and s_real are the dispersions of the twin and the real spectrum over the measured bins and ε is the root mean square of the real per-bin errors. M is 1 for a spectrum as featured as its clear twin and larger when it is more muted. Its uncertainty comes from 2,000 resamples of the real depths with their errors. The most muted atmosphere the population can draw at those bulk parameters (the coldest allowed temperature with the deepest allowed deck) says whether the real muting lies inside the simulator's prior, and the same muting was computed for every planet of POP-I against its own clear twin.

Three controls use the same twins (`panel_mechanism.py`, `panel_control.py`). In the mask control the all-input design reads the twin through the reduction's own mask, scattered 200 times with the real per-bin errors. A label passes if the design gets it right in at least half of the draws, so that the mask alone cannot be blamed for a miss. In the transplants one block of the real spectrum is replaced by the twin's, shifted to the real mean over the measured AIRS bins: the three optical points, Ariel's NIRSpec channel, both, only the filled bins below 1.95 µm, only the measured bins below 1.95 µm, or the AIRS bins. In the scaled-twin test the twin is scaled about its mean so that its dispersion over the measured bins equals the real noise-corrected one, and is then read with the real errors, and with those errors plus the Tier-1 noise. Because the input is normalised per spectrum, scaling alone changes only the ratio of feature to noise.

### 2.7 The routing method: flags, remedies and how they were scored

The method has two flags, two remedies and a withheld verdict. It needs the catalogue's bulk parameters and the spectrum, and no retrieval or published composition; it does assume an H₂/He scale height and a correct catalogue mass.

**The remedies.** The first remedy is the design retrained on a population extended in aerosols. Three training sets were built from POP-III. In the haze set a third of the training planets carry a column-filling Lee-Mie haze with density log-uniform over 10⁶-3 × 10⁸ m⁻³. The haze+deck set adds, on a further sixth of the planets, a grey deck at 5-500 Pa, up to two decades above the prior's 5 × 10² Pa limit. The union set adds at fit time stellar spots at 10 or 20 % coverage on a sixth of the planets and doubled or tripled noise on another sixth, drawn independently, to ask whether covering every named departure buys anything over the two aerosol departures. The designs trained on them are the haze-retrained, haze+deck-retrained and union-retrained designs, each fitted exactly as the re-implemented classifier with the same binning, noise draw and normalisation; the method uses the haze-retrained one. These sets differ from the per-departure retraining of Section 2.3, which trains on the exact departure and measures the irreducible loss, and from the randomised grid: those two are measurements, and these are remedies. The second remedy is the AIRS-only variant (Section 2.3), used only where a flag fires.

**The shape flag.** Four candidate diagnostics were chosen before the runs: (a) the input disagreement between the three variants of the design; (b) the optical-excess score; (c) band-metric consistency, the disagreement between a learner's probability and the consortium's band-metric flag (7); and (d) predicted decidability, a regressor on bulk features trained on the Δχ² of Section 2.4. For a molecule and a learner with present-probabilities p_all, p_no and p_AIRS from the three variants, the input disagreement is

D = max(|p_all − p_no|, |p_all − p_AIRS|) (eq3)

and the optical-excess score, with z the normalised spectrum, is

E = |(mean z over the three optical points − mean z over Ariel's NIRSpec channel) − μ| / s (eq4)

where μ and s are the mean and standard deviation of that difference on the clean training set. Each has a clean threshold, its 90th percentile on the clean test spectra. The shape flag fires on a cell (a molecule and a learner) when D or E exceeds its threshold, because on the real panel they fire on different reductions (Section 3.5). D is also split by level of the nested inputs, D₁ = |p_all − p_no| and D₂ = |p_no − p_AIRS|, each at its own clean threshold, to ask whether the disagreement locates the departure in the inputs that differ between the two variants (`nested_router.py`). Each candidate was scored by selective credit as in Section 2.3, by its area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC; 0.5 is chance) for separating the two error classes, and, used as a router, by the accuracy and recall of the classifier when flagged planets are re-scored by the AIRS-only variant. The optical-excess score was designed from the haze mechanism of Section 3.3 and is evaluated on the same planets under the same haze; the real panel and the confined hazes are its only tests on data it was not built from.

**The amplitude flag.** The flag reads the mismatch the real giants showed (Section 3.4). It is the ratio

R = A₅ / (s² − ε²)^½ (eq5)

where A₅ is the five-scale-height modulation, computed from the catalogue's mass, radius and equilibrium temperature with an H₂/He mean molecular weight, as in the payload noise model (Section 2.2), and the denominator is the noise-corrected dispersion of the measured bins. The muting census of Section 2.6 is its reference: the census reads the same dispersion against a clear twin at the published composition, which a pipeline does not have. Two cuts are read from POP-I through the same bins. The upper threshold is the largest ratio of any clear POP-I planet, so a planet above it is more muted, per scale height, than any clear simulated planet read the same way. The lower cut is the first percentile of POP-I with its own decks, so a planet below it is more featured than 99 % of the population. A spectrum with no dispersion above its errors has no ratio and takes no flag. In simulation all 104 bins are measured and the thresholds are 5.25 and 0.47. A first form of the flag used full-bin thresholds, and its statistic was chosen after its values on the panel had been seen. The per-reduction rule and its sensitivities were committed to the repository before they were computed (Appendix C, rows F1-F3), but after all three batches were known, so the panel is not an independent test of the flag.

**The rule.** The amplitude flag is applied first: a planet above the upper threshold goes to the haze-retrained design, and a planet below the lower cut has its verdict withheld. On every other planet, a cell the shape flag marks takes the AIRS-only variant, and any other cell the all-input design. The verdict is the majority of the four learners.

**How the designs were scored.** Every design is scored on three tiers of evidence. In simulation: mean accuracy over the four learners and four molecules on POP-I clean and under each departure, from one evaluation of all designs on the same test cases and noise draw (`pipeline_sim.py`), a withheld label counting one half. On the real panel: all 66 labels the literature decides, one point for a right verdict, none for a wrong one and a half for a split or withheld one, so that no design gains by leaving labels unscored, with a 95 % bootstrap interval on each difference over the 23 planets, reductions of one planet being resampled together (`pipeline_table.py`). This is done at JWST precision and at Tier-1 noise, where each of 500 draws is scored and every flag is recomputed from the noisy spectrum, with the JWST and Tier-1 noise removed from the dispersion in (eq5) (`panel_tier1.py`). The intervals are descriptive, since about twenty router variants were compared on this panel before the rule was fixed. In the selection: every design in the simulation of Section 2.8, at 20, 40, 60 and 80 % Tier-2 slots.

### 2.8 Retrieval fallbacks and the selection simulation

Two fallback retrievals are defined on the hazed spectra. The retrieval of Section 2.4 run unchanged on a hazed spectrum is the "clear-model retrieval", the existing method applied to a departed spectrum. The same retrieval with the haze added to its forward model as an eighth free parameter, its number density log-uniform over 10³ to 10¹⁰ m⁻³, where the lower bound is haze-free to below a part per million, is the "free-haze retrieval", the fallback a pipeline could run on any spectrum without knowing whether it is hazed. Both were run on all 954 hazed spectra.

The mission-level measure is a selection simulation. The 955 POP-I planets receive 60 % Tier-2 slots, 573 (4), ranked by the mean present-probability of the three learners that give one (KNN, MLP and RFC; the one-vs-one SVC does not); the score is the number of planets carrying the molecule that are not selected, for CH₄, for H₂O and for either ("either-molecule targets"). Ranking by a molecule's probability is a proxy for a selection the mission will make by science theme and signal-to-noise (4). Strategies compared at the same budget: the classifier alone; the classifier with each existing rule (declined planets sent to the back of the queue); the classifier routed by each candidate to the AIRS-only variant; the band metric alone; and a retrieval of every planet, ranking by its present-posterior, with its cost stated. A budget variant re-scores by the retrieval only the fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect, for *f* from 10 to 70 %, and compares each rule to a random budget (200 draws), to the measured Δχ² of Section 2.4 used as an ordering, which starts from the true parameters and is therefore not attainable, and to the retrieval's own undecidability, the unattainable best ordering. The single-departure simulations use one noise draw. The retrievals are of noise-free spectra and the classifier reads a noisy one, a difference Section 3.6 measures on the 200-planet subsample.

The mixed-population test applies the same budget where the retrieval shares the classifier's failure. Each of the 954 planets with a hazed spectrum is hazed with probability *h* (0.1, 0.2 or 0.5; 20 draws); the fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect (10 to 50 %) is re-scored by the free-haze retrieval and the rest keep the classifier; the selection is the 60 % cut above, and the clear-model retrieval is run in the free-haze retrieval's place for comparison. The orderings are random, the five existing rules, the optical-excess score, the input disagreement, band-metric consistency, and an oracle that knows which planets are hazed and nothing else; the fixed strategies are the classifier alone, the optical-excess score routed to the AIRS-only variant without any retrieval, the clear-model retrieval of every planet, and that retrieval re-run with the haze density free wherever its evidence fails. The metrics are targets lost at the selection, with their spread over the draws, the pipeline's accuracy, and its recall on the hazed and on the clean targets. The haze is at 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³, which the optical-excess score flags on every hazed planet (Section 3.5), so the oracle is a haze detector at a density where detection saturates. A second point of the dose-response was run at 2 × 10⁶ m⁻³, the lowest density at which the stress test measured a large loss (12.1 points in Table 4, against 1.4 at 2 × 10⁵ m⁻³), by re-rendering the population at that density and repeating the test; its no-retrieval strategies and the detection of the hazed planets by each rule are reported. Expectations for this test were committed after a pilot of the same test on the 124 planets whose fixed-density hazed posteriors existed first, and before any population result existed (Appendix C). The clean planets a budget reaches are scored by the clean retrieval of Section 2.4, the correctly specified model for them; the free-haze retrieval of clean spectra exists so far for 27 planets.

### 2.9 Expectations committed before the runs

Expectations were written to the repository before their runs for the external test, the identifiability measurements, the error classes, the diagnostics, the mixed-population test, the per-reduction flag and the confined hazes, so that surprises can be told from confirmations. They are listed with their outcomes in Appendix C, including the ones not met and the ones never run, with a list of what was fixed before what on the real panel (Table C3). Those for the stress test, which predate them, and the panel's per-planet expected verdicts remain in the repository. The muting census, the retrained designs and the routers had no prior expectation.

A third to a half of the labels are undecidable at the 0.95 level: a third of the CH₄ and NH₃ labels, 44 % of the CO₂ labels and 54 % of the H₂O labels (Table 3). Part of this is the threshold itself. A fifth of the labels lie within half a decade of 10⁻⁴, and nine in ten of those are undecidable, so they supply 17-20 points of each prevalence; away from the threshold the undecidable fractions are 18, 44, 33 and 19 %. Decidability is set first by that distance and second by the planet's noise: labels one to two decades from the boundary are undecidable in 12-36 % of cases, and absent labels two to three decades below it in 1-8 % for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ but in 31 % for H₂O, on the planets whose noise leaves water unconstrained at any abundance. With the prior's head start removed the fractions rise by 3-8 points, and present labels are undecidable more often than absent ones for every molecule. Retrieved under POP-I's own prior on the 200-planet subsample, the undecidable fraction rises from 41 to 46 %. The prevalence is therefore a property of the named prior to within five points. Only 17 % of the planets have all four labels decidable, 3 % have none, and a third have both CH₄ and H₂O decidable. At Tier-1 precision under this prior, the presence of water, Ariel's primary molecule, above 10⁻⁴ is undecided for half the planets.

The classifier sits 9-16 points below this ceiling even inside its simulator. The retrieval scores 92-94 % on every molecule on the noise-free spectra, 97-98 % on absent labels, where the prior already supplies 0.71, and 82-88 % on present labels (Table 3). Among the classifier's clean errors only 19-25 % fall on labels the spectrum decides; three quarters to four fifths fall on undecidable labels, and effectively none on a label the spectrum decides the other way. On decidable labels the learners score 91-98 % for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and 76-93 % for H₂O; on undecidable ones 58-70 %, a few points above the majority class. Recall of present labels is 82-84 % (H₂O 65 %) where the label is decidable and 35-49 % (H₂O 27 %) where it is not: the classifier's default on an uninformative spectrum is "absent", which is the prior, and Section 3.4 meets that default on real spectra.

**Noisy realisations.** On the 200 random planets retrieved on the noisy spectrum the classifier reads, the population figures held and the labels under them moved. The decidable fractions changed by at most 6 points, but 5-11 % of labels lost decidability under the draw and 5-12 % gained it, and the correlation of p_true between the two runs was 0.67-0.79. The retrieval's accuracy on the realisation was 1.5-6 points lower, and labels with p_true below 0.05, of which the noise-free population has one in 3,820, appeared on 8 of 800: on data, a calibrated posterior is confidently wrong about 1 % of the time. Of the four learners' 590 errors on these realisations, 18.5 % are recoverable by the noise-free posterior and 14.9 % are decidable by the noisy one; the noisy retrieval gets 60 % of them right at p ≥ 0.5 (the noise-free one 73 %), most of that from the prior's absent default. The recoverable share defined by the noise-free posterior is therefore an upper bound that overstates by about a fifth of itself on clean spectra; the hazed-spectrum runs on the same realisations are in Section 3.6.

**The Δχ² test used under departures.** Section 3.3 needs a test that can be rerun with a departure in the counterfactual model, so the Δχ² test of Section 2.4 was calibrated against this posterior. The two agreed in rank: Spearman correlation between Δχ² and logit p_true of 0.86 (CH₄), 0.80 (H₂O), 0.88 (CO₂) and 0.89 (NH₃) over the 955 planets. The test's scale, however, differed by an order of magnitude from the conventional one. The posterior crosses 0.95 at Δχ² = 0.45 (CH₄), 0.98 (H₂O), 0.90 (CO₂) and 0.56 (NH₃), not at 3.84, the χ² value for one parameter at 95 %, or 9, the 3σ value. The reason is that the test represents the opposite class by its single best-fitting point, whereas the retrieval integrates over that class's volume. In plain terms, a label is decidable when the best opposite-class fit sits only about one σ away in total χ², a statement about the prior's volume as much as about the evidence. At the conventional 3σ the test would put 78-88 % of labels in the undecidable class, the value the calibration replaces. The calibrated thresholds are used everywhere below, and the test is a check on the retrieval rather than a substitute: at 207 core-hours against the retrieval's 365 on this population, it is not the cheap method it was meant to be.

The largest loss has a mechanism, which the shape flag of Section 2.7 reads. Under the column-filling haze at 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³, the MLP, RFC and SVC learners reported CH₄ on 0-5 % of planets (26-33 % on clean spectra; 38 % truly present) and their H₂O detection rate fell to about 0 %, while the MLP's mean confidence rose from 0.95 to 1.00. Four versions of the design that differed only in their input located the cause (Table 5). Normalising with the bins above 1.1 µm alone changed nothing, so the failure is in the points themselves and not in the normalisation. Removing the optical points cut the haze loss from 17.7 to 5.6 points and the spot loss from 10.8 to 3.2; using only the AIRS bins cut the haze loss to 0.5 and removed the spot loss. The two transplant experiments of Section 2.3 confirm the attribution: copying only the three hazy optical points into clean spectra reproduced the failure (CH₄ detection 8.8 %, against 11.6 % under haze and 72.3 % clean), and restoring clean points in hazy spectra removed it (73.5 %). The optical points do carry information, since removing them cost 3.8 points on clean spectra and using AIRS alone 7.4, so the AIRS-only variant is a fallback with a price, not a replacement.

The no-optical design loses 1.3, 2.5 and 6.7 points, and the AIRS-only design 0.1, 0.2 and 0.9. The sizes change and the ordering does not: the failure enters through the optical points, whatever the haze's vertical extent. What does change is how much the no-optical design recovers: where the haze slope is confined to the optical points, it is the most accurate of the three input variants under the two weaker hazes (76.1 and 74.9 %).

With the departure in the counterfactual model, haze and spots leave the labels about as decidable as on clean spectra and a high deck does not (Figure 3a; Table 6). Under the column-filling haze at 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³ the haze costs water a little, because its bands sit under the near-infrared slope the haze adds, and makes CO₂ more decidable, because with the density known the slope pins the scale height and removes part of the temperature-abundance degeneracy. Under 20 % unocculted spots, with the spots in the counterfactual at their true coverage and temperature, the decidable fractions move by 0.5 to 2.5 points: the contamination deepens the transit by a few per cent at every wavelength, and a test that knows it loses nothing, which bounds what a test that must fit it would keep. A grey deck at 10² Pa, above the prior's limit of 5 × 10² Pa, is different. With the deck at its true pressure in the counterfactual, the decidable fraction falls to 70 % for CH₄, 28 % for H₂O, 53 % for CO₂ and 63 % for NH₃. The fall is almost entirely on absent labels (H₂O absent labels decidable 226 → 93 of 591, present 194 → 173 of 364), because the deck hides the deep atmosphere where a small abundance would have shown, so the floor on an absence rises. That is information the atmosphere removes, as noise is information the instrument removes.

### 3.4 Real JWST spectra: what the classifier finds and where it fails

**WASP-39 b.** At JWST precision with all 104 inputs, three of the four learners called H₂O present and all four called CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at 10⁻⁴, which agrees with the published composition (24, 25, 30, 76) on every label the literature decides. CO₂ at 10⁻⁵, where its published abundance of about 10⁻⁴·⁵ puts it, was present for two to three learners; NH₃ was absent in all 24 configurations of the sensitivity matrix and with each of three MIRI reductions; and the consortium's own band metric agreed with the majority. The MLP called H₂O absent with a score of 0.00, in 23 of the 24 configurations, and on the benchmark's own best-fit model binned directly onto the 104 bins with no conversion at all, so the miss is not the conversion's. Nor is it the composition or a grey deck: the clear H₂/He twin at the published abundances is called H₂O-present by all four learners (MLP score 1.00), as are twins with grey decks at pressures down to 10 Pa. Under Ariel Tier-1 noise at WASP-39 b's one transit, H₂O is detected in 58 % of draws with all inputs and 86 % without the three optical points, and the clear twin in 100 %.

**The panel.** Over all 26 reductions the literature decides 66 labels, 40 absent and 26 present, so calling every label absent would score 40. At JWST precision with all inputs the classifier was right on 39 of the absent labels and 3 of the present ones (the H₂O of WASP-39 b, WASP-17 b and WASP-94A b), wrong on 17 and split on 7, for a score of 45.5 with a split counted as half. Without the optical points it scored 50.0, and with the AIRS channels alone 52.5, reproducing 14 present labels. On the fifteen reductions with measured data both below 1.1 µm and above 1.95 µm the three scores are 28.5, 32.0 and 34.5 of 40. By batch they are 25.5, 28.0 and 31.5 of 40 on the first, 14.5, 16.0 and 16.0 of 19 on the second, and 5.5, 6.0 and 5.0 of 7 on the third, so most of the gain from dropping the optical points lies in the first batch, which holds six of the eight methane reductions. At Tier-1 noise the three scores fall to 41.7, 43.1 and 43.4 (Table 9), and the re-implemented classifier finds 3.0 of the 26 present labels on average over the draws, 1.7 labels above calling everything absent.

**What the coverage mask does.** Bins with no data are filled flat, and that alone misleads the all-input design. Of the 24 present labels with a clear twin, the all-input design misses 11 on the twin itself, read through the reduction's own mask at the real errors; the AIRS-only variant misses 4 (Section 2.6). These eleven labels fail the mask control: the H₂O of WASP-107 b, HAT-P-26 b, GJ 3470 b, WASP-166 b, WASP-80 b and WASP-94A b, the CO₂ of TOI-270 d, TOI-1231 b and WASP-166 b, and the CH₄ and CO₂ of TOI-270 d in the Holmberg reduction. No absent label fails. A miss on these labels cannot be attributed to the real atmosphere, although with full coverage the same twins of the AIRS-only planets are found by all four learners. They stay in the count because a pipeline would not know, and every design is also scored on the 50 labels that pass, 37 absent and 13 present (Table 9). Two further misses have other causes. GJ 9827 d's H₂O is absent with all inputs and with the AIRS channels, and split without the optical points, although its twin through the same NIRISS mask is found in 99 % of draws, because the real spectrum shows no dispersion above its errors in 63 % of resamples. HIP 67522 b's CO₂ is called present by every learner, every input and every training set, for a reason the muting census gives below. No NH₃ false positive occurred on any panel planet with all inputs; with the AIRS channels alone WASP-52 b's NH₃ is called present where its retrieval reports no evidence but sets no limit, so the cell is ambiguous and not scored.

**The methane planets.** With all inputs the classifier called CH₄ absent on seven of the eight reductions of TOI-270 d, K2-18 b, TOI-1231 b, GJ 1214 b and TOI-732 c, and split on the eighth (Table 7). Under Tier-1 noise the detection frequencies are 4-22 %, and a majority of learners reports methane in at most 2 % of draws. The detections are reported at 4.2-10.8σ on TOI-270 d and K2-18 b (31, 32, 34, 35, 77), with a log Bayes factor of 55-70 on TOI-1231 b (36), and at or below about 3σ on the last two (83, 84). Counted per planet, by majority over reductions, it missed four and split on TOI-1231 b. With the AIRS channels alone it found CH₄ on six of eight and on four of the five planets, missing GJ 1214 b, whose detection is the weakest of the set and whose retrieved atmosphere is CO₂-dominated, a composition the H₂/He grid does not contain. The eight reductions are five planets, so the comparison has an effective sample of five; it is reported as a pattern, not a rate.

Three tests locate these misses. The clear twins, through the same mask at the real per-bin errors, are found by a majority of learners in 63-100 % of draws on six of the seven reductions with a twin, so those misses are not the noise or the classifier's skill inside its simulator. The exception is the Holmberg reduction of TOI-270 d, with 63 filled bins, whose twin is never found, so its miss is the coverage. In the transplants, replacing every real bin below 1.95 µm with the twin's brings all seven reductions to a majority of learners, and replacing the AIRS bins brings three: the miss enters below 1.95 µm, the range the AIRS-only learner does not read. Replaced alone, the three optical points repair five and Ariel's NIRSpec channel four, and the filled bins alone four and the measured bins alone four, so neither the fill nor the measured points carry the whole miss. The optical points are not sufficient as an explanation either: no methane reduction has all three of them measured, two have none, removing them recovers methane on none of the eight, and the optical-excess score fires on two of the eight. This is therefore a failure in the inputs below 1.95 µm, related to the simulated haze mechanism of Section 3.3 but not the same. These are recoverable errors in the operational sense that another learner reading the same spectrum finds the label. The k-NN distance rule flags five of the eight reductions and puts the other three at the 90th percentile of clean distances.

**Muting.** Twelve warm giants and Neptune-mass planets on the panel have a published abundance set ("the twelve giants" below): HAT-P-26 b, WASP-107 b, WASP-39 b, GJ 3470 b and WASP-166 b from the first batch; WASP-17 b, WASP-80 b, HD 209458 b, WASP-96 b and WASP-52 b from the second; WASP-94A b and HAT-P-12 b from the third. Ten of them are muted by a factor of 2.3 to 5.3 against their clear twins (Figure 4; 68 % intervals 0.1-1.2 wide), and in every resample they lie 1.7 to 2.7 times beyond the most muted H₂/He atmosphere the population allows at their mass, radius, irradiation and published composition. The same holds over the AIRS bins alone, so the muting is in the molecular bands and not only in a haze slope. WASP-39 b is the worked example. Its real dispersion over the measured bins is 324 ppm against 1,190 ppm for the clear twin, a muting of 3.7 (68 % interval 3.67-3.79), where the most muted atmosphere the population can draw at its bulk parameters reaches 2.0. Yet it is inside the training distribution by every input-space rule (k-NN distance 6.2 against the decline threshold 11.25), because those rules work on normalised spectra and normalisation removes amplitude. The two giants inside the prior are the two least muted, WASP-96 b (1.5, 1.4-1.6; the clearest water feature of the set, 88) and WASP-52 b (2.3, 2.2-2.4, against a prior maximum of 2.4).

The population's own clouds mute its median planet by 1 %, its 99th percentile by a factor of 2.5 and its most muted planet by 6.3, at the prior's 5 × 10² Pa limit (Figure 4b). The simulator can therefore reach the giants' muting, but in about one planet in a hundred or fewer (0.1-1.2 %) and only near that limit, whereas ten of the twelve real giants sit there. Reaching it in numbers needs what the population does not contain: metallicities of tens to hundreds of times solar, hazes or patchy clouds, which is what their JWST retrievals report. The four H₂-dominated methane planets are muted by only 0.7-2.0, inside the population's range, although three of their seven reductions would need a deck above the prior's limit at their own bulk parameters (Table 7). In both groups the real spectra carry smaller features, relative to their continua, than the clear-grid training spectra, and the classifier's verdict on real spectra is mostly "absent", which is also its default on an uninformative simulated spectrum (Section 3.2).

The scaled-twin test separates scale from shape. Normalisation removes pure scaling, so at JWST precision a twin scaled to the real dispersion is found as often as the unscaled twin: on the 23 present labels whose real spectrum has measurable dispersion, 11 against 12 with all inputs, and 19 against 19 with the AIRS channels alone. Scale is therefore not what the learners miss at that precision; the real spectra differ from their twins in shape as well as in size. At Tier-1 noise scale does cost labels, because the noise does not scale with the features: 4 against 8 of the 23 with all inputs, and 5 against 14 with the AIRS channels alone. Muting therefore lowers what Tier-1 can find on a real planet, whatever the input.

The third batch showed the mirror image. HIP 67522 b is a 17 Myr Jupiter-radius planet of 14 Earth masses (93), its scale height over ten times what the catalogue's mass gives the simulator, so a trace abundance produces a feature the classifier reads as a 10⁻⁴ one. It is nine times more featured than its clear twin at the catalogue's parameters, and its CO₂ is called present although the published abundance is 10⁻⁵·⁶.

### 3.5 A routing method

Both failures of Section 3.4 are recoverable in the operational sense that another input or another training population finds the label in the same spectrum, and the method addresses only those. A spectrum that does not decide a label stays undecided: the method neither recognises nor withholds such labels, and Section 3.6 measures what recognising them is worth. The method's rule is stated in Section 2.7. Its elements are measured one at a time below, then together on the real panel (Table 9), in simulation (Table 10) and in the selection (Table 11).

**The remedies.** The muting census places eleven reductions beyond the prior, ten of them in more than 95 % of resamples. Sent only to these eleven, the haze+deck-retrained design changes five scored labels from split or absent to present: the CO₂ of HAT-P-26 b and GJ 3470 b, the H₂O and CO₂ of WASP-166 b and the H₂O of WASP-80 b. On those planets' 30 scored labels the score rises from 23.0 (21 right, 5 wrong, 4 split) to 26.5 (26, 3, 1), and nothing else that is scored changes. The haze-retrained design also calls HAT-P-12 b's water present (27.0; 27, 3, 0). Among the methane reductions (Table 8), the three that would need a grey deck above the prior's limit at the twin's temperature (K2-18 b in both reanalyses and TOI-1231 b, decks at 47-238 Pa) are the three on which retraining moved the verdict, two to present and one to split under each training set. The four that need no such deck it did not move, apart from a split on the Madhusudhan K2-18 b reduction under the haze+deck set, and it did not move GJ 1214 b, which has no twin to place. Used on every planet, the haze-retrained design raises the all-input panel score from 45.5 of 66 to 50.5, and the haze+deck-retrained design to 52.0. The AIRS-only variant finds six of the eight methane reductions with no new training population, so it remains the cheaper remedy for those planets.

In simulation, the haze set recovers two thirds of the CH₄ loss under the column-filling haze (66 → 79 % against 85 % clean), three quarters of the CO₂ loss, half of the NH₃ loss and two fifths of the H₂O loss, leaves the noise × 3 column unchanged, and costs 1-2 points on clean spectra. Under the confined hazes, which it did not see, it recovers 54, 48 and 38 % of the all-input loss, and the haze+deck set 61, 59 and 46 %. Covering further departures buys nothing: the union-retrained design is within 0.3 points of the haze+deck-retrained one on every simulated case and scores the same 52.0 on the panel, because the multiplicative spot factor is largely removed by the per-spectrum normalisation and the noise is irreducible at fixed precision. Classed by the clean-spectrum posterior, retraining's gain is the removal of recoverable errors down to the undecidable floor. Under the column-filling haze, the haze+deck-retrained design removes 315 of the re-implemented classifier's 675 recoverable errors (mean over the four learners, summed over molecules) and 71 of its 719 undecidable ones. On clean spectra it adds 31 recoverable errors, and under twofold noise it moves nothing. The undecidable count stays at 560-730 in every case for both designs, which is the floor Section 3.2 predicts.

**The shape flag in simulation.** The two scores fire on different departures (Table D2). The optical-excess score flags every planet under the column-filling haze, 76-97 % under the confined hazes and two thirds of the spotted ones at its 10 % clean rate, and almost no clouded or noisier ones. The input disagreement fires on 9 % of clean planets. That localisation does not buy accuracy. A three-level router that sends a cell to the largest input set the two levels agree on costs 5.1 points on clean spectra against 4.6 for the shape flag and is within a point of it under every departure. Routing on D₁ alone, to the no-optical variant, halves the clean cost (2.4 points) and is the best router at the weaker density (76.2 %) but the worst under the compound departure (69.2 against 73.5 %), so the input set to fall back on depends on how far the departure reaches.

**The shape flag on the panel.** The two scores flag different misses (Table 8). The optical-excess score does not depend on the molecule. It flags HAT-P-18 b (the spotted host) and two of the three K2-18 b reductions; routed by it alone, the classifier recovers methane on those two reductions and scores 47.5 of 66, against 45.5. The input disagreement fires on the CH₄ cell on five of the eight reductions; routed by it alone, the classifier recovers methane on four and scores 50.5. The shape flag uses both. It recovers methane on five of the eight reductions (a sixth becomes split; the Felix reduction of TOI-270 d and GJ 1214 b stay absent) and scores 51.5. Five labels go from wrong to right and none the other way, two go for the worse (HD 189733 b's CO₂, absent to split, and HAT-P-12 b's H₂O, split to absent), and three of the improvements lie outside the methane set (GJ 3470 b's CO₂, WASP-166 b's CO₂ and WASP-96 b's H₂O). On the second batch, which the shape flag had not seen, it routed TOI-732 c's methane from absent to present and WASP-96 b's water from split to present, and no label changed for the worse, raising that batch from 14.5 to 16.0 of 19. On the third it lost HAT-P-12 b's water, 5.5 to 5.0 of 7.

**The amplitude flag.** Read through each reduction's own coverage (Table D1), the ratio exceeds its threshold on twelve of the 26 reductions. Eight of them are among the ten giants the muting census places beyond the prior; the other two giants, HAT-P-26 b and HD 209458 b, fall below their thresholds. The other four are HAT-P-18 b, the census's marginal case (beyond the prior in 82 % of resamples), TOI-1231 b, just above at 5.44 against 5.09, and L 98-59 d and GJ 1214 b, which have no clear twin and whose flat spectra (ratios 11 and 63) reflect mean molecular weights far above the H₂/He the flag assumes. The panel score depends little on its form: with the clear 99th percentile as the threshold (14 reductions flagged) it is 54.0, with the own-deck 99th percentile or the AIRS range alone (6 flagged, four of them giants) 53.0, and with the first full-bin form (17 flagged) 53.5. At Tier-1 noise the flag is itself noise-limited: it fires in more than half of the draws on only six of the twelve (WASP-39 b, WASP-107 b, WASP-17 b, WASP-94A b, HAT-P-12 b and HAT-P-18 b). In simulation the flag changes little. At Tier-1 noise it fires on 6.8 % of clean planets, 7.9 % show no dispersion above their noise and take no flag, and accuracy moves by 0.1 points, because every simulated departure stays inside the amplitude range the training decks span. Its lower cut has a false alarm: under threefold noise beyond the nominal, the excess dispersion reads as amplitude and a quarter of the planets are withheld.

### 3.6 The retrieval as fallback, against the existing methods

This section asks a separate question from Section 3.5. The routing method needs no retrieval and was not run in the retrieval budgets below. The retrieval is the existing method a classifier is measured against and the fallback a flag can route to, and the question here is whether it shares the classifier's failure, what repairs it, and what a flag buys at a fixed retrieval budget, on clean spectra and where the departure is present. Unless stated, the haze here is the column-filling one.

**The clear-model retrieval under haze.** The clear-model retrieval shares the classifier's failure on hazed spectra and fails more confidently (Table 12). It did not complete on 18 of the 954 planets, against none of 955 on clean spectra, and on the rest it called almost every present label absent. Recall of present labels fell below 1 % for all four molecules, the posterior of the true class was below 0.05 on 99 % of present labels, and every abundance was driven 3.5-4 dex toward the prior floor. The misfit is visible in the evidence, which fell from a median log Z of −12 on clean spectra to −252. An analyst looking at the fit would see it, but a pipeline that reads only the posterior would not; it is the aerosol misspecification mode known from retrievals of real spectra (16, 96, 97). Used as a gate, the evidence catches nearly every hazed planet: 99.6 % of them fall below the first percentile of the clean evidences. The departure is largest where the classifier's was: 18σ per bin in the three optical points, 4.2σ over Ariel's NIRSpec channel and 1.9σ over AIRS (medians over the population, with the offset removed).

With the haze density added as one free parameter, the same retrieval completed on every spectrum, recovered the density on every planet, recovered 222 of the 224 planets with CH₄ present and decidable on the clean spectrum and kept 75 % of them decidable, which is the price of fitting the departure's strength rather than knowing it. Its accuracy stayed within 5 points of the clean retrieval on every molecule, and it fit with the clean evidence. This repair is a best case, since the truth lies inside the one-parameter family: particle size, extinction law and vertical extent are all the truth's. Four planets, among them the super-puff Kepler-79 d, fit badly with the radius held fixed and still carried the right labels but one. Figure 3c shows the two retrievals side by side: the haze removes nothing the labels need, and the clear-model retrieval's near-zero recall on the same spectra comes from the model.

Both results hold on the classifier's own noisy realisation of the 200-planet subsample (Section 2.4). The free-haze retrieval moved as the clean retrieval had: decidable fractions by 0-4 points, 7-10 % of labels out of decidability and 8-11 % in, accuracy down 3.5-6.5 points, confidently wrong labels up from 2 to 12 of 800, and of the classifier's 1,177 errors on that realisation it got 72 % right against 79 % for the noise-free run, so the repair holds on the realisation a pipeline would see and not only on the noise-free spectrum. The clear-model retrieval did not move: every present label stayed absent with p_true below 10⁻³ on all 196 planets that completed, no label changed decidability, and the posterior of the true class was the same between the two runs to three decimals. The evidence fell a further 47 in median log Z as the fit absorbed noise it could not explain. The noise draw moves the labels of a retrieval whose model is right by a few points and leaves those of a retrieval whose model is wrong where the model put them.

**A retrieval budget on clean spectra.** On clean spectra the retrieval is the selection method, and no cheap rule allocates a partial budget much better than chance. At 60 % Tier-2 slots, ranking by the retrieval's present-posterior loses 0 of 359 CH₄-bearing targets (a truth oracle 0; classifier 20), 2 of 364 H₂O targets (oracle 0; classifier 51) and 39 either-molecule targets (oracle 3; classifier 95). On noise-free spectra under its own forward model a correctly specified retrieval is the Bayes-optimal selector, so this is the expected result, and its value is as the reference. A retrieval of every planet took 365 core-hours for this seven-parameter model matched to the generator, and takes more for richer physics. The comparison does not rest on the noise-free spectrum: on the 200-planet subsample at 60 % slots, the retrieval of the classifier's noisy realisation loses 1 of 74 CH₄ targets and 2 of 80 H₂O targets, the noise-free one 0 and 1, and the classifier 1 and 12. When only the fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect is re-scored by the retrieval (Table C2), the k-NN and Mahalanobis distances beat a random budget by 2-4 CH₄ targets at *f* = 20-30 %, between one and a half and three times the random draw's standard deviation, and nothing else does. The confidence-based rules are no better than random at any *f*. For H₂O only predicted decidability beats random, by 4-5 targets at *f* = 20-30 %, and every other rule is within noise or worse. The confidence rules send the retrieval to the planets the classifier is unsure about, which are the undecidable labels, where the retrieval cannot help either. The retrieval pays off on the planets the classifier is confidently wrong about, and no rule finds those.

**Recognising the undecidable class.** Recognising this class is worth a withheld verdict, not a retrieval. Predicting decidability from bulk features reached an AUROC of only 0.61-0.66 for undecidable labels (0.47-0.63 for separating the error classes). The classifier's own margin and ensemble spread recognise the undecidable labels with AUROC 0.71-0.77 for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and 0.59-0.61 for H₂O (the distance rules reach 0.68 for H₂O). A random forest trained out of fold on the spectrum and bulk features, with the retrieval's labels as targets, reaches 0.69-0.74: it ties them on CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and beats them on H₂O (0.74 against 0.59-0.68). The ceiling of any such recogniser is the measured Δχ² ordering, which at a 20 % budget loses 8 CH₄ targets against 12 for a random ordering and 7 for the retrieval's own posterior. Knowing which labels are undecidable is therefore worth about four targets in 359, because those are the planets the retrieval cannot help either. Declining the 30 % of planets the margin ranks least confident raises the classifier's CH₄ accuracy on the rest from 86 to 95 %, and that is where recognising the class pays. Nor do the undecided planets all fit the slots. For CH₄, the 224 planets decided present and the 310 undecided fit the 573 Tier-2 slots, and ranking by the posterior loses no target. For H₂O they number 156 and 518, 101 more than the slots, and for either molecule 809, 236 more.

**The existing rules under the shift.** Nor do the five rules recognise the haze failure (Table 13). Every rule's selective credit was negative under haze (−17.5 to −25.7 points), spots and the high deck. The margin rule declined 7.4 % of clean planets at the threshold set for 10 % but 0.9 % of hazy ones. The three distance scores separated hazed from clean spectra (AUROC 0.90-0.94) but did not rank errors within them (0.44-0.52), a population alarm rather than a filter, and conformal coverage fell to 73.7 % under haze. No candidate had positive selective credit under haze or spots either (the best, the input disagreement under haze, −6.8, and the optical-excess score under spots, −4.0). The optical-excess score flags every hazed planet, and declining what it flags empties the hazy population, so credit does not measure a shift detector. Confidence does not detect failure under a shift, and the shift detectors that fire do not say which answers are wrong.

## 4. Discussion

**What the two classes mean for Tier-2 selection.** Under the training prior, a third to a half of the Tier-1 labels are not decided by the spectrum at the 0.95 level, and about four fifths of the classifier's errors in its own simulator sit there. A retrieval of such a spectrum gives a posterior near the prior, so a pipeline that reads either answer as a verdict dismisses planets the data did not judge. The undecidable fraction depends on the population, the precision and the forward model, not on the learner. For water it is half the planets at Tier-1 precision under this prior, which can be read alongside the retrieval-based detection fractions of Bocchieri et al. (50) and the tier reassessment of Radica et al. (5). About a third of it is labels within half a decade of 10⁻⁴, a line the label draws and the physics does not.

The measurements point to specific steps for a pipeline. On clean spectra a retrieval of every planet loses almost no target at 60 % slots, at 365 core-hours with this model, and no cheap rule allocates a partial budget much better than chance. Where the retrieval's forward model is wrong in the way haze makes it wrong, the retrieval fails more confidently than the classifier, and its evidence, not its posterior, gives the warning. For the planets a shift flag marks, the destination decides the outcome. For the labels the spectra do not decide, the classifier's own confidence marks most of them, except for H₂O, where a flag trained on the retrieval does better, and allocating a retrieval budget to them does not help. Ranked by the posterior, those planets follow the decided-present ones: for methane the two groups fit the 60 % slots, and for water and for either molecule they do not (Section 3.6). The absolute stakes of a flag are modest in this simulation, 16 of 359 methane targets at a 20 % budget, 3 % of the Tier-2 queue.

**Real spectra against the training prior.** The external test gave neither of the simple outcomes. The re-implemented classifier is right on 39 of 40 absent labels and finds few present ones, so at Tier-1 noise it scores 1.7 labels above calling every label absent. Three things lie behind the missed labels, and they are different in kind. The first is coverage: eleven present labels are missed on the clear twin as well, through the reduction's own mask, which says nothing about Ariel. The second is a failure in the inputs below 1.95 µm: on six of the seven methane reductions with a twin, the twin is found through the same mask and the real spectrum is not, and the twin's bins below 1.95 µm repair all seven. The input that does not read that range finds the methane, so the information is in the spectrum at JWST precision. The third is precision. Muting costs no labels at JWST precision, since normalisation removes scale, and it costs half to two thirds of them at Tier-1 noise. A muting of M lowers the Δχ² of (eq1) by about M² if it scales the features uniformly, as noise at M times σ does, so the ten muted giants (M = 2.3-5.3) sit near or beyond the noise × 3 row of Table 6, where 65-87 % of labels are undecidable. The prevalences of Section 3.2 are therefore likely to be low for real giants. A training population for real Tier-1 spectra needs the aerosols and metallicities the JWST retrievals report, and the muting census is a cheap way to check, planet by planet, whether a real spectrum lies where the prior can reach. For the sub-Neptunes with the highest mean molecular weights the clear H₂/He twin is itself an approximation, since the population contains no such atmosphere. HIP 67522 b adds the mirror image, and there the remedy is the catalogue's mass rather than the classifier.

**Why confidence rules fail and when a shift flag helps.** The five decline rules did not recognise the haze failure because the classifier is confident on hazed spectra, and the three distance rules that do detect the shift rank errors within it no better than chance. This is the pattern reported for image classifiers under dataset shift (51) in a physical setting, and the selective-risk evaluation argued for by Jaeger et al. (70) is what makes it visible: a rule that declines the whole hazed population has no credit, because it has nothing left to be right about. The value of a shift flag is therefore in routing rather than declining, and the destination decides the outcome. Routed to the clear-model retrieval, a flag that correctly detects the shift loses more targets than no flag (71 against 29). Routed to a retrieval that models the departure, the optical-excess score, the Mahalanobis distance and an oracle that knows which planets are hazed lose the same number of targets within the draw-to-draw noise, and the score's advantage over the existing distance rules is its recall and selectivity on the hazed planets, not the selection. That retrieval-budget use was demonstrated in simulation for one departure the fallback models exactly. On real data the routing without a retrieval recovered the methane planets only once the input disagreement was added to the optical-excess score, at the clean cost Section 3.5 measures.

**What the method claims.** The routing method combines known elements, domain randomisation (66) and feature amplitude in scale heights (98-100), in a way that appears to be new. Web searches on 30 October 2026, including arXiv and the main journals, with terms combining classifier, out-of-distribution or distribution shift, routing or flag, and exoplanet transmission spectra, JWST or Ariel, found no published method that routes a simulator-trained atmospheric classifier on real spectra by such flags. The closest work diagnoses the collapse of an amortised retrieval on real WASP-39 b spectra and repairs it with a radius latent (49). The method needs no retrieval and no published composition. On the panel it was built on, it scores 8.5 labels above the re-implemented classifier at JWST precision (descriptive interval 2.8 to 14.0) and 2.7 at Tier-1 noise (1.2 to 4.3). Its limitations are these. It cannot be told apart on that panel from the AIRS-only input or a retrained design. In simulation it costs 4.9 points on clean spectra and stays below the retrained designs, in accuracy and in the selection. About two fifths of its gain at JWST precision lies on labels the coverage mask hides. It does not address the undecidable class. It was built on the panel it is scored on, and the three planets added last show no clear gain, so it has no independent test. The test that would settle the matter is a further batch of planets scored once with the rule frozen, and the rule and its thresholds are committed to the repository for that purpose.

**Beyond Ariel.** The decomposition does not depend on transmission spectroscopy. Any inference system trained on a simulator and used to make decisions produces errors of two kinds, those the data could have decided and those they could not, and the split can be measured by running the expensive method on the population the cheap one is tested on. The measurement gives three things a decision pipeline needs and a classifier cannot supply: the prevalence of undecided answers under a stated prior, the share of the cheap method's errors that any method could fix, and the test of whether a proposed fallback shares the failure. In this study the fallback, applied under the departure it does not model, lost more targets than no fallback. The study carries a second instance of the procedure. The C/O classifier of Appendix B, trained at the same binning for a different objective, was fragile where this one is robust (omitted absorbers cost it half its accuracy on carbon-rich planets) and robust where this one is fragile (haze cost it 5 points), and the same distance rules that rank errors no better than chance here ranked its errors well (AUROC 0.90-0.91). A label's class is a property of the spectrum, but the share of errors in each class and the rules that find them are properties of a classifier under a departure, so the measurement has to be repeated for each. The procedure is what transfers.

**Expectations.** Of the 39 expectations committed before their runs (Appendix C), seventeen were met as written, sixteen in part, three not met, and three were never run. The misses include WASP-39 b's water at Tier-1 precision (58 % against the 80 % expected), the undecidable fractions at the 0.95 level for three of four molecules, the selective credit of every candidate, and the predicted-decidability regressor. Two results were not among the expectations, and each changed the paper. The scale of the Δχ² test differed by an order of magnitude from the posterior, which the calibration was designed to catch. And the real giants lie outside the simulator population in feature amplitude while inside it in every bulk parameter and by every distance rule, which produced the amplitude flag and the second remedy. Measurements with no prior expectation are listed in Section 2.9.

**Limitations of the identifiability measure.** Every retrieval-based number in Sections 3.2, 3.3 and 3.6 is of the noise-free spectrum with the Tier-1 σ in the likelihood, so decidability is that of a spectrum without noise and the recoverable shares are upper bounds. The 200-planet subsample retrieved on the classifier's realisation bounds the difference on clean spectra (Section 3.2) and for the free-haze and clear-model retrievals of hazed spectra (Section 3.6). The retrieval's abundance prior is wider than the test population's, which gives absent labels a head start of 0.71 that the Bayes-factor row of Table 3 removes and that the retrieval under the population's own prior measures at five points of prevalence (Section 3.2); the ceiling of 92-94 % includes that default. The decision scale itself is weak: the posterior crosses 0.95 at a Δχ² of only 0.45-0.98, because the prior's volume does much of the work, and a fifth of the labels lie within half a decade of the threshold. The retrieval holds the planetary radius fixed with only a small depth offset free, and the radius-abundance-cloud degeneracy (101) is the dominant nuisance of absolute-abundance retrieval, so the undecidable fractions are lower bounds and the ceiling an upper bound under that model. The forward model is the training model throughout, and a retrieval with a free radius and a full aerosol model is what a consortium would run on a flagged planet. The departure-aware error classes for spots and the high deck hold the departure's strength at its true value and rest on the Δχ² test, which on clean spectra credits more errors as recoverable than the posterior does. For haze both measures were run, and the price of fitting the strength was a quarter of the decidable present CH₄ labels and 18 % of all decidable labels. The nested-sampling settings (100 live points, 20,000 calls, a median of 3,961 calls per planet) are modest beside the 10⁵-10⁸ calls of richer retrievals. They carry a Monte Carlo error near the 0.95 cut that no repeat-seed check bounds.

**Limitations of the departures and the simulations.** The haze of the retrievals and of the retrained designs fills the column at one number density up to the model's top boundary, so its size depends on that boundary and on an order-of-magnitude conversion to the observed opacity scale that holds at one pressure level (Section 2.3). The classifier's failure and the flags' firing were re-measured under three confined hazes (Section 3.3), but the retrievals, the error classes and the budget were not. The mixed-population test with the retrieval budget hazed the planets at one density and one particle size, with the fallback's forward model correct up to one parameter, and at a density where the optical-excess score flags every hazed planet, so matching the oracle there is the expected outcome. At the weaker density the flag's sensitivity falls to 60 %, and the no-retrieval routing recovers little of the loss, which is in H₂O (Section 3.6). Not reported are the budgeted retrieval at that density, mixed particle sizes, and the free-haze fallback on spotted spectra, which the score sends to it. The rules' thresholds are quantiles of the clean test scores, so their clean decline rates are fixed by construction; set on a random half of the test population and applied to the other half (20 splits), every rule's decline rate under every departure is reproduced within 0.5 points, and the clean rates fall at 7.6-10.7 %. The single-departure selection simulation of Table 11 is one noise draw; over ten draws its rows move by 3-5 targets and the routing's recovery under haze is about half of the added loss rather than two thirds (Section 3.5). The selection simulation ranks by a molecule's probability, a proxy for a selection the mission will make by theme and signal-to-noise; observing costs, target priorities and follow-up opportunities are not modelled. The payload noise model was checked against published bands for AIRS only, so the size of the optical points' noise, which the mechanism of Section 3.3 depends on, is unchecked.

**Limitations of the real panel and the method.** No stellar-contamination correction was applied beyond the source papers' own. Seven panel hosts are M dwarfs, among them the hosts of all five methane planets, so a stellar contribution to their short-wavelength shape cannot be excluded, and the simulated spot departure shows what 10-20 % coverage would cost; the shape flag fires on two thirds of the spotted simulated planets. Labels for the real planets come from the literature, the ambiguous ones are not scored, and thirteen of the 104 cells carry a departure from the rule or a judgement call (Section 2.6). Coverage of the Ariel grid by JWST is partial for every panel planet, fourteen reductions have at least 40 filled bins and eleven have no optical data. The flat fill itself misleads the all-input design (Section 3.4), and the conversion was validated only at near-complete coverage, with the mask control standing in at partial coverage. The flags' clean thresholds were set at Tier-1 noise and are applied also at JWST precision, where a spectrum carries much less noise than the flags saw in simulation. The eight methane reductions are five planets, and two of the eight labels rest on detections at or below about 3σ. The method was assembled on this panel and about twenty router variants were compared on it before the rule was fixed, so its intervals are descriptive, and half credit for a split or withheld verdict is itself a scoring choice. The flag's lower cut has fired on one real planet. The amplitude flag assumes an H₂/He scale height, so high-metallicity or steam atmospheres read as muted for reasons other than aerosols, and it relies on catalogue masses: the candidate sample lists HIP 67522 b at 186 Earth masses, with an uncertainty of −120/+338 and a mass flag it also carries on 13 % of its entries, an order of magnitude above the 13.8 measured (93). Several panel planets are listed by the candidate sample with eclipse as the preferred method, so their Tier-1 transit counts are not what the mission would schedule.

**Scope.** The results concern the design as re-implemented, on the stated simulator, under one named population, and the prevalences are limits under that population and not physical limits. A second population with equilibrium chemistry and a metallicity distribution would show how far they move with the prior. The experiments cover a selected collection of departures, and they do not show how far the classifier can be relied on outside it. The second classifier of Appendix B, built for a different objective, showed a nearly opposite departure profile, so one classifier's error classes do not stand in for another's.

## 5. Conclusion

A simulator-trained classifier for Ariel's Tier-1 spectra can be tested before it is used, by asking which of its labels the spectra decide, which of its errors could be repaired, and what it does on real spectra. Under the training prior, at the 0.95 level and on noise-free spectra, a third to a half of the labels are undecided at Tier-1 precision, and about four fifths of the re-implemented classifier's clean errors fall on them. Haze leaves the labels decidable when its strength is known, noise does not, and a high deck is partly each. On JWST spectra of 23 planets the classifier is right on almost every absent label and misses most present ones. Some of those misses are coverage, some enter through the inputs below 1.95 µm and are found by the AIRS-only input, and at Tier-1 noise muting costs labels whatever the input. The routing method proposed here reads these mismatches with two flags that need no retrieval and sends each to its own remedy. It gains 8.5 of 66 labels at JWST precision and 2.7 at Tier-1 noise over the re-implemented classifier, at 4.9 points of clean simulated accuracy. It cannot be separated from two simpler fixes on that panel, and retraining does better in simulation. It has yet to be tested on planets it was not built on. The clear-model retrieval shares the haze failure and fails more confidently than the classifier, so a flag's destination decides whether it helps. For any simulator-trained inference system used to make decisions, which of its answers the data decide is a question the data can answer and the system cannot.
