# Separating recoverable from irrecoverable errors in Ariel Tier-1 molecular classification: a new design tested on model grids and James Webb Space Telescope spectra

## Abstract

Machine learning classification has been proposed for sorting the exoplanet atmospheres in Ariel's Tier-1 transmission spectroscopy survey of about 1,000 planets by composition. Each label states whether a molecule's abundance exceeds 10⁻⁴, and target selection on these labels may dismiss a planet for good. Such classifiers are trained and tested on one simulator, so it has not been known which of their errors under distribution shift and model misspecification a better design could repair. This study re-implemented the Ariel consortium's classifiers, checked them against 48 published accuracies, separated their errors by identifiability into those on labels the spectrum settles with confidence and those on labels it does not, measured how much of the error a better rule removes, and introduced a new design, the matched ensemble, that removes much of it. The ensemble trains networks on a population with correlated chemistry, using each spectrum's measured bins at their real errors, and sets its thresholds on held-out simulations. Frozen and then scored once on two model grids read through JWST coverage, one built with the development grid's code and one photochemical, it reached a balanced credit (0.5 for any fixed answer) of 0.711 and 0.773 at JWST precision and 0.636 and 0.700 at Tier-1 noise, against 0.531-0.658 for a routing method tested first, every gain with an interval above zero under a bootstrap over both planets and source models, at false-present rates of 8-17 %. On 90 randomly chosen photochemical-grid spectra, a nested-sampling retrieval with this study's four-gas model scored below the ensemble by 0.197 to 0.236 at JWST precision and by 0.095 to 0.126 at Tier-1 noise. On the photochemical grid, 82 % of its correct present calls vanished when the molecule's opacity was removed. At Ariel's coverage in simulation its accuracy was 84.2 % on clean spectra and 80.5 % under a haze included in its training, against 81.2 and 63.5 % for the re-implemented classifier; under that haze it made under a third as many recoverable errors and lost about half as many CH₄ and H₂O targets at 60 % Tier-2 slots. On real labels, seen beforehand but used for no design choice, it led the routing method at Tier-1 noise (63.4 against 57.9 of 88; 95 % interval 0.7 to 10.7) and at JWST precision on labels outside the panel that method was built on (35.0 against 29.5 of 41; 0.5 to 10.5); both lower bounds are close to zero. Over all 105 labels it scored 84.0 against 81.5 (−6.5 to 11.5). At Tier-1 noise it found methane on 4.7 of eight reductions, where every other design found at most 1.7. An atmospheric retrieval of 955 simulated targets set the limit. Under the training prior, on noise-free spectra, 32-54 % of the CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃ labels were not settled at Tier-1 precision at a posterior of 0.95. About four fifths of the classifier's clean errors fell on these labels; a reference rule reading the same noisy spectra still removed 38-48 % of its error, so these labels are unsettled rather than unreadable, and the ensemble did not settle them either. Under equilibrium chemistry on 200 simulated Mission Candidate Sample targets the share fell to 7-24 %, so the limit depends on the population; there the ensemble made about half as many errors as the classifier on labels the spectrum settles (21 against 43). Among the aerosols, labels stayed decidable under haze of known strength but not under a high cloud deck; they also stayed decidable under spots of known strength but not under extra noise. On WASP-39 b's JWST spectrum the classifier agreed with both scored labels, one of them a judgement call. Over 23 planets it was right on 36 of 38 absent labels and found 3 of 26 present ones, and the routing method built on these planets gained nothing on 12 new planets. The measured gain lies in the design and its confidence together: declining on the ensemble's probability alone added about as much as the best existing rule added to the classifier. The procedure, which measures how many labels a survey's spectra settle with confidence and how much of a classifier's error a better rule removes, applies to any survey that selects targets with a simulator-trained classifier, wherever the expensive method's model is adequate.

**Keywords:** exoplanet atmospheres; transmission spectroscopy; Ariel; JWST; machine learning classification; identifiability; atmospheric retrieval; target selection; distribution shift; model misspecification; aerosols; WASP-39 b

## 1. Introduction

When a planet transits its star, part of the starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere, and molecules in the atmosphere absorb that light at their characteristic wavelengths. The resulting transmission spectrum shows which molecules are present. Ariel, a European Space Agency mission scheduled for launch in 2029 (1), will measure transmission spectra of about 1,000 exoplanets between 0.5 and 7.8 µm. Below 1.1 µm it will use three broadband photometers, which give the three optical points read by the classifier studied here. At longer wavelengths it will use three spectrometer channels, NIRSpec, AIRS-CH0 and AIRS-CH1 (1). Each target is observed first at low signal-to-noise in a Tier-1 reconnaissance survey, and a subset is re-observed at higher precision in Tier 2 (2-5). Tier 3 is the mission's highest-resolution binning, and the classifier reads spectra in this binning even at Tier-1 noise. Tier-1 spectra are the basis for choosing which planets receive deeper observations, so a planet dismissed at Tier 1 may never be observed again.

An atmospheric retrieval infers an atmosphere's composition by fitting a forward model, which computes the spectrum a given atmosphere would produce, to the observed spectrum. It typically requires 10⁵-10⁸ model evaluations per planet (6) and specialist choices of assumptions and priors (7). These requirements make retrievals costly as a first pass over 1,000 planets. Even so, simulated Tier-2 spectra of the 146 transit targets of an Ariel Mission Reference Sample have been retrieved as a population (8). With the simple forward model used here, retrieving the test population took 365 core-hours (Section 3.6; Appendix C). Machine-learning classifiers are a faster alternative: once trained, they answer in seconds. As a preliminary assessment, the Ariel consortium's population study of the Tier-1 survey proposed four scikit-learn classifiers that flag the presence of CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃. They reached 64-89 % accuracy on simulated planets (7). Their code and trained models were not released.

These classifiers were trained and tested on spectra from the same simulator. So were the models developed for the Ariel Data Challenges (6) and most of the wider machine-learning retrieval literature (9-14), although one study also tested its networks under deliberately incorrect model assumptions (13). A test of this kind shows that a classifier has learned its simulator. It does not show how the classifier behaves when real atmospheres differ from the simulator. Real atmospheres do differ. Hazes and clouds mute or reshape spectra (15, 16), and unocculted star spots, on the part of the star the planet does not cross, imprint false features (17). Opacity databases, which tabulate how strongly each molecule absorbs, are built from different line lists and broadening data (18, 19). Training grids also omit absorbers that real atmospheres contain. Misspecification of this kind, in which the training model does not match reality, biases simulation-based inference in general (20-23). JWST transmission spectra of warm giants and sub-Neptunes now show SO₂, hazes, patchy clouds and metallicities of tens to hundreds of times solar (24-44). A classifier trained on clear hydrogen-helium atmospheres with grey clouds has seen none of these.

Simulator-trained retrieval models have been checked against real Hubble and JWST spectra (9, 13, 45-47). On WASP-39 b the retrieved abundances move by more than an order of magnitude with the data reduction alone, that is, with how the raw observations are processed into a spectrum (48). One amortised retrieval, a neural network trained once and then applied to any spectrum, collapsed on the real WASP-39 b spectrum until the planetary radius was added to its latent parameters (49). A web search on 30 October 2026, covering arXiv and the main journals, found no molecule-presence classifier proposed for Ariel that had been checked against a real spectrum.

A classifier's error on a spectrum is of one of two kinds. In the first, the information was in the spectrum and the classifier missed it. Retraining, a different input or a fallback method can fix such an error. In the second, the spectrum did not settle the label at that precision, given the population from which it is drawn. The posterior, the probability of each answer given the spectrum, may still lean towards the right answer. This study calls the first kind recoverable and the second irrecoverable. A label is decidable if the spectrum settles it, so decidability depends on the spectrum. The test is whether an atmosphere with the opposite label fits the spectrum within its noise. The share of a classifier's errors in each class, and the rules that find them, depend on the classifier.

The consortium study tested decidability for one planet, with a retrieval that gave a 48 % probability of CH₄ above 10⁻⁴ at Tier-1 precision (7). Bocchieri et al. (50) then ran retrievals on about 300 simulated Tier-1 targets. They defined a P-statistic: the posterior probability that a molecule's abundance exceeds a threshold, which was about 10⁻⁵ in their study. This study's decidability thresholds the same posterior quantity, with the abundance threshold at 10⁻⁴. Radica et al. (5) retrieved three benchmark planets at each tier to assess which molecules are constrained. They used a bounded-posterior criterion rather than the P-statistic. Neither study measured decidability on the test population of a classifier, against that classifier's errors, or under departures from the simulator. The classifier's own confidence cannot stand in for this measurement, because it does not survive a distribution shift, a change in the data away from the training set (51). Sections 3.3 and 3.6 show this under haze. What a classifier can do instead is decline spectra it is unsure of or that look unlike its training set. Five such decline rules from the failure-detection literature were scored alongside (Section 2.3).

This study asks four questions. First, under an explicitly defined population, how often do physically distinct compositions give Ariel spectra that do not settle a molecule's label? And what share of a classifier's errors falls in each class, under the simulator and under each departure from it (Sections 2.4, 2.5, 3.2 and 3.3)? Second, how does the classifier behave on real spectra, beginning with WASP-39 b? Answering this requires JWST spectra to be made compatible with its inputs by a justified and validated procedure (Sections 2.6 and 3.4). Third, can a diagnostic recognise when the classifier is unreliable? And does using one improve classification or target selection over the existing methods, namely a retrieval of every planet and the standard decline rules (Sections 2.7, 2.8 and 3.5 to 3.7)? Fourth, what can a better design repair, what must it leave, and does that hold on data from outside its training code (Sections 2.10 and 3.7)?

Before these questions were addressed, the classifiers were re-implemented and checked against all 48 published accuracies (Section 3.1). The third question was first addressed with a routing method, which sends flagged spectra to another input or design. It gained labels on the panel of planets it was built on, but it gained nothing when frozen and applied to 12 new planets. An audit after that failure found errors in the data path and faults in the training recipe. The errors are corrected throughout. The faults led to a new design, the matched ensemble, which this study proposes. Its settings were frozen, and it was then scored once on model grids. The real labels had all been seen by then, so its results on them are description and not confirmation. Section 2.9 and Appendix C give the expectations committed before each run, and their outcomes.

The main findings are these. Under the training prior, a Tier-1 spectrum does not settle a third to a half of the labels. About four fifths of the re-implemented classifier's errors on clean spectra fall on these labels. A better rule can still lean the right way on them: on the same noisy spectra, a reference retrieval rule makes 6-10 points fewer errors than the classifier on every gas, so about 40-50 % of the classifier's error is excess that a better design could remove. On 200 simulated Mission Candidate Sample targets under equilibrium chemistry, the share of such labels is 7-24 %, so this limit depends strongly on the assumed population. On real JWST spectra the classifier is right on almost every absent label but finds few present molecules, because the spectra of real giant planets are muted beyond its training prior. When a flag sends the spectra affected by a departure to a fallback retrieval that does not model that departure, more targets are lost than with no fallback. Frozen and scored once on two model grids, the matched ensemble scores above every earlier design, and on the photochemical grid above this study's four-gas retrieval; the gains hold under a bootstrap over both planets and source models, and most of the gain over the routing method is shared with a simpler candidate built on the same principle, training on each spectrum's measured bins at its real errors on a broad population. On 200 simulated Mission Candidate Sample targets it makes about half the classifier's errors on labels the spectrum settles. For the mission, these results give a budget for the selection step: how much of a classifier's error a better design can remove and how much needs more data, both of which depend on the population assumed. The stakes in simulation are a few per cent of Tier-2 targets, and the classifier that flies may differ from the one tested here, so what carries over is the measurement, which the mission can repeat with its own choices.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 The published design and its re-implementation

This section describes how the consortium's design was re-implemented and defines the terms used for its parts. The re-implementation followed Sections 2.2 and 2.5 of the consortium study (7) element by element (Table A1). For every planet of the Ariel Mission Candidate Sample (52), isothermal atmospheres were generated with random temperatures, abundances of CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃, and grey cloud decks. Each planet received four such atmospheres for training (POP-III) and one for testing (POP-I). Of the sample's 977 planets, 955 have a clean test spectrum, meaning one with no departure from the training simulator. These 955 planets form the test population. The reproduction of Section 3.1 and the mixed-population test of Section 2.8 use the 954 that also have a hazed spectrum. Counts differ slightly where a few spectra failed to render or bin. Spectra were binned at Tier-3 resolution, perturbed with a random draw of Tier-1 noise and normalised per spectrum. Three abundance thresholds were used: 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻³. At each threshold, a molecule was labelled present if its abundance exceeded that threshold. Four scikit-learn classifiers at default settings (53) were trained for each molecule and threshold.

The consortium study did not state whether the classifier input included the three optical points. They were included here for two reasons: the study describes the classifiers as learning "from their spectral shape over the whole wavelength range sampled by Ariel" (7), and its figures of observed Tier-1 spectra show those points.

Four terms are used throughout. A *learner* is one of the four scikit-learn models: k-nearest neighbours (KNN), multilayer perceptron (MLP), random forest (RFC) and support-vector classifier (SVC). The *classifier* is the average of the four learners in simulation and their majority on a real spectrum. The *design* is the recipe, which can be retrained on another population or given another input. The *re-implemented classifier* is this recipe, with the deviations of Section 2.2, trained on POP-III with all 104 bins as input. No claim here concerns the consortium's original trained models, which were not released.

### 2.2 Forward model, noise and deviations

This section describes how spectra and their noise were simulated, and where the simulation deviates from the consortium study. Spectra were computed with TauREx 3 (54) at a resolving power of about 1,000 and then binned by integration. The model atmospheres had constant abundances and included molecular absorption, Rayleigh scattering, a grey cloud deck and, where stated, a Mie haze. They were first rendered through the MultiREx wrapper (55). The released code calls TauREx directly and reproduces them bit for bit. TauREx 3.3.2 reads the pressure grid of Exo-Transmit opacity tables as bar, whereas Exo-Transmit means Pa. The grid was therefore divided by 10⁵ on loading. The inconsistency was reported and has since been corrected on the TauREx main branch in pull request 174, which is not yet in a release (56). With the correction, TauREx spectra of 1,804 test atmospheres matched Exo-Transmit's own code better (median shape correlation 0.9986, against 0.986 without it).

Per-bin noise came from a payload model built with ExoSim 2 (57). The model takes the one-hour noise-to-signal ratio for the host star's effective temperature. It scales this ratio to the host's distance and radius and to the transit duration, and divides it by the square root of the number of transits. The model's one free parameter was calibrated so that a signal-to-noise ratio of 7 on the transit-depth change across five atmospheric scale heights reproduced the Mission Candidate Sample's Tier-3 transit counts. A scale height is the height over which atmospheric pressure falls by a factor of e. After calibration, the model predicted these counts with a standard deviation of 0.07 dex over 929 targets, where a dex is a factor of ten. The consortium study's Figure 1 shows three example planets: HD 209458 b, GJ 1214 b and WASP-79 b. For these planets, the model's median AIRS noise of 73, 421 and 227 ppm per bin matches the noise bands read from that figure. The photometric and NIRSpec channels were not checked (Section 4).

Three deviations from the consortium study remain. (i) Opacities came from Exo-Transmit's tables (18, 58-60). Substituting ExoMol cross sections (19) cost the classifier 2.9 percentage points (Table 5), which bounds the effect of this choice. (ii) Collision-induced absorption, the absorption by pairs of colliding molecules, was omitted. On 200 random test planets, adding it from the HITRAN tables (61) changes the most affected bin of a spectrum by a median of 1 ppm (90th percentile 49 ppm, worst 500 ppm; `cia_effect.py`). Added to the test spectra alone, it cost the classifier 0.1 points. When both populations were re-rendered with it and the design refitted, the 48 accuracies moved by a mean absolute 0.4 points (at most 1.9). They came no closer to the published values (mean absolute difference 1.6 against 1.4). (iii) Noise came from the payload model instead of ArielRad (62), which is not public. An earlier reading of the consortium study missed the published accuracies by 10 points on average and by up to 38. That reading binned the spectra to the seven Tier-1 points, truncated the atmosphere at 1 Pa and set the noise exactly at the Tier-1 requirement.

### 2.3 The departures and the stress test

This section describes the departures from the simulator, the stress test that measured their cost and the decline rules scored alongside. For each departure (Table 1), the same test planets were re-rendered or modified. Every loss was therefore a within-planet difference under the same noise draw. Haze followed the Lee et al. prescription (63). Its particles had a radius of 0.1 µm and one number density in every layer, from the model's base to its top at 10⁻⁴ Pa. The haze's mixing ratio, its abundance relative to the gas, therefore rises with height. Its optical excess is the depth it adds at the optical points, and it depends on where the model's top sits. On three test planets, moving the top by two decades changes the excess by up to a factor of two (`haze_top_test.py`).

Haze strength can be expressed by *a*, the haze opacity at 0.35 µm as a multiple of the H₂ Rayleigh opacity (64). Retrievals of ten observed hot Jupiters found log₁₀ a = 2.1-5.7 (16). At 100 Pa, the densities used here, 3 × 10⁷, 2.4 × 10⁸ and 10¹⁰ m⁻³, correspond to log₁₀ a ≈ 2.4-2.7, 3.3-3.6 and 4.9-5.2. A constant density makes *a* rise by two decades for every two decades of pressure, so the correspondence holds at that level only. The same planets and decks were therefore also rendered under the three confined hazes of Table 1. The power-law hazes were given a constant mixing ratio, so that *a* is the same at every height. The three confined hazes gave a median optical excess of 409, 373 and 546 ppm (`haze_confined.py`).

Spots used PHOENIX spectra from the STScI atlas (65), with a spot-to-photosphere temperature ratio of 0.85 for every host type. This is an M-dwarf contrast, close to the 0.86 of Rackham et al. (17). The contrast differs for FGK hosts (66).

**Table 1.** Departures from the simulator.

Unless stated otherwise, results are for the 10⁻⁴ threshold, averaged over the four learners and four molecules. Three input variants of the design were trained on the same spectra. The first read all 104 bins, the input of the re-implemented classifier. The "no-optical variant" read the 101 bins without the three optical points. The "AIRS-only variant" read the AIRS bins above 1.95 µm alone. Recall is the share of present labels a design reports as present. Two transplant experiments located the haze failure. In the first, hazed optical points were copied into clean spectra. In the second, clean optical points were restored in hazed spectra. Section 3.3 reports the levels that matter for what follows, which were chosen after the runs. The other levels, and per-learner values, are in the repository.

The stress test, in the manner of a corruption benchmark (67), measured three quantities. The loss is the clean accuracy minus the accuracy under the departure. The irreducible loss is the clean accuracy minus the accuracy of the design retrained with the departure present. The third quantity was the loss of the design trained on a randomised grid, in which each planet independently carried haze, spots and extra noise (68).

Five failure-detection decline rules were scored alongside. A decline rule withholds the classifier's answer on spectra it flags as unreliable. Two are confidence rules: the probability margin ("margin") and the spread of the four learners' probabilities ("learner-spread"; 69). Three are distance rules, which read a spectrum's distance from the training set. They are the Mahalanobis distance (70), the mean distance to the 10 nearest training spectra ("k-NN distance"; 71) and the principal-component reconstruction error ("PCA"). Each rule's threshold is the 90th percentile of its score on the clean test spectra. Each rule therefore declines about 10 % of the clean spectra by construction; the margin rule declines 7.4 % (Section 3.6). A held-out check is reported in Section 4. A rule's selective credit is defined here within the selective-classification framework (72, 73). It is the rule's accuracy on the planets it accepts under a departure, minus its accuracy on clean spectra at the same coverage, that is, the same fraction of planets accepted. Split-conformal coverage (74, 75) is reported alongside. It is the fraction of planets whose conformal prediction set contains the true label, calibrated to 90 % on clean spectra. These five rules are the "existing rules" below.

### 2.4 Identifiability: which labels a spectrum decides

This section defines when a spectrum decides a label and describes the two tests used to measure it: a cheap fitting test and a full retrieval. Consider planet *i* with true parameters θ and molecule *X*. The label "X > 10⁻⁴" is called decidable at precision σ when the spectrum gives the true class a posterior probability above 0.95 under a stated population and forward model. This is a measure of posterior assurance: it says how confidently the spectrum, read under that prior, settles the label, not that every opposite-label atmosphere has been excluded. A cheap fitting test approximates it:

Δχ²ᵢ(X) = min over θ′ with X = 10⁻⁴ and over δ of Σ_b [(S_b(θ) − S_b(θ′) − δ) / σ_b]² (eq1)

where S_b(θ) is the model transit depth in bin *b* for parameters θ, σ_b is the planet's Tier-1 noise in that bin and δ is an additive depth offset. The sum runs over the 104 Tier-3 bins. Δχ² is thus the noise-weighted misfit of the best-fitting atmosphere with *X* at the boundary; a large value means that no such atmosphere fits the spectrum within the noise. The minimum runs over the population's nuisance parameters, the quantities that shape the spectrum but are not being tested, with *X* held at the 10⁻⁴ boundary. These are the temperature, over 0.7-1.05 times the equilibrium temperature T_eq; the grey cloud top, over 5 × 10² to 10⁶ Pa; and the other three abundances, over 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻². Band depth is monotone in abundance when the other parameters are held fixed, so the boundary is where the opposite class comes closest in that sense; once the nuisance parameters are re-optimised, a better opposite-class fit can lie away from the boundary, which would inflate Δχ². On 100 planets, minimising over the full opposite abundance range as well lowered Δχ² by more than 0.1 on 8.8 % of labels and changed the verdict on 0.8 %, with 99 % of the optima at the boundary; the restriction therefore does not inflate the statistic, although the optimiser's incomplete convergence does, in that direction, on about 9 % of labels (Table C1, row AD8; `results/dchi2_full_class.txt`). The minimum was found by the Nelder-Mead method, started from the true parameters and from two random draws from the prior (median 569 forward calls per planet-molecule). If the search misses the minimum, Δχ² is inflated and decidability overstated. This bias has the opposite sign to the one discussed next. Under a departure, the counterfactual model also includes the departure's physics at its true strength. The test therefore assumes a correct model of the departure, which a mission would not have. Section 3.6 measures the price of fitting the strength for haze. White noise at *c* times σ needs no run, since Δχ² scales as 1/*c*².

The test represents the opposite class by its single best-fitting point, which over-credits that class. Its scale was therefore calibrated against a nested-sampling retrieval (76, 77) of all 955 test planets. A retrieval of this kind combines a prior, the distribution of atmospheres assumed before the spectrum is seen, with the fit to the spectrum. The result is the posterior, the probability distribution of the atmosphere's parameters given the spectrum. The retrieval used the nestle implementation with its multi-ellipsoid sampler, 100 live points and at most 20,000 likelihood calls. It fitted the same free quantities as the test and the four abundances, under uniform priors on the population's ranges. It took a median of 3,961 calls and 19.9 min per planet. For every label it gave p_true, the posterior probability of the true class. For a present label, p_true is the P-statistic of Bocchieri et al. (50), which that study used at a threshold of about 10⁻⁵. For an absent label, p_true is one minus the P-statistic. A label is decidable at level *q* if p_true > *q*, with *q* = 0.95 throughout. For each molecule, the threshold was set by a logistic regression of the indicator 1[p_true > 0.95] on Δχ² over the 955 planets, at the Δχ² where the fitted probability crosses one half; the test thus represents the posterior property "p_true > 0.95 is more likely than not at this misfit", and its rank agreement with the posterior is reported with it (Section 3.2). The looser level 0.84, which corresponds to one sigma on one side, is reported as a sensitivity check. This level was in the plan and the calibration script before any retrieval ran, but no expectation for it was recorded. The retrieval's own accuracy, the fraction of labels with p_true > 0.5, is reported as reference-model performance under its prior and forward model. It is not an upper bound on another classifier's accuracy: posterior thresholding is Bayes-optimal only under the matching population and observation model, and here the test population is POP-I, the prior POP-III's, and the main inputs noise-free. The classifier's excess error over this reference on the 200 noisy realisations is reported in Section 3.2.

Unless stated otherwise, the retrieval was run on the noise-free spectrum, with the Tier-1 σ in the likelihood only. The classifier, by contrast, reads one noisy realisation. "Tier-1 precision" refers throughout to this retrieval of the noise-free spectrum, and "Tier-1 noise" to spectra with Tier-1 noise added. To measure the difference, a fixed random set of 200 planets was also retrieved on the classifier's own noisy realisation (Section 3.2).

The retrieval's abundance prior is POP-III's, 10⁻⁹-10⁻², which is wider than POP-I's. Under it, an uninformative spectrum already gives p_true = 0.71 for an absent label and 0.29 for a present one. An absent label therefore reaches 0.95 with a Bayes factor of 8 against the opposite class, whereas a present label needs 47. Here a Bayes factor is the factor by which the spectrum changes the odds of one class against the other. Prevalences are therefore reported for present and absent labels separately. They are also reported with decidability defined by a Bayes factor above 19 relative to the prior odds, which removes the unequal class odds of the prior but not its within-class abundance and nuisance priors, on which the class evidences still depend. The 200-planet subsample was retrieved a third time under POP-I's own prior, 10⁻⁷-10⁻², to measure the prior's effect directly (Section 3.2).

The retrieval's forward model is the training model itself, with the planetary radius fixed and a small depth offset free. "Undecidable" therefore means not decided at the 0.95 level under this prior and this forward model. A chemically informed model or a narrower prior could decide some of these labels, and a free radius could make others undecidable.

The population is stated explicitly. It is the planets and abundances of POP-I at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, retrieved under POP-III's prior. The true joint distribution of atmospheric conditions among Ariel's targets is unknown. Every prevalence reported here is therefore a prevalence for POP-I under POP-III's prior. Section 3.4 measures how the real spectra sit against it.

### 2.5 The two error classes

Each classifier error was assigned a class according to the decidability of its label on the same spectrum. On clean spectra a label is decidable if p_true > 0.95, undecidable if 0.05 ≤ p_true ≤ 0.95 and misled if p_true < 0.05. A misled label is thus one on which the retrieval strongly favours the wrong class. An error is called recoverable if its label is decidable and irrecoverable if its label is undecidable or misled. An irrecoverable error is one on which the spectrum, read under this prior, gives no confident answer; a better classifier can still lean the right way on such a label, as Section 3.2 shows, but cannot settle it. Misled labels are one in 3,820 on clean spectra (Section 3.2). In practice, therefore, irrecoverable errors are errors on undecidable labels. Under a departure, the class follows from the departure-aware Δχ² at the threshold calibrated on clean spectra. The departure changes the parameter degeneracies, so the calibration need not carry over; the departure rows are therefore likelihood-based diagnostics, read against the test's own clean row, rather than extensions of the posterior-defined classes.

Some errors arise from an unlucky noise draw on a decidable label. Such an error is counted as recoverable, although a retrieval of that noisy realisation may not recover it. The recoverable shares are therefore upper bounds, and Section 3.2 measures the gap.

The prevalence of each class is the fraction of the classifier's errors in it. The expectation recorded before the runs was that haze errors would be mostly recoverable and noise errors mostly irrecoverable (Appendix C). As a post-hoc refinement, the errors under a departure were further split into two groups. Shared errors are those the learner also makes on the clean spectrum; the others are those the departure introduces.

On real spectra, the posterior under the training prior is not a reliable reference. For these spectra, Section 3.4 also reports a weaker, operational sense of recoverable: another learner reading the same spectrum finds the label. That learner can, however, be wrong.
### 2.6 Real JWST spectra on the Ariel grid

This subsection describes the real JWST spectra, how they were converted onto Ariel's bins, how their labels were set, and the controls used to interpret the verdicts. The primary external input was the WASP-39 b benchmark of Carter & May et al. (24). It combines the four JWST Early Release Science modes (26-29), using one published reduction of each. A reduction is one published processing of the raw observations. In the benchmark, the light curves were fitted uniformly over 0.52-5.3 µm and the remaining offsets between modes were measured. The MIRI LRS spectrum of Powell et al. (25) extended the benchmark to 7.8 µm. Twenty-two further planets with public JWST transmission spectra, in 25 reductions, formed a panel (Table 2).

Each of the 104 Tier-3 bins took the inverse-variance-weighted mean of the JWST points inside it. A point only partly inside a bin was weighted by the fraction that overlapped. Modes were aligned onto a reference mode through the wavelengths where they overlap. For WASP-39 b the reference was NIRISS order 1; this alignment gave PRISM the −177 ppm offset that Carter & May fit against their atmospheric model. For the panel the reference was the mode with the most points. The two G395H detectors do not overlap; they share one offset and were fitted as a single mode (Appendix E). A mode that overlapped no aligned mode by at least five points kept its published level.

A reduction's mask is the set of Tier-3 bins it measures. Bins with no data were filled with a flat value at the measured mean and flagged as filled. A verdict is the classifier's call on a label. The number of filled bins is reported with every verdict (Table 2). Each spectrum was then normalised over all 104 bins, filled ones included, which removed any global offset. The mask control below tests the effect of this normalisation.

Two noise regimes were run. JWST precision used the spectra as delivered. Ariel Tier-1 noise added the payload model's per-bin σ, at the planet's Tier-1 transit count, to the JWST noise as 500 Gaussian draws (300 for WASP-39 b). For WASP-39 b the Tier-3 transit count was also run for context. A reporting frequency is the share of draws, averaged over the four learners, in which a learner reports the molecule. No stellar-contamination correction was applied beyond each source paper's own (Section 4).

The conversion was validated on the benchmark's best-fit model of WASP-39 b. The model was converted in two ways. In the first, it was binned directly onto the 104 bins. In the second, it was sampled on each mode's JWST wavelength grid with the per-point errors and then converted. The two versions differed by 22 ppm rms (104 ppm at worst, in a MIRI bin). No verdict changed on the noise-free spectra. This check validates the conversion at near-complete coverage. At the panel's partial coverage, the control is each planet's clear twin, a cloud-free model of the planet defined below, read through the same mask (Section 3.4). Retrieved abundances depend on the reduction (48). The verdict's dependence on the reduction was therefore measured with a sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations (Appendix E).

The panel was assembled in three batches. Ten planets in thirteen reductions joined WASP-39 b in the first batch. Nine more followed once the first batch's verdicts were known and the shape flag of Section 2.7 had been designed. Three more followed once the muting-census routing of Section 2.7 had been designed and scored on the first two batches. The panel therefore tests each of those elements only on later batches (Table C3).

A label is the published answer, present or absent, for one molecule on one planet, against which a verdict is scored. Labels came from published free-chemistry retrievals, which fit each gas's abundance freely rather than through a chemical model, at the 10⁻⁴ threshold. Under this label rule, a label is present where the 1σ lower bound lies above 10⁻⁴. It is absent where the upper bound or quoted upper limit lies below 10⁻⁴. It is ambiguous, and not scored, where a paper's abundance straddled 10⁻⁴ or the literature disagreed.

Labels were committed, that is, recorded with a date in the project repository, before the verdicts for the first batch (15 October 2026) and the third (29 October). The second batch's labels were committed together with its verdicts on 29 October, so the repository does not show their order. WASP-39 b's scored labels were set on 29 October, after its verdicts. Its H₂O and CH₄ labels match the expectations of 16 October. The H₂O label rests on the free retrieval of Deka et al. (78): log₁₀ −2.08 ± 0.09 with patchy clouds and −2.64 ± 0.08 with Mie aerosols. No free retrieval prints a CH₄ limit. The CH₄ label therefore rests on the 3σ limit of 5 × 10⁻⁶ from the equilibrium models of Rustamkulov et al. (29).

Three labels rest on detections at or below about 3σ. They are kept but marked tentative. They are GJ 1214 b's CH₄ and CO₂ (2.0 and 2.4σ alone, 3.3σ jointly) and TOI-732 c's CH₄ (3.2σ in the set-up used, 3.0-4.6σ across set-ups).

The first batch's labels came from a literature survey made before this rule was written down. Two K2-18 b H₂O labels had been frozen as absent, although their published limits lie above the threshold. These two labels were withdrawn after the verdicts were known (Appendix E). A split verdict, with two of the four learners each way, earns half credit (Section 2.7).

All 104 panel label cells were audited against their sources after the verdicts were known (`results/jwst_panel/label_sources.csv`; counts in Appendix E). Apart from the two withdrawals, labels were left as committed. The routing method's score is also reported on two other sets. One is the set that follows the rule throughout (65 labels). The other is the set that also drops every judgement call (59 labels; `label_sensitivity.py`, Appendix E).

The batches were not a systematic sample. They were the planets whose public reductions and free-chemistry constraints came to hand in three rounds of searching (`jwst_validation_brief.md`). The methane planets were sought because methane had been reported. Six rocky planets with flat spectra fix no label and were dropped: TOI-836 b, TOI-776 b and c, GJ 357 b, LTT 1445 A b and GJ 1132 b. HAT-P-1 b, LTT 9779 b and WASP-69 b had no public reduction when the panel was assembled. HAT-P-1 b's reduction appeared later, and the planet joined the fourth batch. GJ 3090 b had no limit that held across its retrievals. Throughout, "right" means agreement with these published retrievals, which are themselves model-dependent.

The muting census is a population-level comparison of how flat each real spectrum is against a cloud-free model of the same planet. For it, each real planet's clear H₂/He twin (a model spectrum with no clouds or haze) was rendered at the published abundances and bulk parameters (Table 2). The muting is

M = s_twin / (s_real² − ε²)^½ (eq2)

where s_twin and s_real are the dispersions of the twin and the real spectrum over the measured bins, and ε is the root mean square of the real per-bin errors. The denominator is thus the real dispersion with the noise removed. M is 1 for a spectrum with features as strong as its clear twin's, and larger for a more muted one. Its uncertainty comes from 2,000 resamples of the real depths. The most muted atmosphere the population can draw at those bulk parameters marks the edge of the simulator's prior. That atmosphere has the coldest allowed temperature with the highest allowed deck. Muting was also computed for every POP-I planet against its clear twin.

Three controls use the same twins (`panel_mechanism.py`, `panel_control.py`). In the mask control, the re-implemented classifier reads the twin through the reduction's own mask, scattered 200 times with the real per-bin errors. A label passes if it is called right in at least half of the draws. For a label that passes, the mask alone cannot be blamed for a miss.

The transplants replace one block of the real spectrum with the same block of the twin, shifted to the real mean over the measured AIRS bins. The block is the three optical points, Ariel's NIRSpec channel, both, only the filled or only the measured bins below 1.95 µm, or the AIRS bins.

In the scaled-twin test, the twin is scaled about its mean to the noise-corrected dispersion of the real spectrum over the measured bins. The scaled twin is then read with the real errors, with and without added Tier-1 noise.

These twins share the training physics. A label found in the twin but missed in the real spectrum therefore cannot separate aerosols from forward-model error, unmodelled absorbers or limb asymmetry (differences between the planet's morning and evening limbs).

Each converted spectrum was also retrieved with the sampler of Section 2.4 (`panel_retrieval.py`). It was retrieved twice: clear, and with the haze density free. As for the simulated spectra, the filled bins were unweighted and the Tier-1 σ entered the likelihood. The radius stays at the catalogue value. The depth offset is free over ±2 %, because the real spectra sit up to 1.1 % from the depth at that radius.

### 2.7 The routing method: flags, remedies and how they were scored

This subsection defines the routing method and the scoring used to compare it with the other designs. The routing method was the first answer to the third question of Section 1. It sends a spectrum that a flag marks as unreliable to another input or design. A flag is a test, computed from the spectrum, that marks it as unreliable. The method needs only the spectrum and the Mission Candidate Sample's bulk parameters (the "catalogue" below). It needs no retrieval or published composition. It does assume an H₂/He scale height and a correct catalogue mass.

**The flags.** The routing method uses two flags, each sending a spectrum to a remedy (details in Appendix D). The shape flag reads the spectrum's shape. It acts on a cell, which is one molecule for one learner. It fires when either of two scores exceeds its clean threshold, the 90th percentile of that score on the clean test spectra. The first score is the input disagreement of (eq3): the largest difference between the present-probabilities of the three input variants. The second is the optical-excess score of (eq4): the standardised difference between the normalised depth of the three optical points and that of Ariel's NIRSpec channel. A flagged cell takes the AIRS-only variant (Section 2.3).

The amplitude flag reads the spectrum's size. Its statistic is the ratio R of (eq5). R divides the five-scale-height modulation expected from the catalogue's bulk parameters by the noise-corrected dispersion of the measured bins, so R is larger for a more muted spectrum. Above the upper threshold, a planet is more muted per scale height than any clear POP-I planet. Such a planet goes to the haze-retrained design, which was refitted on POP-III with a third of the planets hazed. Below the lower cut, a planet is more featured than 99 % of POP-I, and its verdict is withheld. The haze+deck-retrained and union-retrained designs extend that training set with high decks, and with spots and extra noise.

Four candidate diagnostics, among them band-metric consistency and predicted decidability, were chosen before the runs. Each was scored in three ways: by selective credit; by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC; 0.5 is chance) for separating the two error classes; and as a router.

The optical-excess score was designed from the haze mechanism of Section 3.3. Its only tests on data it was not built from are therefore the real panel and the confined hazes. The amplitude flag's statistic was chosen after its panel values had been seen. Its rule was committed after all three batches were known. The panel is therefore not an independent test of the amplitude flag.

**The rule.** The amplitude flag is applied first. A planet above the upper threshold goes to the haze-retrained design, and a planet below the lower cut has its verdict withheld. On every other planet, a cell that the shape flag marks takes the AIRS-only variant, and every other cell takes the re-implemented classifier. The verdict is the majority of the four learners. Muting-census routing is the same rule with the muting census of Section 2.6 in the amplitude flag's place.

**How the designs were scored.** Designs are scored on three tiers: in simulation, on the real panel and in the selection. A learner's vote counts as present at a probability of 0.5 or above; this convention decides one exact tie on the panel.

In simulation, the score is the mean accuracy over the four learners and four molecules on POP-I, clean and under each departure, with a withheld label counting one half. All designs were evaluated once, on the same test cases and noise draw (`pipeline_sim.py`).

On the real panel, the score covers all 64 labels the literature decides. A right verdict earns one point, a wrong one none, and a split or withheld one a half, so that no design gains by leaving labels unscored. Each difference between designs has a 95 % bootstrap interval over the 23 planets. The bootstrap resamples planets with replacement, keeping one planet's reductions together (`pipeline_table.py`). The intervals are descriptive, since about twenty router variants were compared on this panel before the rule was fixed. The panel is scored at JWST precision and at Tier-1 noise. At Tier-1 noise, each of 500 draws is scored with every flag recomputed from the noisy spectrum. In each draw, both the JWST and the Tier-1 noise are removed from the dispersion in (eq5) (`panel_tier1.py`).

In the selection, every design enters the simulation of Section 2.8, at 20, 40, 60 and 80 % Tier-2 slots.

The five existing rules were added after an external reading of the draft. They are also scored on all three tiers, as routers to the AIRS-only variant. So is the amplitude flag with the AIRS-only variant on every other planet. A muted case carries the departure measured on real planets into simulation. In it, every feature is scaled about the spectrum's mean by a third, roughly the median muting of the muted giants, and the noise is left unscaled.

**The independent test.** The independent test applied the committed rule, unchanged, to a fourth batch of new planets. The rule, its code and six expectations (Appendix C, rows H1-H6) were committed before any new planet's label or spectrum entered the code. A systematic search on 30 October 2026 then listed the planets of the Mission Candidate Sample outside the panel that had a public JWST transmission spectrum and a published free-chemistry retrieval fixing at least one label. The file `jwst_fourth_batch_survey.md` gives the sources searched and every planet rejected and why.

The label rule was stricter than for the panel: any printed free-chemistry variant crossing 10⁻⁴ leaves the label unscored. A second read of all 52 cells against the sources, also blind to every verdict, agreed on 51. Its two changes were applied before any verdict. HAT-P-30 b's NH₃ became scored. V1298 Tau b left the batch, because a second retrieval's CH₄ disagrees with the first by two decades (two orders of magnitude) near the threshold. That left 12 planets and 24 labels, 8 present and 16 absent (Table 3).

Where a paper states the range its retrieval used, the spectrum was trimmed to that range. Nothing was retrained or re-thresholded. The amplitude thresholds were read through each reduction's own mask. The batch was scored once (`fourth_batch.py`).

### 2.8 Retrieval fallbacks and the selection simulation

This subsection defines two fallback retrievals and the simulation that measures what they, and the flags, change in target selection. A fallback retrieval is one that a pipeline could run on a spectrum instead of trusting the classifier's verdict. The two fallbacks are defined on the hazed spectra. The retrieval of Section 2.4, run unchanged, is called the "clear-model retrieval" on a hazed spectrum and the "clean retrieval" on a clean one. The "free-haze retrieval" adds the haze number density as an eighth free parameter, log-uniform over 10³ to 10¹⁰ m⁻³. At the lower bound the spectrum is haze-free to below a part per million. A pipeline could run the free-haze retrieval on any spectrum without knowing whether it is hazed. Both retrievals were run on all 954 hazed spectra.

The mission-level measure is a selection simulation. Of the 955 POP-I planets, 60 % (573) receive Tier-2 slots (4). The planets are ranked by the mean present-probability of the three learners that give one (KNN, MLP and RFC); the one-vs-one SVC does not give one. Ranking by a molecule's probability is a proxy. The mission will make its selection on scientific yield, target diversity and observing efficiency (4). "Targets lost" counts the planets carrying the molecule that are not selected. It is counted for CH₄, for H₂O and for either molecule ("either-molecule targets").

At the same budget, the strategies compared are as follows. The classifier is used alone, with each existing rule (declined planets sent to the back of the queue), or routed by each candidate to the AIRS-only variant. The band metric is used alone. Finally, a retrieval of every planet ranks the planets by its present-posterior.

A budget variant re-scores by retrieval only the fraction *f* (10 to 70 %) of planets that a rule ranks most suspect. Each rule is compared with three other orderings. The first is a random budget (200 draws). The second is the measured Δχ² of Section 2.4, which is not attainable, since it starts from the true parameters. The third is the retrieval's own undecidability, the unattainable best ordering. The single-departure simulations use one noise draw. The retrievals are of noise-free spectra, whereas the classifier reads noisy ones. Section 3.6 and Appendix C measure this difference on the 200-planet subsample.

The mixed-population test applies the same budget in a case where the retrieval shares the classifier's failure. Each of the 954 planets with a hazed spectrum is hazed with probability *h* (0.1, 0.2 or 0.5; 20 draws). The fraction *f* (10 to 50 %) that a rule ranks most suspect is re-scored by the free-haze retrieval, or, for comparison, by the clear-model retrieval. The rest keep the classifier's verdict, and the selection is the 60 % cut above.

The orderings are a random one, the five existing rules, the optical-excess score, the input disagreement, band-metric consistency, and a haze oracle that knows which planets are hazed and nothing else. The fixed strategies are the classifier alone, the optical-excess score routed to the AIRS-only variant, the clear-model retrieval of every planet, and that retrieval re-run as the free-haze retrieval wherever its evidence fails. The metrics are targets lost, with their spread over the draws, the pipeline's accuracy, and its recall on the hazed targets ("hazed recall") and on the clean targets.

A second haze density gives a second point on the dose-response curve, which describes how the loss grows with haze density. For it, the population was re-rendered at 2 × 10⁶ m⁻³. This is the lowest density at which the stress test measured a large loss (12.1 points, against 1.4 at 2 × 10⁵ m⁻³; Section 3.3). At this density the no-retrieval strategies and each rule's detection of the hazed planets are reported.

Expectations for this test were committed after a pilot and before any population result (Appendix C). The pilot used the 124 planets whose fixed-density hazed posteriors existed first. The clean planets that a budget reaches are scored by the clean retrieval, which is the correctly specified model for them. The free-haze retrieval of clean spectra exists so far for 27 planets.

### 2.10 The corrections, and a new design

This subsection lists the errors corrected after the independent test, then describes the new design, the matched ensemble, and how it was chosen and tested.

**Corrections.** An audit of the data path after the independent test found errors, now corrected in every result and design. Separate offsets for the two G395H detectors had put an artificial step between them. On TOI-270 d this step reversed the 3.3 µm methane contrast: +87 ppm in the published file, −110 ppm after alignment, and +83 ppm in the independent Holmberg reduction. Half-widths in the WASP-107 b NIRCam and MIRI files were read as full widths, which discarded 13 measured bins (67 of 104 are now measured). An instrument profile width in the fourth column of the Felix TOI-270 d files was read as a bin width. HIP 67522 b carried a catalogue mass of 186 Earth masses, against the 13.8 ± 1.0 inferred from its transmission spectrum (96). The two K2-18 b H₂O labels were withdrawn (Section 2.6), leaving 64 panel labels. The corrections changed the conversions of the WASP-107 b and Felix TOI-270 d files and of the panel reductions combining G395H with another mode. They did not change the independent batch's spectra. Earlier values are in the repository's history (`REVISION_EXPECTATIONS.md`, section M).

**Why a new design.** The routing method keeps the published training population and only moves spectra between its inputs. A second audit, prompted by the same failure, measured three faults in that population and recipe as re-implemented. First, abundances are drawn independently. A present gas is therefore usually the most abundant of the four in training (CO₂ in 69 % of its present cases). Held-out recall falls sharply when it is not (H₂O 0.71 against 0.19, one learner on six reductions). Second, hard votes on a population about 29 % present set a threshold that follows from class balance. On the development grid (the rainout grid, described below), CO₂ was ranked well (AUROC 0.86) but found in 18 % of its present cases. Third, the flat fill itself misleads the learners (Section 3.4). The matched ensemble below addresses each fault.

**The training population.** Two populations were rendered with the forward model of Section 2.2 on POP-III's planets. The first is the correlated population (16,000 spectra, 15,286 rendered). Its temperature is log-uniform over 250-2,500 K, with the modulation of Section 2.2 rescaled to it. Its compositions come from FastChem (111, 112), at metallicity log-uniform over 0.1-1,000 times solar and C/O uniform over 0.2-1.5. Half are at equilibrium and half have the carbon chemistry quenched (`generate_grid.py`). Quenching holds the abundances at the values of deeper, hotter layers.

Every gas is then scattered by a normal distribution whose width is drawn uniformly over 0-1.5 dex per planet. With probability 0.15 per gas, the gas is also replaced by a log-uniform draw over 10⁻⁹-10⁻¹, standing for photochemistry and other disequilibrium. Abundances are capped at 10⁻⁰·³, CO is kept, and the mean molecular weight follows from the composition.

The second is the broad population (8,000 spectra, 7,825 rendered). It has CH₄, H₂O, CO₂, NH₃ and CO independently log-uniform over 10⁻⁹-10⁻¹. In both populations, a third of the planets are clear, a third carry a Lee-Mie haze (density log-uniform over 10⁶-3 × 10⁸ m⁻³) and a third a grey deck at 5-500 Pa. A gas is labelled present, as before, above 10⁻⁴.

**The learners.** Each reduction gets its own learners, trained only on the bins it measures. Each training spectrum is scaled about its mean to the target planet's five-scale-height modulation. Half are then muted by a further factor log-uniform over 0.1-1. The reduction's own per-bin errors are added, with the Tier-1 σ in quadrature for a Tier-1 target. The input is the normalised measured bins plus the logarithm of the dispersion over the noise. Amplitude therefore reaches the learner instead of being normalised away.

Five multilayer perceptrons (a type of neural network; two hidden layers of 256 and 128 units, early stopping) predict the four labels together. Each is trained on three fresh noise and muting draws of every row, and their probabilities are averaged. Each molecule has its own threshold for each reduction. The threshold is chosen on 15 % of the rows, held out from training, at their own noise. Among the thresholds with a simulated false-present rate of at most 10 %, it is the one with the highest balanced accuracy (the mean of the accuracies on present and on absent labels). The verdict is present or absent, with no split. At Ariel coverage (Section 3.7) one such ensemble is trained on all bins at each planet's Tier-1 noise. Rendering took about 5 core-hours and one reduction's fit about 2 core-minutes.

**How it was chosen and tested.** The design was developed on the rainout grid in three rounds. The first round was the per-reduction design, the published population with learners for each reduction. The second was the four-learner vote on the broad population. The third was the matched ensemble, which added the correlated population. The per-reduction design was motivated partly by the fourth batch, so that batch is not an independent test of this line of work.

The tests independent of the real labels use three model grids from codes other than the training code. One grid was used for development and two were held out. The grids are independent of the training code but not of each other. The local-condensation grid shares its code with the development grid, and both ATMO grids use equilibrium chemistry. For each of the 38 reductions, each grid supplied 200 models within 200 K of the planet's temperature (fewer where the grid has fewer). These models were rescaled to the planet's bulk parameters and read through the reduction's mask with its real errors, at JWST precision and at Tier-1 noise.

The rainout grid and the local-condensation grid are sets of the ATMO generic grid (113) with a scattering haze and a grey cloud. They differ in how condensation removes elements. In the rainout grid, condensing species deplete their elements from the layers above. In the local-condensation grid, they deplete them only in the layer where they condense. The photochemical grid is the Crossfield et al. grid (114) of 936 VULCAN photochemical models (115). It spans 250 to 2,050 K and up to 1,000 times solar, without clouds or hazes. It uses one planet's geometry, rescaled by scale height.

On the grids, a gas is present in truth where its mean mole fraction over 10-10⁴ Pa is above 10⁻⁴. A right verdict earns 1 and a wrong one 0. Balanced credit is the mean of the shares earned on present and on absent labels, so any fixed answer earns 0.5. It is given with a 95 % bootstrap interval over planets.

Candidates and their selection rule were committed before any third-round score, with the rainout grid as the only development data. There were three candidates: the ensemble on the correlated population, the ensemble on both populations, and the four-learner vote on the broad population with balanced classes. The last of these had come first in the second round on the same grid. The rule chose the candidate with the highest mean balanced credit over the two precisions. The votes on the correlated population, alone and with the broad one, were not run, at a cost of about 15 core-hours. The ensemble on both populations scored 0.732, against 0.724 and 0.720. Its settings were frozen. It was then scored once on the local-condensation grid and the photochemical grid, which no design had seen.

Four checks followed, each with its expectation committed first. The first repeated the whole design with two further seeds. The second was a refit with each molecule's main bands removed from the input. The third was a counterfactual on the photochemical grid, whose files give each model's spectrum with one gas's opacity removed and its chemistry unchanged. The fourth was a nested-sampling retrieval of 90 spectra of the photochemical grid (`grid_retrieval.py`; Section 3.7).

Besides the panel and the fourth batch, 17 further real labels come from HST and JWST spectra of 12 planets outside both (`results/independent_test/`; Table 15). The real labels were not used in any choice, but all had been seen by then. They are therefore reported as description.

This subsection asks what share of labels the spectra can settle at Tier-1 precision, and where the classifier's errors fall relative to them. A third to a half of the labels were undecidable at the 0.95 level (Table 4). Part of this came from the threshold itself. A fifth of the labels lay within half a decade of 10⁻⁴, and nine in ten of these were undecidable; they supplied 17-20 points of each prevalence. Decidability was set first by that distance and second by the planet's noise. Labels one to two decades from the boundary were undecidable in 12-36 % of cases. Absent labels two to three decades below it were undecidable in 1-8 % of cases for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ but in 31 % for H₂O. These undecidable H₂O labels lay on the planets whose noise leaves water unconstrained at any abundance.

The prior moved the prevalence by a few points. Removing the prior's head start raised the fractions by 3-8 points. Under POP-I's own prior, the 200-planet subsample's undecidable fraction rose from 41 to 46 %. Between these two log-uniform priors, the rise fell on absent labels (up to 12 points for one molecule; Table 4). Present labels moved by less than three points. The priors agreed on decidability for 92-95 % of labels, and no label was misled under either.

A population of another form moved the prevalence much further. The comparison used 200 Mission Candidate Sample planets with FastChem equilibrium chemistry and clear atmospheres, under the same prior. Their undecidable fraction was 19 % for CH₄, 24 % for H₂O, 14 % for CO₂ and 7 % for NH₃ (95 % intervals 14-25, 18-29, 10-19 and 4-11). For POP-I on the same planets it was 32, 57, 48 and 30 % (`results/identifiability_mcs.txt`). Almost no planet had CO₂ or NH₃ above 10⁻⁴, so those labels were nearly all absent. On this population, more of the re-implemented classifier's errors were recoverable: 56 % fell on undecidable labels, against 88 % for POP-I on the same planets by the same vote. Its errors on decidable labels rose from 17.5 of 788 labels for POP-I on the same planets to 42.5 of 800 (43.3 over ten draws; `results/identifiability_mcs_split.txt`, `identifiability_mcs_ensemble.txt`). This run had no committed expectation for the prevalence, but it had one for the split.

Physically distinct compositions do produce indistinguishable spectra under this population. On TOI-2136 b, one atmosphere had H₂O at 10⁻²·²⁸ and another had it at the 10⁻⁴ threshold. Their spectra differed by at most 55 ppm, against a median σ of 269 ppm (Δχ² = 0.46 over 104 bins; full parameters in `results/worked_example.txt`). The retrieval gave the true class a posterior of 0.70. In POP-I, 51 planets had H₂O more than a decade above the threshold and an undecidable H₂O label. Only 17 % of the planets had all four labels decidable, 3 % had none, and a third had both CH₄ and H₂O decidable. The one planet retrieved in the consortium study (7) carried a typical undecidable label: CH₄ at 10⁻³·¹, with a 48 % posterior at Tier 1.

Even inside its simulator the classifier was 9-16 points below this reference (Table 4). Three quarters to four fifths of its clean errors fell on undecidable labels. Only 19-25 % fell on labels the spectrum decides, and effectively none on a label the spectrum decides the other way. This split depended on the 0.95 level. The decided share was 38 % at 0.84, and 74 % at 0.5, where the posterior merely leans to the right answer. An undecidable label is unsettled, not uninformative. The retrieval was right on 81-86 % of undecidable labels, against a majority class of 53-60 %. Only 15-19 % of these labels lay within 0.1 of the prior's 0.29.

On the 200 noisy realisations, the noisy posterior read at p ≥ 0.5 was more accurate than the classifier on every gas, by 6.4-9.6 points: the classifier's error was 14.4, 25.6, 16.9 and 16.9 % for CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃ against 7.5, 16.0, 10.5 and 9.0 % for that rule, so 38-48 % of its error was excess over a reference that reads the same noisy spectrum (`results/excess_error.txt`). The reference is not a bound: it was run under POP-III's prior, wider than the test population's, so it is not the Bayes rule for POP-I, and its remaining error is not an irreducible floor. <<PENDING AE1>> It shows that a sizeable share of the error on unsettled labels can be removed. The learners did well on decidable labels but only a few points better than the majority class on undecidable ones. On decidable labels they scored 91-98 % for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and 76-93 % for H₂O. On undecidable labels they scored 58-70 %, a few points above the majority class. Recall of present labels was 35-49 % (H₂O 27 %) where the label is undecidable, against 82-84 % (H₂O 65 %) where it is decidable. On an uninformative spectrum the classifier defaulted to "absent", the prior's answer, as it did on real spectra (Section 3.4).

**Noisy realisations.** The figures above come from noise-free spectra, but the classifier reads a noisy one. Retrieved on that noisy spectrum, 200 random planets kept the population figures, but individual labels moved. The decidable fractions changed by at most 6 points, while 5-11 % of labels lost decidability and 5-12 % gained it. Between the runs, p_true correlated at 0.67-0.79, and the retrieval's accuracy was 1.5-6 points lower. Labels with p_true below 0.05 (one in 3,820 in the noise-free population) appeared on 8 of 800. This is about the 1 % rate at which a calibrated posterior is confidently wrong on data. The four learners made 590 errors on these realisations. Of these, 18.5 % were recoverable by the noise-free posterior and 14.9 % were decidable by the noisy one. The noisy posterior got 60 % of the 590 errors right at p ≥ 0.5, against 73 % for the noise-free one. Of those right answers, 71 % were on present labels. The recoverable share defined by the noise-free posterior is therefore an upper bound. On clean spectra it overstates the share by about a fifth of its own value.

**The Δχ² test used under departures.** The Δχ² test of Section 2.4 was calibrated against this posterior, for use with a departure in the counterfactual model (Section 3.3). The test and the posterior agreed in rank but not in scale. Over the 955 planets, the Spearman correlations between Δχ² and logit p_true were 0.86 for CH₄, 0.80 for H₂O, 0.88 for CO₂ and 0.89 for NH₃. The scale, however, differed by an order of magnitude from the conventional one. The posterior crossed 0.95 at Δχ² = 0.45 (CH₄), 0.98 (H₂O), 0.90 (CO₂) and 0.56 (NH₃). The conventional thresholds would be 3.84, the χ² value for one parameter at 95 %, or 9, the 3σ value. The retrieval integrates over the opposite class's volume. As a result, a label is decidable when the best opposite-class fit sits only about one σ away in total χ². At the conventional 3σ, the test would call 78-88 % of labels undecidable. The calibrated thresholds are used everywhere below. The test took 207 core-hours on this population, against the retrieval's 365. It is therefore a check on the retrieval and not the cheap substitute it was meant to be.

### 3.4 Real JWST spectra: what the classifier finds and where it fails

This section answers the second question of Section 1. It reads real JWST spectra, converted to the classifier's input as described in Section 2.6, first for WASP-39 b and then for the panel.

**WASP-39 b.** At JWST precision with all 104 inputs, the classifier agreed with the published composition (24, 25, 29, 30, 78) on every label the literature decides. Three of the four learners called H₂O present, and all four called CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at 10⁻⁴. The published CO₂ abundance of about 10⁻⁴·⁵ exceeds a threshold of 10⁻⁵, and at that threshold two to three learners called CO₂ present. NH₃ was called absent in all 24 configurations of the sensitivity matrix and with each of three MIRI reductions. The consortium's own band metric agreed with the majority. The MLP called H₂O absent (score 0.00) in 23 of the 24 configurations. It also called H₂O absent on the benchmark's own best-fit model binned directly onto the 104 bins, so the conversion did not cause the miss. Neither the composition nor a grey deck caused it either. All four learners called H₂O present on the clear H₂/He twin at the published abundances (MLP score 1.00), and on twins with grey decks at pressures down to 10 Pa. Under Ariel Tier-1 noise at WASP-39 b's one transit, H₂O was reported in 58 % of draws with all inputs, 86 % without the three optical points and 100 % on the clear twin. Under the training prior at the Tier-1 σ, the retrieval decided CH₄ absent. It gave H₂O a p_true of 0.0006, although three of the four learners called H₂O present.

**The panel.** After the two withdrawals of Section 2.6, the literature decides 64 labels over the 26 reductions: 38 absent and 26 present. Calling every label absent would therefore score 38. At JWST precision the re-implemented classifier was right on 36 of the absent labels but on 3 of the present ones: the H₂O of WASP-39 b, WASP-17 b and WASP-94A b. Its score was 43.5, with a split counted as half. The AIRS-only variant reproduced 15 present labels. On the fifteen reductions with measured data both below 1.1 µm and above 1.95 µm, the all-input, no-optical and AIRS-only scores were 26.5, 29.5 and 32.0 of 38. Over all 64 labels, the three scores were 23.5, 25.5 and 29.0 of 38 on the first batch, 14.5, 16.0 and 16.0 of 19 on the second, and 5.5, 6.0 and 5.0 of 7 on the third. The first batch holds six of the eight methane reductions and most of the gain from dropping the optical points. At Tier-1 noise the re-implemented classifier found 3.1 of the 26 present labels on average over the draws. Its score was 1.6 labels above calling every label absent.

**What the coverage mask does.** Bins with no data are filled flat, and that alone misled the re-implemented classifier. The mask control tests this by reading each clear twin through the reduction's own mask at the real errors. Of the 24 present labels with a clear twin, the re-implemented classifier missed 11 on the twin itself, and the AIRS-only variant missed 3. These eleven labels fail the mask control. They are the ten rows of Table E2 whose twin is missed, plus the H₂O of WASP-94A b. No absent label fails. A miss on these labels could not be attributed to the real atmosphere. With full coverage, all four learners found the same twins of the planets with AIRS-range data only. The eleven labels stay in the count, because a pipeline would not know the cause. Every design is also scored on the 47 labels that pass, 34 absent and 13 present (Table E4).

Four single labels need separate comment. GJ 9827 d's H₂O was missed even though its twin, read through the same NIRISS mask, was found in every draw. The miss arose because the real spectrum showed no dispersion above its errors in 63 % of resamples. Every learner called HIP 67522 b's CO₂ present, with every input and under every training set. That false label stands. No NH₃ false positive reached a majority on any panel planet with all inputs, although TOI-270 d's NH₃ in the Felix reduction was split. The AIRS-only variant called WASP-52 b's NH₃ present. That cell is not scored, because its retrieval reports no evidence but sets no limit.

**The methane planets.** With all inputs, the classifier called CH₄ absent or split on all eight reductions (Table 8). Under Tier-1 noise, a majority of learners reported methane in at most 1 % of draws (reporting frequencies 4-23 %). The published methane detections on TOI-270 d and K2-18 b are at 4.2-10.8σ in each paper's preferred analysis, and at 3.8σ at the lowest across the cases of one study (31, 32, 34, 35, 79). TOI-1231 b's detection has ln B = 54.5-69.6 (36). The detection on GJ 1214 b is at 2.0-2.4σ per molecule, and that on TOI-732 c at 3.0-4.6σ depending on the retrieval set-up (86, 87). Counted per planet by majority over reductions, the classifier missed four of the five planets and split on TOI-1231 b. The AIRS-only variant found CH₄ on seven of the eight reductions and four of the five planets, with no false methane on the 14 absent CH₄ labels. It missed GJ 1214 b, the weakest detection of the set. Its retrieved atmosphere is CO₂-dominated, a case the H₂/He grid does not contain.

On six of the seven reductions with a twin, the clear twins show that the methane misses were not caused by the noise or by the classifier's skill inside its simulator. Read through the same mask at the real per-bin errors, these twins were found by a majority of learners in 63-100 % of draws. On the seventh, the Holmberg reduction of TOI-270 d with 63 filled bins, the twin was never found, so the coverage causes its miss. Replacing every real bin below 1.95 µm with the twin's brought methane to a majority on all seven reductions. It also brought 9 of 34 absent labels to a majority, however, and a methane-free twin's bins restored methane on three of the seven. These bins therefore move the re-implemented classifier's verdicts in general and do not locate a methane signal. In the operational sense of Section 2.5, these errors are recoverable.

**Muting.** Most of the giant planets on the panel had smaller spectral features than any H₂/He atmosphere the population allows for them. Twelve giants and Neptune-mass planets on the panel have a full published abundance set and a clear twin at their catalogue mass. They are called "the twelve giants" below: HAT-P-26 b, WASP-107 b, WASP-39 b, GJ 3470 b, WASP-166 b, WASP-17 b, WASP-80 b, HD 209458 b, WASP-96 b, WASP-52 b, WASP-94A b and HAT-P-12 b. HD 189733 b and HAT-P-18 b, which do not meet that test, also appear in Figure 4. Ten of the twelve are muted by a factor of 2.3 to 5.3 against their clear twins (Figure 4; 68 % intervals 0.1-1.2 wide). In every resample, these ten lie 1.7 to 2.7 times beyond the most muted H₂/He atmosphere the population allows at their mass, radius, irradiation and published composition. The same holds over the AIRS bins alone, so the muting is in the molecular bands and not only in a haze slope. The result depends on the published composition. With a deck at 5 × 10² Pa, H₂O at 10⁻⁴ and the other gases at 10⁻⁷, the population reached the real dispersion on nine of the ten; with H₂O at 10⁻⁵, it reached it on all ten. Within the prior, amplitude is degenerate with abundance. "Absent" is therefore what the prior would answer for a muted spectrum. The two giants inside the prior are the two least muted. They are WASP-96 b (1.5, 1.4-1.6; the largest water feature of the set, 88) and WASP-52 b (2.3, 2.2-2.4, against a prior maximum of 2.4).

WASP-39 b shows that the distance rules miss this muting. Its real dispersion over the measured bins is 325 ppm, against 1,190 ppm for the clear twin. That is a muting of 3.7, where the population reaches 2.0 at its bulk parameters (Table D1). Yet WASP-39 b is inside the training distribution by every distance rule (k-NN distance 6.2 against the decline threshold 11.25). Those rules work on normalised spectra, and normalisation removes amplitude.

The simulator can reach the giants' muting, but only rarely. The population's own clouds mute its median planet by 1 %, its 99th percentile by a factor of 2.5 and its most muted planet by 6.3, at the prior's 5 × 10² Pa limit (Figure 4b). The giants' muting therefore occurs in about one planet in a hundred or fewer (0.1-1.2 %), and only near that limit, whereas ten of the twelve giants are muted that far. Reaching it in numbers requires what the giants' JWST retrievals report and the population lacks: metallicities of tens to hundreds of times solar, hazes or patchy clouds. The four H₂-dominated methane planets are muted by only 0.6-2.0, inside the population's range (Table 8).

The scaled-twin test separates scale from shape. At JWST precision, a twin scaled to the real dispersion was found about as often as the unscaled twin. The counts were 11 against 12 of the 23 present labels whose twin can be scaled, and 20 against 20 AIRS-only. GJ 9827 d's twin was not scaled, because its spectrum shows no dispersion above its errors. Normalisation removes pure scaling, so the real spectra differed from their twins in shape as well as size. At Tier-1 noise, scale did cost labels: 4 against 8, and 6 against 15 AIRS-only. Uniform muting therefore lowers what Tier-1 can find on a real planet, whatever the input. Aerosol training recovered five of the shape losses (Appendix E).

HIP 67522 b's false CO₂, on which no flag fires, is not explained by these tests (Appendix E).

### 3.5 A routing method, and its test on new planets

The routing method gained labels on the panel it was built on and nothing on 12 new planets. It addresses only those misses of Section 3.4 that another input or training population can find, which are recoverable in the operational sense of Section 2.5. It neither recognises nor withholds labels the spectrum does not decide; these stay undecided (Section 3.6). Its rule is in Section 2.7.

**On the panel and in simulation.** On the panel it was built on, the routing method scored 52.0 of 64 at JWST precision, with none withheld. That is 8.5 labels above the re-implemented classifier. About two fifths of that gain lay on labels the coverage mask hides, and at Tier-1 noise the gain was 2.3. Most of the panel gain lay in the first batch, on which the flags were built. The method led the two simpler fixes by only 2.0 (−1.0 to 5.6) and 1.5 (−2.9 to 5.3) labels. These fixes are the AIRS-only variant and the haze+deck-retrained design used everywhere. The five existing rules used as routers scored 45.0-47.0.

In simulation the routing method cost 4.8 points of clean accuracy and gained 8.7 under the column-filling haze. In the selection, it lost fewer CH₄ targets than the re-implemented classifier only under haze and spots. Retraining on hazes and decks, which is not part of the routing method, removed mostly recoverable errors, as classed by the clean-spectrum posterior. Under the column-filling haze, it removed 315 of the re-implemented classifier's 675 recoverable errors and 71 of its 719 undecidable ones. The remedies, the two flags, every design's scores and the selection results are in Appendix E.

**The independent test.** The gain on the panel did not carry over to new planets. Frozen and scored once on the 12 new planets, the routing method scored 16.0 of 24 at JWST precision (Table 10). That is the same as calling every label absent, and the same as the re-implemented classifier, which called all 24 labels absent. At Tier-1 noise the routing method scored 15.9 against 16.2. The interval on the difference, −3.5 to 4.3, is wide enough to include both no gain and the panel's rate, which is about three labels on this batch. The test therefore gives no support for a gain, but does not rule one out. Five of the 8 present labels fail the mask control. On the 19 labels that pass, the method scored 15.5 against 16.0.

The method found 2.5 of the 8 present labels and lost 2.5 of the 16 absent ones. It called CH₄ present on HATS-6 b, where the published abundance of 10⁻⁵·⁴ is a detection below the threshold. It called H₂O present on TOI-5205 b, whose spectrum the source paper finds heavily contaminated by its star. It also split WASP-121 b's CO₂. Under the label rule, which was fixed before the run, these verdicts are errors. The methane pattern of the panel did repeat. All three methane labels were called absent with all inputs. The AIRS-only variant found two and split the third, and the method found TOI-6894 b's and split the other two.

Of the six expectations, three were not met, among them the positive gain (Appendix C, rows H1-H6). One was met in part, and two were met, one of them only trivially. The evidence shows that the method's gain on the panel did not repeat on new planets, whereas the AIRS-only variant's methane recovery did.

### 3.6 The retrieval as fallback, against the existing methods

This section tests the existing methods of the third question (Section 1) in simulation. It asks whether the retrieval, the fallback for every design so far, shares the classifier's failure under haze, and what repairs it. It also asks whether the five existing rules recognise that failure, and what a flag buys at a fixed retrieval budget. Unless stated otherwise, the haze is the column-filling one.

**The clear-model retrieval under haze.** It shared the classifier's failure on hazed spectra and failed more confidently (Table 11). Where it completed, it called almost every present label absent and drove every abundance 3.5-4 dex toward the prior floor. The misfit showed in the Bayesian evidence Z. It is the aerosol misspecification mode known from retrievals of real spectra (16, 116), in which the aerosol parametrisation changes the retrieval solution (117). An analyst inspecting the fit could see it, but a pipeline that reads only the posterior could not. Used as a gate, the evidence caught nearly every hazed planet: 99.6 % fell below the first percentile of the clean evidences. The departure was largest where the classifier failed. In population medians, with the offset removed, it was 18σ per bin in the three optical points, 4.2σ over Ariel's NIRSpec channel and 1.9σ over AIRS.

The free-haze retrieval fits the haze density as one free parameter. It completed on every spectrum and recovered the density on every planet. It stayed within 5 points of the clean retrieval's accuracy on every molecule, and its evidence stayed near the clean retrieval's. It recovered 222 of the 224 planets with CH₄ present and decidable on the clean spectrum and kept 75 % of them decidable. The repair is a best case. The truth, with its particle size, extinction law and vertical extent, lies inside the one-parameter family. Four planets, among them the super-puff Kepler-79 d, fit badly with the radius held fixed. Even so, all their labels but one were right. Figure 3c compares the two retrievals. The haze removed nothing the labels needed; the clear-model retrieval's near-zero recall came from its model.

**A repair from the wrong family.** An aerosol model from the wrong family recovered a little over half of the accuracy the clear-model retrieval lost. A power-law aerosol opacity was fitted to the hazed spectra; this family does not contain the Lee-Mie truth. Its log cross-section at 1 µm (−34 to −24) and its slope (0 to 10) were free (`powerlaw_summary.py`). The run was stopped at 117 of a fixed random set of 200 planets to free the machine for other checks. The accuracies had moved by at most 4 points since 90 had finished. Its accuracy was 84, 73, 84 and 78 % for CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃. This lay between the accuracies of the clear-model retrieval (66, 54, 65 and 56 %) and the free-haze retrieval (98, 83, 96 and 93 %) on the same planets. Its recall of present labels was 84, 60, 68 and 86 %, against none for the clear-model retrieval. It found all 26 planets in this subset with CH₄ present and decidable on the clean spectrum. Labels with p_true below 0.05 fell to 3-8 %, against 32-44 % for the clear-model and at most 1 % for the free-haze retrieval. The fit revealed the wrong family. The slope sat above 9, near the prior's limit, on 97 % of planets. The median log Z was −31, against −16 for the free-haze and −264 for the clear-model retrieval. Most of the clear-model retrieval's failure therefore came from having no aerosol at all, not the wrong one, although the wrong one cost 10-15 points of accuracy.

On the classifier's noisy realisations of the 200-planet subsample, noise moved the free-haze retrieval's labels by a few points and left the clear-model retrieval's where its model had put them (Appendix C).

**A retrieval budget on clean spectra.** On clean spectra the retrieval was the selection method. At 60 % Tier-2 slots, ranking by its present-posterior lost 0 of 359 CH₄-bearing and 2 of 364 H₂O targets, against 20 and 51 for the classifier. It cost 365 core-hours. No cheap rule allocated a partial budget much better than chance. The confidence rules were no better than random. They sent the retrieval to the undecidable labels, where it reads the right way on 81-86 % of them but where, at 60 % slots, the decided-present and undecidable planets largely fit the queue already, so a better ranking there moves few targets across the cut. Recognising those labels is worth a withheld verdict, not a retrieval. An ordering by the measured Δχ², the ceiling of any such recogniser, saved only about four of 359 CH₄ targets at a 20 % budget. By contrast, declining the 30 % of planets the margin ranked least confident raised CH₄ accuracy on the rest from 86.4 to 94.6 %. The full comparison is in Appendix C.

**The existing rules under the shift.** The five existing rules did not recognise the haze failure either (Table D3). Every rule's selective credit was negative under haze (−17.5 to −25.7 points), spots and the high deck. At the threshold set for 10 %, the margin rule declined 7.4 % of clean planets but 0.9 % of hazed ones. Conformal coverage fell to 73.7 % under haze. The three distance rules separated hazed from clean spectra (AUROC 0.90-0.94) but did not rank errors within them (0.44-0.52). They were a population alarm, not a filter. No candidate had positive selective credit under haze or spots either (Table C1). Selective credit cannot measure a shift flag, which is a score that detects a departure from the training population. The optical-excess score is such a flag, and it flagged every hazed planet.

**A budget routed by a shift flag.** Routed to a retrieval that models the departure, a shift flag allocated a 20 % budget within noise of a haze oracle. Routed to the clear-model retrieval, it lost more targets than no retrieval (CH₄ 71 against 29, H₂O 94 against 73, either molecule 141 against 124). This happened although that retrieval's evidence flagged 99.6 % of those spectra (Table 12; Figure 5). The free-haze retrieval of the optical-excess score's top 20 % matched the oracle within the draw-to-draw noise, for H₂O as well. It lost 40 H₂O targets, as the oracle did, against 73 for the classifier alone. With a fifth of the planets hazed, a clear-model retrieval of every planet, read by its posterior, lost more CH₄ targets than the classifier alone. It lost fewer H₂O (70 against 73) and either-molecule targets (115 against 124). With a tenth hazed, it beat the classifier on H₂O (35 against 65) and either molecule (65 against 114) but still lost more CH₄.

The confidence rules could not route the budget, but the rules that detect the shift could. The margin and learner-spread rules did no better than a random budget, because they held none of the hazed planets. The classifier was confident on those planets. The distance rules also detect the shift, and as routers they too were within noise of the oracle. They were less selective, however. By the optical-excess score, the top 20 % held 98 % of the hazed and 1 % of the clean planets; by the Mahalanobis rule, it held 73 and 7 %. The optical-excess ordering again matched the oracle with 10 % of the planets hazed. With 50 % hazed, more than a 20 % budget can cover, it stayed within noise of the oracle.

A clear-model retrieval of every planet, re-run as the free-haze retrieval wherever its evidence fails, lost no CH₄ target and two H₂O targets at every hazed fraction. It cost about 520 core-hours, against 127 for the budget. The limit on the routed pipeline was therefore the budget, not the fallback. With a budget for every planet, the evidence gate took the place of the flag. Of the expectations committed after the pilot, three were met in full and two in part (Table C1).

### 3.7 A new design: the matched ensemble

This section answers the fourth question of Section 1: what the matched ensemble of Section 2.10 repairs, what it leaves, and whether that holds on grids from other codes and on real labels.

**On model grids it had not seen.** Frozen and scored once, the matched ensemble scored above every other design on both held-out grids at both precisions (Table 13; Figure 6). On the local-condensation grid, it gained 0.137 of balanced credit over the routing method at JWST precision (95 % interval 0.110 to 0.156) and 0.105 at Tier-1 noise (0.089 to 0.119). On the photochemical grid, it gained 0.115 (0.089 to 0.136) and 0.133 (0.120 to 0.144), a result the committed expectation had rated less likely than not. The gain held for each of the four gases at both precisions on both grids, and every interval excluded zero. These intervals resample planets, with a planet's reductions kept together. Because each grid's models are reused across planets (up to 13 rows per photochemical model), a bootstrap crossed over planets and source models was also run after the grids had been scored (`review_checks/grid_crossed_bootstrap.py`). It widened the four pooled intervals by 5-29 % and left all of them, and all 16 per-gas intervals, above zero; the narrowest was CH₄ on the photochemical grid at JWST precision (0.005 to 0.120). The gain was positive in every temperature, metallicity and C/O bin of the photochemical grid; the one bin whose crossed interval reached zero was solar metallicity at JWST precision (+0.042; −0.038 to +0.102), where the routing method does best. Grid truth is the mean mole fraction over 10-10⁴ Pa; other windows (1-10³ Pa, 10²-10⁵ Pa) and a log-mean flip 1-4.5 % of labels, mostly within half a decade of 10⁻⁴, and move the gain by at most 0.017 (`results/round3/grid_truth_windows.txt`). The grid scoring uses a simplified observation operator, each reduction's coverage mask with independent noise per bin. On 50 models per reduction, every design was also scored through the full conversion of Section 2.6, with the model spectrum sampled onto each reduction's native JWST wavelengths, noise drawn per JWST point at its own error, and the real overlap-weighted binning and fitted inter-mode offsets applied (Appendix E; `review_checks/grid_full_operator.py`). This check was run after the grids had been scored and chose nothing. Under the full operator the ensemble lost 0.003-0.009 of balanced credit, the other designs moved by less than 0.005, and the gain over the routing method changed by at most 0.008 (−0.008 to −0.002), every interval still excluding zero. The loss is the price of training with independent noise: under the full conversion, neighbouring measured bins carry correlated noise (median lag-1 correlation 0.12, up to 0.56), from JWST points that straddle bin edges and from fitted offsets that act as common-mode noise. It was largest for H₂O on the local-condensation grid at JWST precision (0.703 against 0.512) and for CH₄ on the photochemical grid at Tier-1 noise (0.828 against 0.630).

The price was false alarms, which the balanced credit already counts. On the photochemical grid, the false-present rate was 8.1 and 11.6 % at JWST precision and Tier-1 noise, against 5.5 and 3.0 % for the routing method. On the local-condensation grid, it was 17.3 % at both precisions, against 13.0 and 5.4 %. The local-condensation rate exceeded both the 15 % limit committed beforehand and the 10 % the thresholds were set for. The simulated cap therefore did not transfer exactly to another code's chemistry. By gas, the rate reached 34 % for CH₄ on the local-condensation grid. The share of present calls that were right held up: 0.66 and 0.59 on the local-condensation grid (routing method 0.54 and 0.51) and 0.87 and 0.80 on the photochemical grid (0.85 and 0.80). At a prevalence of 10 %, these shares would be 0.28 and 0.22 on the local-condensation grid, and 0.46 and 0.33 on the photochemical grid. At low prevalence, a present call from any of these designs is therefore a reason to look again, not a detection (`results/round3/round3_real.txt`). NH₃ stayed weak, at 0.550 on the photochemical grid at JWST precision. At the routing method's own false-present rate, with the ensemble's threshold moved per gas to match it, the ensemble's recall of present labels was higher in all 16 gas-grid-precision cases, pooled 0.55 against 0.27 and 0.22 against 0.10 on the local-condensation grid and 0.55 against 0.35 and 0.29 against 0.14 on the photochemical grid (`results/round3/matched_fp_recall.txt`).

**Against a full retrieval.** A full retrieval scored below the matched ensemble at both precisions, against the committed expectation. A nested-sampling retrieval with the model of Section 2.4 (four free gases in H₂/He, the radius fixed and CO off; `grid_retrieval.py`) was run on 90 spectra drawn at random from the photochemical grid. The spectra passed through each reduction's mask and carried the noise draws the designs saw. The retrieval was expected to score above the matched ensemble at JWST precision and level with it at Tier-1 noise (Table C1, row Y7). Instead it scored below it at both. It fell short by 0.197 (0.128 to 0.269) at JWST precision, on the 77 spectra that finished within three hours, and by 0.095 (0.039 to 0.150) at Tier-1 noise. An error that worked against the retrieval was then found. Its temperature prior was centred on the grid model's own temperature, not on the catalogue temperature to which each spectrum had been rescaled. A rerun with the prior centred correctly did not help (row Y8). It scored 0.540 against 0.777 on 89 spectra at JWST precision and 0.523 against 0.649 at Tier-1 noise. These were differences of 0.236 (0.165 to 0.305) and 0.126 (0.065 to 0.186).

The retrieval's verdicts on these spectra were poorly determined: between the runs, 4 to 15 of each gas's verdicts flipped. The likely cause is a misfit. The grid's atmospheres carry other absorbers and mean molecular weights up to 15. A four-gas H₂/He model can match these only by misplacing abundance. The retrieval's false-present rate was 22-29 %, against 8-13 % for the ensemble. The comparison is therefore with this study's retrieval, not with retrievals of richer chemistry. The routing method also scored above the retrieval at JWST precision, by 0.060 and 0.099 in the two runs (the first interval reaching −0.010). At Tier-1 noise it scored level with the retrieval. The retrieval took a median of 22 to 143 minutes per spectrum.

**Stability.** Between seeds, the per-reduction design's vote moved by up to 2.5 of the 24 fourth-batch labels. Over three seeds of the whole matched ensemble, balanced credit moved by at most 0.011 on any grid. The scores of the fourth batch and of the 17 further real labels did not move. **What the verdict reads.** Training on correlated chemistry could make a verdict follow the other gases and not the molecule. Two checks tested this. The first was a band refit, with each gas's main bands removed from the input; it may relearn from other gases and bands. The second was a counterfactual, with the gas's opacity removed everywhere and its chemistry unchanged; it asks what the trained ensemble uses. Without the main bands, most of the score survived on the grids, so those bands were not all the matched ensemble used. At JWST precision the score was 0.736 of 0.765, 0.678 of 0.711 and 0.726 of 0.773.

Without the opacity, the verdict largely went (Table 14). Where a gas truly exceeded 10⁻⁴ on the photochemical grid, it was called present on 62.7 % of full spectra and on 11.4 % of spectra without its opacity. So 82 % of the present calls flipped to absent. The committed expectation was that about 35 % would still be called present without the opacity, against the 11.4 % found. The labels could thus be largely predicted from other information on these grids. Yet on the photochemical grid the trained ensemble mostly read the molecule's own absorption, over more of the spectrum than its main bands. About a fifth of its present calls, and a quarter for H₂O and CO₂, rested on other gases. On the same spectra, the routing method fell from 0.354 to 0.020, and 95 % of its present calls flipped. Its own-absorption share was 0.334, against the ensemble's 0.513 (`results/round3/routing_counterfactual.txt`). This was measured on one grid without aerosols.

On truly absent labels, removing the gas's opacity lowered the false-present rate for every gas at both precisions (Table 14). At JWST precision, most of the ensemble's false-present calls on this grid therefore came from the gas's own absorption below the threshold, not from other gases. On the real labels, removing the main bands halved the credit on present labels (30.0 to 15.0 of 41; 15 verdicts lost, none gained) and left absent credit level (54.0 to 55.0 of 64). The plan also named the false-present rate on labels outside a molecule's correlated range for reporting beside the score. It was not computed.

**The two error classes again.** Under every departure, most of the errors the matched ensemble removed were recoverable (59-73 %), classed by the clean-spectrum posterior of Section 2.5. On clean spectra, by contrast, most lay on undecidable labels (72 of 112). For comparison, the re-implemented classifier made 154 recoverable errors and 562 on undecidable labels on clean spectra. Under the column-filling haze, the re-implemented classifier made 675 recoverable errors and 719 on undecidable labels (mean over the four learners, summed over molecules). The haze+deck-retrained design made 360 and 648, and the matched ensemble 202 and 544. The ensemble's errors on undecidable labels stayed at 490 or above in every case. This was below the re-implemented classifier's count but about twice the roughly 250 the retrieval makes on those labels. A better learner leans the right way more often on an unsettled label, as the retrieval does on 81-86 % of them (Section 3.2), but it does not settle them. These counts ignore information a departure removes. The 372 errors counted as recoverable under threefold noise (`results/round3/round3_ariel.txt`; not in Table 16) therefore lie on labels decided at the nominal noise. Many of those labels are not decided at threefold noise.

**Recognising its own unreliable verdicts.** The ensemble's probability ranked labels well. Over all gases, its AUROC was 0.75-0.79 on local condensation and 0.87-0.91 on the photochemical grid. On the 105 real labels at JWST precision, it was 0.89. Whether a decline rule adds to that has to be measured within a gas. A cut pooled over gases keeps more of the gases the design reads best. That raises the score with no gain in recognition. Pooled, the margin rule, which is the distance of the probability from 0.5, appeared to add 0.075 to 0.117, mostly from this mix. The within-gas test kept 70 % of labels within each gas and averaged balanced credit over the gases. At JWST precision and Tier-1 noise, the margin rule then added 0.038 and 0.016 on rainout, 0.012 and 0.007 on local condensation and 0.045 and 0.035 on the photochemical grid. The spread of the five networks added 0.007 to 0.035, and the three distance rules −0.019 to +0.008. The candidate committed beforehand, the distance from the molecule's threshold, did worse. The margin rule was therefore preferred after these grids had been seen (`results/round3/round3_rules.txt`, `round3_margin_within.txt`). A variant stratified by call within each gas added 0.044 to 0.078 on the three grids but did not help on the real labels. It was chosen after the grids had been seen, so this result is post hoc (Appendix C).

**Against the existing rules.** The gain from declining was not separated from that of the best existing rule. On the re-implemented classifier, the same two confidence rules lowered balanced credit by 0.012 to 0.037. The best rule there, the k-NN distance in every case, added 0.006 to 0.033. The ensemble's best-rule gain minus the classifier's was −0.002 to +0.026. Its interval excluded zero in two of six cases (rainout at JWST precision, 0.004 to 0.044; the photochemical grid at Tier-1 noise, 0.011 to 0.029).

What differs is the combination. Confidence rules that failed on the classifier worked on the ensemble, and its kept labels scored far higher. On the photochemical grid at JWST precision, the kept labels scored 0.768 averaged over gases, against 0.614 for the classifier with its best rule. This difference was almost entirely due to the better learner.

At Ariel's coverage, the margin rule ranked errors better under aerosols and spots than under extra noise and muting. Under aerosols and spots, it ranked errors at AUROC 0.70-0.78 (the classifier 0.64-0.78). The kept labels reached 89.4 % accuracy under the column-filling haze, against 72.8 %. Under extra noise and muting, its AUROC fell to 0.62-0.67, below the classifier's 0.67-0.70, with H₂O at 0.49-0.56. Declining did not help selection, here or with the classifier's rules. Sending the 10 % least confident labels to the back of the queue lost more targets than not (Table C1). The ensemble's distance from the threshold recognised undecidable labels on clean spectra about as well as the classifier (0.59-0.79; Table C1, row Y4).

**On real Ariel targets under equilibrium chemistry.** The second population of Section 3.2 tested whether the design repairs the errors the spectrum can settle. The matched ensemble was run unchanged, at Ariel's coverage and Tier-1 noise, over ten noise draws on the 200 retrieved planets. It made about half as many errors on decidable labels as the re-implemented classifier: 21.1 ± 2.6 against 43.3 ± 3.3 (one draw, difference 20.0; 9.0 to 30.0). On undecidable labels it made 58.1 ± 2.1 errors against 56.0 ± 2.4, as the two classes predict. Over all 965 Mission Candidate Sample planets rendered with this chemistry (200 of them retrieved), its balanced credit was 0.883, 0.853, 0.731 and 0.920 for CH₄, H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃, against 0.839, 0.752, 0.547 and 0.660. These expectations were committed beforehand. Its training population shares FastChem with this test, which flatters it. The atmospheres are also clear. The false-present rate reached 23 % for CH₄ and 25 % for H₂O (the classifier 29 and 18 %), above the 15 % expected. CO₂ accuracy fell from 97.0 to 92.2 % on a label present on 2 % of planets (`results/identifiability_mcs_ensemble.txt`).

**Where the gain comes from.** The candidate that came second in the selection, a four-learner vote on the broad population with balanced classes (Section 2.10), was scored on the held-out grids after they had been seen, so this comparison is post hoc (`review_checks/ablation_vote.py`). Frozen and unchanged, it scored 0.691 and 0.608 on the local-condensation grid and 0.793 and 0.682 on the photochemical grid, between the routing method and the ensemble in three of the four cases and above the ensemble at JWST precision on the photochemical grid (−0.020; −0.047 to +0.002); the ensemble led by 0.019 (−0.006 to 0.039), 0.028 and 0.018 elsewhere, at false-present rates of 8-17 % against the vote's 11-22 %. Most of the gain over the routing method therefore lies in what the two candidates share: training on each spectrum's measured bins at its real errors, a broad training population and calibrated thresholds. The network ensemble and the correlated chemistry add about 0.02 and a lower false-present rate. Of the intermediate steps, the per-reduction design gained 0.016 to 0.050 over the routing method on the three grids (Table 13). This was a real but small gain, and it led to changing the population. On the 17 further real labels, the matched ensemble scored the same as the haze+deck-retrained design (Table 15). Those labels therefore did not separate the better designs (Appendix E).

## 4. Discussion

**What the two classes mean for Tier-2 selection.** A pipeline that reads either answer of the classifier as a verdict dismisses planets that the data did not settle. The reason is that about four fifths of the classifier's clean errors sit on labels that the spectrum does not decide at the 0.95 level under the training prior (Section 3.2). Those labels are unsettled, not unreadable: a reference rule reading the same noisy spectra removes 38-48 % of the classifier's error, so the 0.95 partition measures assurance, and the excess error is the part a better design can take. The undecidable fraction depends on the population, the precision and the forward model, not on the learner. For water it is half the planets at Tier-1 precision. This figure can be read alongside the retrieval-based detection fractions of Bocchieri et al. (50) and the tier reassessment of Radica et al. (5). About a third of this water fraction consists of labels within half a decade of 10⁻⁴. That line is set by the label definition, not by the physics.

**Real spectra against the training prior.** At JWST precision the re-implemented classifier is right on 36 of 38 absent labels but finds few present ones. On the new planets it called every label absent (Section 3.5). Several causes contribute. Coverage explains part of the shortfall. Ten of the missed present labels are missed on the clear twin as well, and this part says nothing about Ariel. The inputs below 1.95 µm explain another part. The AIRS-only learner finds methane that the re-implemented classifier misses, both on the panel the routing method was built on and on new planets, at the price of some false positives. Another part may be the prior. At their published compositions the real giants are muted beyond what the training prior allows, but not at lower abundances. A retrieval under that prior at Tier-1 precision also reads them, and the methane planets, as absent, as a shared prior would. The evidence does not separate that cause from others. The classifier's input carries no amplitude, the classifier and the retrieval disagree on WASP-39 b's water, and the retrieval also fixes the radius.

Precision then limits any input. A uniform muting by M lowers the Δχ² of (eq1) by about M². Even these bright planets, with a median Δχ² of 15.9 against 1.45 for the population, therefore keep about 43 % of their labels undecidable. That share is near the population's 39 % by the same test (Table 7). A training population for real Tier-1 spectra therefore needs the aerosols, metallicities and abundances that the JWST retrievals report. The matched ensemble trains on such a population. Its present credit at Tier-1 noise on the real labels rises from the routing method's 7.2 to 17.3 of 34. This rise is consistent with this reading, although those labels had been seen. The muting census is a cheap check of whether a real spectrum lies where the prior can reach at its published composition. For the sub-Neptunes with the highest mean molecular weights, the spectrum's clear H₂/He twin is itself an approximation.

**Why confidence rules fail and when a shift flag helps.** The five existing rules did not recognise the haze failure, because the classifier is confident on hazed spectra. The three distance rules do detect the shift, but they rank errors within it no better than chance. The first of these results is the loss of calibration under dataset shift reported for image and text classifiers (51), here seen in a physical setting. The second, the failure of the distance rules to rank errors within the shift, is a finding of this study. The selective-risk evaluation argued for by Jaeger et al. (72) makes it visible. A rule that declines the whole hazed population accepts no hazed planet, and so it has no selective credit. The value of a shift flag therefore lies in routing rather than declining. Where the flag sends a spectrum decides the outcome. Routed to the clear-model retrieval, a flag that correctly detects the shift loses more targets than no flag (71 against 29). Routed to a retrieval that models the departure, the optical-excess score, the Mahalanobis rule and the haze oracle lose the same number of targets, within the draw-to-draw noise. The score's advantage over the distance rules therefore lies in its recall and selectivity on the hazed planets, not in the selection outcome.

Three points matter for a pipeline (Section 3.6). The classifier's own confidence marks only some of the labels that the spectra do not decide (AUROC 0.59-0.77). A retrieval budget spent on those labels does not help. The stakes of a flag are also modest in this simulation. With a fifth of the planets hazed and a 20 % budget, they amount to 16 of 359 methane targets (Table 12), or 3 % of the Tier-2 queue.

**What the routing method and the matched ensemble claim.** The routing method combines known elements in a way that appears to be new. The elements are domain randomisation (68) and feature amplitude in scale heights (118-120). Searches of arXiv and the main journals on 30 October 2026 found no published method that routes a simulator-trained atmospheric classifier on real spectra by such flags. The closest work repairs an amortised retrieval on WASP-39 b with a radius latent and domain-randomised training (49).

The matched ensemble does not route. Instead, it changes what the learners are trained on and how they decide. None of its elements is new alone. Simulation-based inference conditioned on the instrument, chemically consistent populations and calibrated thresholds are all established. Its claim rests on measurement. On the two held-out grids it scored above every earlier design. On the photochemical grid it also scored above a nested-sampling retrieval with this study's four-gas model. That retrieval likely misfits the grid's chemistry and is not the richest available. The counterfactual on that grid shows that the matched ensemble mostly reads the molecule's own absorption (Section 3.7). The design needs no retrieval and no published composition, only the catalogue's bulk parameters and the reduction's mask and errors.

On real labels the same gain appears at Tier-1 noise. At JWST precision it appears on the labels outside the routing method's panel. No design choice used these labels, but they had been seen, so they cannot serve as a blind test. On the routing method's panel the matched ensemble is not separated from the routing method, and it calls more absent labels present. Its false-present rate exceeded the committed limit on one grid, so a present verdict calls for follow-up. The grids are also models. Only the photochemical grid comes from another code family, and it has no aerosols. The ATMO grids' equilibrium chemistry may flatter a design trained on correlated chemistry, as the plan stated beforehand. About a fifth of the matched ensemble's present calls rest on other gases, so an atmosphere chemically unlike the training population could fool it. Declining on its probability adds little within a gas and should not be relied on where noise is underestimated. Undecidable labels stay undecided.

The next step is a prospective test. Because the rules are committed, the design can now be applied unchanged to each new public spectrum. Its verdict can be recorded before the spectrum's published retrieval is scored.

**Beyond Ariel.** The decomposition does not depend on transmission spectroscopy. Any inference system trained on a simulator and used to make decisions can be audited in two ways by running the expensive method on the population the cheap one is tested on, where that method's model is right: by the share of answers the data settle with confidence, and by the share of the cheap method's error that the expensive rule removes. The two are not the same partition: here a reference rule removed 38-48 % of the classifier's error although only 19-25 % of it fell on confidently settled labels (Section 3.2). Here the measurement worked on simulated spectra. On hazed and on real spectra the retrieval shared the misspecification and could not sort the errors. Where the condition holds, the measurement gives three things that a decision pipeline uses and a classifier cannot supply. The first is the prevalence of undecidable answers under a stated prior. The second is the share of the cheap method's error that a reference rule removes, a measured target for a better design. The third is a test of whether a proposed fallback shares the failure. In this study the fallback was applied under the departure it does not model. When a flag sent it the departed spectra, it lost more CH₄ and H₂O targets than no fallback. The procedure has been run on one classifier and its successor, so what could transfer is the procedure and not these numbers.

**What the mission can use.** Four things follow for the Tier-1 selection step. First, a label the classifier reports with low confidence should not be read as a verdict: under the training prior a third to a half of the labels are not settled by the spectrum, and the classifier's confidence marks those labels only in part (AUROC 0.59-0.77), so recognising them is worth a withheld verdict rather than a dismissal. A withheld verdict is an audit of the selected sample, not a queue policy: no policy for withheld labels was tested against straight probability ranking, and declining to the back of the queue lost targets (Section 3.7), so the recommendation is to flag such labels for the selection, not to prevent target loss by withholding. Second, the consortium's classifier, read on real JWST spectra, misses most present molecules on giant planets, whose features are weaker than its training assumes (Section 3.4); a classifier trained on the published population should not be used on Tier-1 giants without a test of this kind. Third, a better design removes a measured share of the error, 38-48 % of it on the simulated targets by the reference rule and about half of the errors on settled labels by the matched ensemble, at a cost of 8-17 % false-present calls; the stakes in the selection simulation are a few per cent of Tier-2 targets (16 of 359 methane targets at a 20 % budget), so the gain is worth having but does not change the survey. Fourth, every one of these numbers depends on the population, the prior and the forward model, and the classifier that flies in 2029 may differ from the one tested here, so the measurement, not the numbers, is what the mission can take: run the expensive method on the population the cheap one is meant for, split the errors, and size the two parts before the labels are used to select targets.

**Expectations.** Of the 99 expectations committed before their runs (Appendix C), 48 were met as written, 34 in part and 15 not met, and two were never run. Several expectations about the matched ensemble erred in its favour: how much it reads from other gases, and its accuracy at Ariel's coverage. The misses include WASP-39 b's water at Tier-1 noise and the undecidable fractions at the 0.95 level for three of four molecules. They also include the selective credit of every candidate, the routing method's gain on new planets and the matched ensemble's fall in absent credit on real labels. Two results were not among the expectations, and each changed the paper. First, the scale of the Δχ² test differed by an order of magnitude from the posterior. Second, at their published compositions the real giants lie outside the simulator population in feature amplitude. Yet they lie inside it in every bulk parameter and, for seven of the ten, by all three distance rules.

**Limitations of the identifiability measure.** Every retrieval-based number in Sections 3.2, 3.3 and 3.6, and the error classes of Section 3.7, comes from the noise-free spectrum with the Tier-1 σ in the likelihood. Decidability is therefore that of a spectrum without noise, and the recoverable shares are upper bounds. Retrievals of the classifier's realisation on a 200-planet subsample bound the difference on clean and hazed spectra (Sections 3.2 and 3.6). The retrieval's abundance prior is wider than the test population's. This gives absent labels a head start of 0.71, which the reference accuracy of 92-94 % includes. The Bayes-factor row of Table 4 removes this head start. The retrieval under the population's own prior measures it at five points of prevalence in the mean (Section 3.2). The decision scale itself is weak. The posterior crosses 0.95 at a Δχ² of only 0.45-0.98, because the prior's volume does much of the work, so a label can be decidable at 0.95 while an opposite-class atmosphere fits within the noise; the fitting test and the posterior are different measures, calibrated to agree on clean spectra, and under departures the test's classes are diagnostics (Section 2.5).

The main retrieval holds the planetary radius fixed, with only a small depth offset free. The degeneracy among radius, reference pressure, clouds and abundances is a known nuisance of absolute-abundance retrieval. With broadband data and a full model, however, the radius-pressure part has little effect on abundance estimates (121). In a test on 200 planets the radius was free to vary from 0.9 to 1.1 times the catalogue value. The undecidable fraction moved by at most two points for any gas (CH₄ 29.5 to 28.5 %, H₂O 56.0 to 56.5 %, CO₂ 45.0 to 46.0 % and NH₃ 33.5 to 31.5 %; `results/identifiability_free_radius.txt`). The result is therefore robust to freeing the radius alone on these spectra. Clouds were not freed with it, and the forward model is the training model throughout. A consortium, by contrast, would run a retrieval with a free radius and a full aerosol model on a flagged planet.

The departure-aware error classes for spots and the high deck hold the departure's strength at its true value. They also rest on the Δχ² test, which on clean spectra credits more errors as recoverable than the posterior does. For haze both measures were run. There, fitting the strength cost a quarter of the decidable present CH₄ labels and 18 % of all decidable labels. The nested-sampling settings (Section 2.4) are modest beside the 10⁵-10⁸ calls of richer retrievals. Two repeats on 50 planets, with another seed and with 400 live points, moved p_true by a median of 0.005. They moved no label across 0.5 and changed decidability for at most 5 % of labels.

**Limitations of the departures and the simulations.** The size of the column-filling haze used by the retrievals and the retrained designs depends on the model's top boundary. It also depends on an order-of-magnitude conversion to the observed opacity scale that holds at one pressure level (Section 2.3). For 0.1 µm particles of density 10³ kg m⁻³ at 1,000 K, a haze of 3 × 10⁷ m⁻³ exceeds 1 % of the gas mass above about 0.05 Pa. Its upper layers are therefore not physical. The classifier's failure and the flags' firing were re-measured under three confined hazes (Section 3.3). A power-law aerosol was also tried as a wrong-family repair (Section 3.6). The retrievals, the error classes and the budget, however, were not re-measured under confined hazes. On the model grids every design is scored through a simplified observation operator: each reduction's binary coverage mask with independent Gaussian noise at its per-bin errors (Section 2.10), whereas the real inputs pass through overlap-weighted binning and fitted inter-mode offsets (Section 2.6), which correlate neighbouring bins. The comparison between designs is fair, since all are scored through the same operator, and the gains were re-measured on a subset through the full conversion (Section 3.7; Appendix E): they moved by at most 0.008.

The mixed-population test of the retrieval budget used one haze density and one particle size, with the fallback's forward model correct up to one parameter. At that density the optical-excess score flags every hazed planet, so matching the haze oracle is the expected outcome. At the weaker density it finds only 60 % of the hazed planets. There the no-retrieval routing recovers little of the loss, which is in H₂O (Section 3.6). The budgeted retrieval at the weaker density or with mixed particle sizes is not reported. Nor is the free-haze fallback on spotted spectra, which the score sends to it.

The rules' clean decline rates are fixed by construction. In the held-out check of Section 2.3 (20 splits), every rule's decline rate under every departure is reproduced within 0.5 points. The clean rates lie at 7.6-10.7 %. The single-departure selection simulation of Table E6 is one noise draw. Over ten draws its rows move by 3-5 targets. Over the same ten draws the optical-excess score's routing recovers about half of the added loss, rather than the two thirds of Table E6 (Appendix E). The selection ranks by a molecule's probability, as a proxy for the mission's selection by theme and signal-to-noise. It does not model observing costs, target priorities or follow-up opportunities. The payload noise model was checked against published bands for AIRS only. The size of the optical points' noise is therefore unchecked, and the mechanism of Section 3.3 depends on it.

**Limitations of the real panel and the routing method.** No stellar-contamination correction was applied beyond the source papers' own, and all five methane planets orbit M dwarfs (Section 2.6). A stellar contribution to their short-wavelength shape therefore cannot be excluded. The simulated spot departure shows what 10-20 % coverage would cost, and the shape flag fires on two thirds of the spotted simulated planets. The real labels come from the literature, and ambiguous ones are not scored. Thirteen of the 104 cells carry a departure from the rule or a judgement call (Section 2.6; Appendix E). For every panel planet, JWST covers the Ariel grid only in part. The flat fill itself misleads the re-implemented classifier (Section 3.4). The conversion was validated only at near-complete coverage, and the mask control stands in at partial coverage. The flags' clean thresholds, set at Tier-1 noise, are also applied at JWST precision. There a spectrum carries much less noise than the flags saw in simulation. The eight methane reductions come from five planets, so the methane result is a pattern, not a rate. Two of the eight labels rest on detections at or below about 3σ.

The routing method was assembled on this panel, so its intervals are descriptive (Section 2.7). Half credit for a split or withheld verdict is itself a scoring choice. The muting intervals cover the depth errors only, not the reduction, the mass, the temperature or the published composition behind each twin. Fifteen of the 24 present labels with a twin have published abundances above the training ceiling of 10⁻² (Table 2). Those labels may therefore be out of the classifier's reach by construction. The real-panel retrievals use the training prior and a fixed radius. They and the grid retrieval of Section 3.7, whose verdicts moved between its two runs, are no test of what a richer retrieval could do. The independent batch is small, and five of its eight present labels fail the mask control. Many of its absent labels are also detections below the threshold. A classifier that reads real features is scored as wrong on those labels.

The amplitude flag assumes an H₂/He scale height, so high-metallicity or steam atmospheres read as muted for reasons other than aerosols. It also relies on catalogue masses. The Mission Candidate Sample lists HIP 67522 b at 186 Earth masses (−120/+338). That entry carries a mass flag, which the sample also carries on 13 % of its entries. The catalogue mass is an order of magnitude above the 13.8 ± 1.0 inferred from the planet's JWST transmission spectrum (96). The flag's lower cut has fired only there, at the wrong catalogue mass. It has fired on no real planet of the panel or the fourth batch at a correct mass. The sample also lists several panel planets with eclipse as the preferred method, so their Tier-1 transit counts are not what the mission would schedule. The audit of Section 2.10 came after the independent test, was prompted by its failure and changed scores on the panel. It is therefore post hoc, and the corrected panel is a development set for everything designed after it.

**Scope.** The results concern the design as re-implemented, on the stated simulator, under two named populations. The prevalences are limits under each population, not physical limits. The two populations give undecidable fractions of 32-54 % and 7-24 %, so the prevalence is chiefly a statement about the population's chemistry. The Mission Candidate Sample run used equilibrium chemistry and clear atmospheres on 200 planets. The correlated population of Section 2.10, which lies between the two, was not retrieved. The experiments cover a selected collection of departures and do not show how far the classifier can be relied on outside that collection. The second classifier of Appendix B, built for a different objective, showed a nearly opposite departure profile. This limits any generalisation from one classifier to another.

## 5. Conclusion

This study separated a classifier's errors into two kinds: those the spectrum can settle and those it cannot. It also introduced a design, the matched ensemble, that repairs much of the first kind. The design does this by changing the training population, the input and the thresholds. It was frozen and then scored once on two model grids, one of them from another code family. It scores above the routing method at both precisions, at false-present rates of 8-17 %. On the photochemical grid it also scores above this study's four-gas retrieval. At Ariel's coverage it raises simulated accuracy under a haze included in its training from 63.5 to 80.5 %. On real labels it leads the routing method at Tier-1 noise and, at JWST precision, on labels outside that method's panel. Those labels had been seen, however, so a prospective test on new public spectra is the step that would confirm this.

The limit set by the spectra is measured as well. Under the training prior, at the 0.95 level and on noise-free spectra, a third to a half of the molecular labels are not settled at Tier-1 precision. About four fifths of the re-implemented classifier's clean errors fall on these labels, which are unsettled rather than unreadable: a reference rule on the same noisy spectra removes 38-48 % of that error. Under equilibrium chemistry on 200 simulated Mission Candidate Sample targets the share is 7 to 24 %. The limit is therefore a property of the population as much as of the instrument. On real JWST spectra the classifier is right on almost every absent label and misses most present ones. A routing method reading these mismatches gained nothing on 12 new planets. The clear-model retrieval shares the haze failure and fails more confidently than the classifier. A flag's destination therefore decides whether it helps. For the mission's selection step the results are a budget, a warning and a procedure: a measured share of the classifier's error that a better design can remove, a demonstrated miss on real giant planets, and a measurement the mission can repeat with its own population before Tier-1 labels are used to choose targets.

## Appendix B. A second classifier

A gradient-boosted classifier (122) for the carbon-to-oxygen ratio (C/O > 1) (123, 124) was trained on 18,040 atmospheres with equilibrium chemistry from FastChem (111, 112) at the Tier-3 layout of the mission's radiometric model (62), and reached 96.1 % on its own test sets. At the same Tier-3 binning, one classifier was robust to omitted absorbers and fragile to haze, and the other the reverse. The stress test of one Ariel classifier did not predict the other's.

**Sampler convergence.** Two repeats on 50 planets test the nested-sampling settings (Section 2.4): another sampler seed (200 labels), and 400 live points with the call cap raised to 80,000 (45 planets finished within a three-hour limit per planet; 180 labels, a median of 14,191 calls). In both, p_true moved by a median of 0.005 (at most 0.075 and 0.064) and no label crossed 0.5. Ten labels (5 %) changed decidability at 0.95 under the seed, five each way, and seven under the live points, three to decidable and four from it; the undecidable fraction was 38.5 % in both seed runs, and 71 labels were undecided at 400 live points against 70 at 100.

**The clean-spectrum budget in detail.** On clean spectra the retrieval was the selection method, and no cheap rule allocated a partial budget much better than chance. At 60 % Tier-2 slots, ranking by the retrieval's present-posterior lost 0 of 359 CH₄-bearing targets, 2 of 364 H₂O targets and 39 either-molecule targets; a truth oracle, which ranks by the true labels, lost 0, 0 and 3, and the classifier 20, 51 and 95. This is expected of a correctly specified retrieval, the Bayes-optimal selector on noise-free spectra under its own forward model; it took 365 core-hours for this seven-parameter model matched to the generator, and takes more for richer physics. For H₂O the comparison does not rest on the noise-free spectrum: on the 200-planet subsample at 60 % slots, the retrieval of the classifier's noisy realisation lost 1 of 74 CH₄ and 2 of 80 H₂O targets, the noise-free retrieval 0 and 1, and the classifier 1 and 12. When only the fraction *f* of planets a rule ranks most suspect is re-scored by the retrieval (Table C2), only the k-NN distance and Mahalanobis rules beat a random budget, by 2-4 CH₄ targets at *f* = 20-30 %. The confidence rules were no better than random at any *f*: they sent the retrieval to the planets the classifier is unsure about, which carry the undecidable labels. The retrieval does read those labels better than the classifier (Section 3.2), but at 60 % slots the decided-present and undecidable planets largely fit the queue already (below), so a better ranking there moves few targets across the cut. For H₂O only predicted decidability beat random, by 4-5 targets at *f* = 20-30 %. The retrieval paid off on the planets the classifier was confidently wrong about, which no rule found.

**Recognising undecidable labels in detail.** Recognising these labels is worth a withheld verdict, not a retrieval. Predicted from bulk features, decidability reached an AUROC of only 0.61-0.66 for undecidable labels (0.47-0.63 for separating the error classes). The classifier's margin and learner-spread rules reached 0.71-0.77 for CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and 0.59-0.61 for H₂O (the distance rules 0.68), and a random forest trained out of fold on the spectrum and bulk features, with the retrieval's labels as targets, reached 0.69-0.74, tying them on CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ and beating them on H₂O. The ceiling of any such recogniser is the measured Δχ² ordering (Table C2), which at a 20 % budget lost 8 CH₄ targets against 12 for a random ordering and 7 for the retrieval's own posterior: knowing which labels are undecidable was worth about four targets in 359, because at 60 % slots few targets were lost. Recognising them paid in accuracy instead: declining the 30 % of planets the margin ranked least confident raised the CH₄ accuracy of the mean KNN, MLP and RFC probability on the rest from 86.4 to 94.6 %. Nor did the planets with undecidable labels always fit the slots: for CH₄ the 224 planets decided present and the 310 with undecidable labels fit the 573 Tier-2 slots, so ranking by the posterior lost no target, but for H₂O the 156 and 518 exceeded the slots by 101, and for either molecule the 809 by 236.

**A stratified margin rule.** Part of the small gain had a simple cause: the calls on a gas's rarer answer sat near a probability of 0.5, so one cut declined most of them (on local condensation at JWST precision, 7 % of the CO₂ calls kept were present against 61 % of those declined), and accuracy rose while balanced credit fell. Keeping the most confident 70 % of present calls and, separately, of absent calls within each gas added 0.044 to 0.078 on the three grids, more than the unstratified rule in all six cases. Treated the same way, the classifier's best rule added 0.003 to 0.033, and the difference, +0.034 to +0.055, had an interval above zero in five of six cases (the sixth, the photochemical grid at JWST precision, −0.007 to +0.055). This rule was chosen after the grids had been seen, so these figures are post hoc. On the real labels, which played no part in choosing it, it did not help: balanced credit went from 0.788 to 0.754 at JWST precision (−0.034; −0.078 to +0.025) and from 0.682 to 0.643 at Tier-1 noise (−0.038; −0.072 to +0.007), on 105 and 88 labels. Its gain is therefore supported on model grids only, and it needs a prospective test (`results/round3/round3_margin_stratified.txt`, `round3_margin_stratified_real.txt`).

## Appendix D. The flags in detail

**The remedies.** The first remedy is the design retrained on a population extended in aerosols, from one of three training sets built from POP-III: the haze set, in which a third of the training planets carry a column-filling Lee-Mie haze with density log-uniform over 10⁶-3 × 10⁸ m⁻³; the haze+deck set, which adds on a further sixth a grey deck at 5-500 Pa, up to two decades above the prior's 5 × 10² Pa limit; and the union set, which adds at fit time stellar spots at 10 or 20 % coverage on a sixth of the planets and doubled or tripled noise on another sixth, drawn independently. The haze-retrained, haze+deck-retrained and union-retrained designs were fitted exactly as the re-implemented classifier; the routing method uses the haze-retrained design. The second remedy is the AIRS-only variant (Section 2.3), used only where a flag fires.

**The shape flag.** Four candidate diagnostics were chosen before the runs: (a) the input disagreement between the three variants of the design; (b) the optical-excess score; (c) band-metric consistency, the disagreement between a learner's probability and the flag from the consortium's band metric (7), which compares the mean depth in a molecule's bands with that in a normalisation band in units of their dispersions; and (d) predicted decidability, a regressor on bulk features trained on the Δχ² of Section 2.4. For a molecule and a learner with present-probabilities p_all, p_no and p_AIRS from the three variants, the input disagreement is

D = max(|p_all − p_no|, |p_all − p_AIRS|) (eq3)

and the optical-excess score, with z the normalised spectrum, is

E = |(mean z over the three optical points − mean z over Ariel's NIRSpec channel) − μ| / s (eq4)

where μ and s are the mean and standard deviation of that difference on the clean training set. Each has a clean threshold, its 90th percentile on the clean test spectra. The shape flag fires on a cell (a molecule and a learner) when D or E exceeds its threshold; both are used because they fire on different real reductions (Appendix E). To locate the departure, D is also split by level of the nested inputs into D₁ = |p_all − p_no| and D₂ = |p_no − p_AIRS|, each at its own clean threshold (`nested_router.py`). Each candidate was scored by selective credit as in Section 2.3, by its area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC; 0.5 is chance) for separating the two error classes, and as a router (a rule that sends a flagged spectrum to another input or design), by the classifier's accuracy and recall when flagged planets are re-scored by the AIRS-only variant. The optical-excess score was designed from the haze mechanism of Section 3.3 and is evaluated on the same planets under the same haze; its only tests on data it was not built from are the real panel and the confined hazes.

**The amplitude flag.** Reading the mismatch the real giants showed (Section 3.4), the flag is the ratio

R = A₅ / (s² − ε²)^½ (eq5)

where A₅ is the five-scale-height modulation from the catalogue's mass, radius and equilibrium temperature with an H₂/He mean molecular weight, as in the payload noise model (Section 2.2), and the denominator is the noise-corrected dispersion of the measured bins. Its reference is the muting census (Section 2.6), which reads the same dispersion against a clear twin at the published composition, which a pipeline does not have. Two cuts are read from POP-I through the same bins: the upper threshold, the largest ratio of any clear POP-I planet (a planet above it is more muted per scale height than any of them), and the lower cut, the first percentile of POP-I with its own decks (a planet below it is more featured than 99 % of the population). A spectrum with no dispersion above its errors has no ratio and takes no flag. With all 104 bins measured, as in simulation, the thresholds are 5.25 and 0.47. A first form of the flag used full-bin thresholds, and its statistic was chosen after its values on the panel had been seen. The per-reduction rule and its sensitivities were committed before they were computed (Appendix C, rows F1-F3) but after all three batches were known, so the panel is not an independent test of the flag.

## Appendix E. The routing method in detail

This appendix gives the detail summarised in Section 3.5: the present labels the re-implemented classifier missed on the panel, the remedies, the two flags, every design's scores on the panel and in simulation, and the selection.

**The grid scores through the full conversion.** The model grids are scored through a simplified operator: each reduction's coverage mask, with independent Gaussian noise added per bin at the reduction's per-bin error (Section 2.10). To test whether the designs' scores hold under the real conversion of Section 2.6, 50 source models per reduction and grid, drawn with a fixed seed, were passed through it: the model spectrum, on its own wavelength grid, was averaged over each native JWST point's interval for every mode of the reduction; Gaussian noise was drawn per point at that point's published error; the modes were aligned by the fitted offsets and combined by overlap-weighted inverse-variance binning onto the 104 bins; and bins with coverage below one half were filled flat, as for the real spectra. For Tier-1 noise the Tier-1 σ was then added per bin. Twenty draws were made per model at each precision, and the same models were scored through the shortcut operator on the same draws. The matched ensemble was refitted for each reduction with the seeds of Section 2.10, which reproduces its stored votes, and the routing method and the re-implemented classifier were scored as in Table 13. The full operator reproduces the stored mask in every draw and the stored per-bin errors. Under it, neighbouring measured bins carry correlated noise, with a median lag-1 correlation of 0.12 across the 38 reductions and values above 0.3 on 11 of them (0.56 on WASP-39 b); the two sources are JWST points that straddle bin edges and the fitted offsets, which act as common-mode noise and raise the noise above the propagated error on the multi-mode reductions. **What the gain consists of.** Of the 8.5 labels, 3.5 lay on labels failing the mask control and 5.5 on the 47 that pass (44.0 against 38.5; interval 1.1 to 9.7), and the labels with no twin lost 0.5. On the fifteen reductions with measured data both below 1.1 µm and above 1.95 µm, the gain was 7.5 of 38 (2.9 to 11.8). About two fifths of the gain therefore lay on labels the mask itself hides, which Ariel's full coverage would not; the rest survived the control. Nor did the gain rest on doubtful labels: with the one remaining label that departs from the rule removed and the two missed ones added (65 labels), the routing method scored 52.5 against 45.0, a gain of 7.5 (0.6 to 13.9), and with every judgement call also removed (59 labels) 46.5 against 39.0, the same 7.5 (1.0 to 13.9). All of these scores are at JWST precision; at Tier-1 noise the gain was 2.3 (Table E4), and a majority of learners found methane on 1.2 of the eight reductions on average over the draws, against none. The gain shrank by almost three quarters because every design missed most present labels at that noise: of the 26 present labels the designs found 3.1 to 6.6. Under the two corrected label sets the Tier-1 gains were 2.2 (0.7 to 3.7) and 1.8 (0.4 to 3.3).

**Against the existing rules.** Used as routers to the AIRS-only variant, the five existing rules scored 45.0-47.0 of 64 at JWST precision and 39.7-40.8 at Tier-1 noise, below the shape flag and the routing method (Table E4). Their simulated accuracy was higher on clean spectra because they rarely fired there, and lower under most departures (Table E5). Behind the amplitude flag, in the shape flag's place, the distance rules scored 49.0-50.0 and the confidence rules 48.0. The amplitude flag with the AIRS-only variant on every other planet matched the routing method on the panel, but on clean spectra it cost the AIRS-only variant's 7.4 points; the shape flag's part in the method is to keep the other inputs where nothing is flagged.

**The price, in simulation and in the selection.** The price was paid in simulation, on the population the classifier was built for (Table E5). Counting a withheld label as half, the routing method cost 4.8 points of clean accuracy, nearly all from the shape flag, which fired on 17 % of clean cells, where the less accurate AIRS-only variant differed; the optical-excess score alone cost 1.5. The method gained 8.7, 5.8 and 2.8 points under the column-filling haze, the confined haze and spots and 0.2 under the high deck, and lost 2.1 under twofold noise. In the selection (Table E6; one noise draw) it lost fewer CH₄ targets than the re-implemented classifier only under haze and spots, and more H₂O targets on clean spectra and no fewer under any haze. The haze-retrained design used everywhere lost fewer targets than the routing method in all but three of the fourteen CH₄ and H₂O entries of Table E6, and the haze+deck-retrained design led it in accuracy in every column. The slot count changed the size of these differences more than their sign: under haze the order was the same at 40 and 80 % slots, the retrained designs first and the re-implemented classifier last (94 and 19 CH₄ targets lost, against 74 and 7 for the routing method), and on clean spectra the re-implemented classifier led at 40 % slots (56 against 65). At 20 % slots, 191 for 359 targets, every design lost about half (178-194, where 168 must be lost). What the simulation lacked was what the real spectra showed: the retrained designs found methane on two of the eight reductions, and the AIRS-only variant on seven.

**The optical-excess score alone, over ten noise draws.** Routed to the AIRS-only variant, the score raised CH₄ recall under the column-filling haze from 11.6 to 53.7 % and stayed within 0.5 points of the classifier on clean spectra. In the selection the classifier lost 16 ± 3 CH₄ targets on clean spectra and 36 ± 5 under haze, and the routed classifier 27 ± 5 under haze, about half of the targets haze adds. For H₂O no flag helped, because the water loss under haze was not routed through the optical points to the same degree.

**Detecting the weaker haze.** At 2 × 10⁶ m⁻³, with every planet hazed, the classifier's CH₄ recall at the 0.5 threshold fell from 75 to 27 %, where the haze costs 12 points of accuracy. With a fifth of the planets hazed, the optical-excess score's top 20 % held 60 % of the hazed and 10 % of the clean planets (PCA 56 and 11 %, the Mahalanobis rule 46 and 14 %, the k-NN distance rule 31 and 17 %), and the confidence rules held 6-9 % of the hazed planets, below the clean rate. So the haze the score detects fully moves CH₄ targets out of the selection, and the one it detects in part costs accuracy and H₂O targets but few CH₄ targets.

**Against the simpler fixes.** The routing method scored higher on the panel than the two simpler fixes, the AIRS-only variant and the haze+deck-retrained design used everywhere, by 2.0 (−1.0 to 5.6) and 1.5 (−2.9 to 5.3) at JWST precision and by 0.6 (−0.5 to 1.7) and 0.3 (−0.7 to 1.5) at Tier-1 noise. At JWST precision the lead over the AIRS-only variant survived removing any one planet, but without TOI-270 d the method and the haze+deck-retrained design tied. The lead was small and partly reflected choices made after those planets were seen. The method and the AIRS-only variant differed on 6 labels, 4 in the method's favour and 2 against; the difference of 2.0 equalled the gains on the water of WASP-166 b and HAT-P-12 b, which came from the order of the flags, and the other four were half labels that cancelled. In simulation the method was 2.6 points above the AIRS-only variant on clean spectra and 1.5 under twofold noise, 0.5-1.1 below it under each haze, spots and the high deck, and below the haze+deck-retrained design in every column. Over dropping the optical points everywhere, it gains the use of those points wherever they are not flagged, and it names a failure for each planet, which the simpler fixes do not.

**A muted population.** Because the input is normalised, scaling every feature by 1/M with the noise unscaled is the same, for a fixed design, as noise at M times σ. A population muted by three cost the re-implemented classifier more targets than the column-filling haze, and no design in Table E6 repaired it: all lost 54-65 CH₄ and 93-114 H₂O targets, and accuracy stayed at 65-68 % (Table E5). At fixed precision much of the information had gone below the noise, although the matched ensemble of Section 3.7 kept more of it. Since only the giants measured here are muted so far, this bounds the cost: the departure seen on real planets would cost more targets than the simulated haze, and retraining on the published recipe or routing would not recover them.

**Simulated robustness against real reliability.** Across the seven designs that use no existing rule, the ranking by simulated accuracy on clean spectra ran opposite to the ranking on the real panel at JWST precision (Spearman −0.50). The ranking under haze ran with it (+0.64), under twofold noise against it (−0.29), and the other simulated cases said little (at most 0.22); with seven designs these correlations are descriptive. On the 12 new planets the sign reversed (+0.34), and the matched ensemble led the clean benchmark, so these results do not show that the clean benchmark picks the wrong design.

**What the later batches show.** Most of the gain over the re-implemented classifier lay in the first batch, on which the flags were built (30.0 against 23.5 of 38); the second batch gave 16.0 against 14.5 of 19, and the third 6.0 against 5.5 of 7. Only the shape flag, the retrained designs and the census routing were fixed before a later batch (Table C3). The routing method's gain of half a label on the third batch (HAT-P-12 b's water) came from choices made after those planets were seen.

**The per-reduction design, and a test on a further 17 labels.** The per-reduction design with the published population gained 0.021 and 0.033 over the routing method on the rainout grid at the two precisions, 0.034 and 0.016 on local condensation and 0.038 and 0.050 on the photochemical grid, a real but small gain that led to changing the population. A survey of further public spectra (`independent_data_survey.md`) found 17 labels on 12 planets outside the panel and the fourth batch, 11 of them water labels from HST WFC3 G141 at eight usable bins, all committed before any verdict on them (`results/independent_test/`). There the matched ensemble missed only V1298 Tau c's water in the HST reduction at JWST precision, but the haze+deck-retrained design scored the same (Table 15), so these labels did not separate the better designs.
