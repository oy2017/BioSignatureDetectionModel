## Part 1. Reply to the editor's message of 15 October

Dear Dr Apte,

Thank you for reading the manuscript and for the discussion with the reviewer. I accepted the suggestions in full and have done the work. The revised manuscript is resubmitted as an original article, and the four requests of your message are each answered with a measurement:

1. **External validation on WASP-39 b.** The JWST benchmark spectrum of WASP-39 b (the four Early Release Science modes re-reduced uniformly by Carter & May et al. 2024, extended with the MIRI spectrum of Powell et al. 2024) was converted to the classifier's 104-bin Tier-3 input by a documented procedure, and the procedure was validated on a spectrum whose answer is known: the benchmark's own best-fit model, pushed through the conversion, differs from the same model binned directly by 22 ppm rms and changes no verdict. A 24-configuration sensitivity matrix (each mode's individual reductions, two NIRISS pipelines, offsets on and off, masked lines, with and without MIRI) measures how much each verdict depends on the reduction. At JWST precision the classifier reproduces the published composition: three of four learners call H₂O present, all four call CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at the 10⁻⁴ threshold, and CO₂ appears at 10⁻⁵ where its published abundance puts it. The one wrong learner is wrong for a reason the paper locates in the real spectrum, not in the conversion. Because one planet is a weak test, nine further planets with public JWST spectra were converted the same way (Section 2.6; Section 3.5).

2. **Identifiability.** A nested-sampling retrieval of every one of the classifier's 955 test planets, under the consortium's own test prior (POP-I) at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, gives for every label the posterior probability of the true class. Under that named population, a third of the CH₄ and NH₃ labels, 44 % of the CO₂ labels and 54 % of the H₂O labels cannot be decided at Tier-1 precision (posterior of the true class below 0.95), only 17 % of planets have all four labels decidable, and the retrieval's own accuracy, 92 to 94 %, is the ceiling the spectra allow, 9 to 16 points above the classifier (Section 2.4; Section 3.3; Table 5).

3. **A remedy with measurable value.** The value is measured as Tier-2 targets lost at a fixed 60 % selection, against the existing methods: the classifier alone, the five decline rules of the original note, and a retrieval of every planet. The result has three parts. On clean spectra, the retrieval of every planet is the selection oracle at 365 core-hours, and no cheap rule allocates a smaller retrieval budget better than chance. Under haze, the existing retrieval fails more confidently than the classifier. And on a population in which a fifth of the planets are hazed, a one-number score on the three optical inputs, routed to a retrieval with the haze density free, allocates a 20 % retrieval budget as well as an oracle that knows which planets are hazed, recovering 80 % of the hazed methane targets against 3 % for the classifier alone, 17 % for a random budget and 3 % for the confidence rules; without any retrieval, the same score routed to the input variant without the optical points recovers a third of the targets haze removes, at no retraining, and is never worse than the classifier (Sections 2.7 and 3.6; Tables 8 and 9).

4. **The two classes of error.** Every classifier error, on clean spectra and under each departure, was assigned to a class by the retrieval (recoverable: the spectrum decides the label; irrecoverable: it does not). Three quarters of the classifier's errors on clean spectra are irrecoverable. Haze and unocculted spots leave the labels decidable, and 57 to 87 % of the errors they introduce are recoverable; a deep cloud deck removes information on absent labels; threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction and its added errors are mostly irrecoverable. The same two classes appear on the real spectra: the classifier missed methane on five of six JWST reductions of the three methane-bearing sub-Neptunes while the same design without its optical inputs found it on five of six (recoverable), and the five real warm giants are muted beyond anything the simulator population can produce at their bulk parameters (a prior limit no input-space rule can see). A retrieval that models the haze with one free parameter recovers 222 of the 224 methane labels the clean spectra decide; the same retrieval without that parameter is confidently wrong on 99 % of them (Sections 2.5, 3.4 and 3.5; Table 6; Figure 4).

The reviewer's further points (the scope of the "trust" claim, the mission-level decision problem, attribution to the re-implementation, and the compression of the second classifier and the decline-rule results) are each answered in Part 2. Every expectation was committed to the public repository before the corresponding run, and the outcomes, including the ones not met, are in Table 10. All code, result tables and the conversion of the JWST spectra are public.

I would be grateful if the manuscript could be moved to the revision stage and considered as an original article.

With thanks,
Owen Yang

## Part 2. Point-by-point response

Reviewer and editor comments are quoted in italics; the response follows each, with the section of the revised manuscript where the change is.

### The editor's four conditions

**E1.** *"The author could compare the classifier's predictions against JWST-derived atmospheric properties, provided the observed spectra are made compatible with the classifier's input requirements with adequate justification of the compatibility procedure."*

Done, and the compatibility procedure is the part of Section 2.6 with the most care. The classifier was trained on transit depths in 104 Tier-3 bins, so no change of quantity is needed; each bin takes the inverse-variance-weighted mean of the JWST points inside it, modes are aligned through their overlaps (for WASP-39 b this recovers the PRISM offset of −177 ppm that Carter & May report from their joint fit), bins with no JWST data are filled flat and flagged, and the number of filled bins is reported with every verdict (one of 104 for WASP-39 b). Three justifications are given rather than one. (a) A round trip: the benchmark's own best-fit model binned directly onto the 104 bins, against the same model sampled at the JWST grids with the per-point errors and pushed through the conversion, differs by 22 ppm rms and 104 ppm at worst, and no verdict changes. (b) A sensitivity matrix of 24 configurations covering the reduction choices Roy-Perez et al. (2026) showed can move retrieved abundances by an order of magnitude. (c) Three noise regimes, so the verdict is reported both at JWST precision and at the Ariel Tier-1 precision the classifier was designed for, where the spectrum is scattered 500 to 1,000 times with the payload model's per-bin noise and the detection frequency per molecule is the result.

The outcome (Section 3.5): at JWST precision three of four learners call H₂O present and all four call CH₄, CO₂ and NH₃ absent at 10⁻⁴, matching the published composition; NH₃ is absent in all 24 configurations and with each of three MIRI reductions; the consortium's own band metric agrees with the majority. The multilayer perceptron calls H₂O absent with a score of 0.00 in 23 of 24 configurations and on the directly binned model, so this miss is not the conversion's. It is the shape of a real hot Saturn: WASP-39 b's spectrum is 3.7 times more muted than its clear twin, beyond the most muted atmosphere the population can draw at its bulk parameters (2.0), while sitting inside the training distribution by every input-space rule. Under Tier-1 noise, H₂O is detected in 58 % of draws with all inputs and 86 % without the three optical points, so the optical points decide the Tier-1 outcome on a real planet.

Because one planet cannot support a population claim, the same conversion was applied to nine further planets with public JWST transmission spectra (TOI-270 d, K2-18 b in three reductions, TOI-1231 b, TOI-421 b, HAT-P-18 b, WASP-166 b, and four with AIRS-only coverage). Over the 35 labels the literature decides, the all-input classifier was right on 63 %, below the share of absent labels, and the AIRS-only variant on 78 %. The classifier missed methane on five of six reductions of the three methane-bearing sub-Neptunes while the AIRS-only variant found it on five of six (Table 7).

**E2.** *"Establish identifiability. Determine whether physically distinct atmospheric compositions can produce indistinguishable Ariel spectra, and quantify how frequently such ambiguity occurs under explicitly defined atmospheric populations."*

Done with two instruments, on the whole test population (Section 2.4). The first is a Δχ² test: for each planet and molecule, the best-fitting atmosphere of the opposite label, with the molecule held at the 10⁻⁴ boundary and the population's nuisance parameters free (temperature, cloud top, the other abundances, an offset), against the true spectrum at the planet's Tier-1 noise. The second is a nested-sampling retrieval of every one of the 955 test planets with the same free quantities, giving for each label the posterior probability of the true class, which is the P-statistic of Bocchieri et al. (2023) evaluated for the true class. The two agree in rank (Spearman 0.80 to 0.89), and the retrieval calibrates the test's scale, which turned out to be wrong by an order of magnitude (Section 3.3); the uncalibrated prevalence first computed was withdrawn, and the withdrawal is recorded in Section 3.7.

The population is explicit: POP-I, the consortium's own test prior, at each planet's Tier-1 transit count, with the payload noise model. Under it (Table 5), 32 % of CH₄ labels, 54 % of H₂O, 44 % of CO₂ and 32 % of NH₃ are undecidable at the 0.95 level (17 to 28 % at 0.84); 17 % of planets have all four labels decidable and 3 % none. Decidability is set first by the label's distance from the 10⁻⁴ boundary (labels within half a decade of it are undecidable in about 90 % of cases) and second by the planet's noise. The retrieval's own accuracy, 92 to 94 %, is the ceiling the spectra allow under this prior, and the classifier sits 9 to 16 points below it. The manuscript states (Sections 2.4 and 3.8) that the true joint distribution of Ariel's targets is unknown and that every prevalence is a prevalence under this named prior; Section 3.5 then measures how far the real planets sit from it.

**E3.** *"Establish a remedy with measurable value. Develop and evaluate a diagnostic that recognizes when the classifier is likely to be unreliable, and demonstrate that its use improves molecular classification or target selection compared with existing methods."*

Done, with the value measured in the mission's own currency and against the strongest existing method, and the answer is in three parts, because the first part is a negative result that the paper reports rather than hides.

First, on clean simulated spectra the existing method, a retrieval of every planet, is the Tier-2 selection oracle: ranking by its present-posterior loses 0 of 359 CH₄-bearing targets at a 60 % selection, against 20 for the classifier, at 365 core-hours for the population. No diagnostic on the classifier can improve on that, and no cheap rule routes a smaller retrieval budget better than a random one (Table 8); the confidence rules are worse than random, because they send the budget to the undecidable labels where the retrieval cannot help either. The "remedy" question therefore has content only where the retrieval shares the classifier's failure, or at a budget below a full retrieval.

Second, the existing retrieval does share the failure. Run with the training forward model on the hazed spectra of all 954 planets, it calls every present label absent (recall below 1 %) and is confidently wrong on 99 % of present labels, worse than the classifier (Section 3.4).

Third, the diagnostic: a one-number optical-excess score, the three photometric points against the NIRSpec continuum as a z-score on the clean training set, chosen before the runs because Section 3.2 had located the haze failure in those inputs. It flags 100 % of hazed and 66 % of spotted planets at a 10 % clean false-alarm rate, and fewer than 3 % of clouded and noisier ones. Used to allocate a retrieval budget on a mixed population with a fifth of the planets hazed (Section 2.7; Table 9), routing the 20 % of planets it ranks most suspect to a retrieval with the haze density as a free parameter loses 13 of 359 CH₄ targets against 29 for the classifier alone, 20 for a random budget, 20 to 22 for the margin and ensemble rules, 69 for the existing retrieval of every planet, and 12 for an oracle that knows which planets are hazed; its recall on the hazed targets is 80 % against 3 % for the classifier and the confidence rules and 81 % for the oracle. The same budget routed to the existing clear-model retrieval loses 71 targets, worse than no retrieval at all. Without any retrieval, routing the flagged planets to the design variant without the optical inputs raises CH₄ recall under haze from 12 to 54 % and reduces the targets lost from 38 to 26, is never worse than the classifier on clean or noisier spectra, and needs no retraining. The expectations for this test were committed before the population data; four of five were met in full and the fifth for one of the three distance rules (Table 10).

What the diagnostic does not do is also stated: it does not beat a departure-aware retrieval of every planet, which loses nothing at 635 core-hours; its value is at a budget below that. And it does not help the irrecoverable class, for which Section 3.6 recommends that undecided planets reach Tier 2 as undecided rather than dismissed.

**E4.** *"The most interesting possible outcome is that you discover two distinct classes of error: recoverable computational errors ... [and] irrecoverable information limitations. Distinguishing these two categories, quantifying their prevalence, and developing a method to recognize them would be a much stronger scientific contribution."*

This became the paper's spine, its title, and its abstract. The definition (Section 2.5): an error is recoverable if the posterior probability of the true class on the same spectrum exceeds 0.95 (a retrieval of that spectrum gets the label right), undecidable if it lies between 0.05 and 0.95, and misled if below 0.05; under a departure, the departure-aware Δχ² at the calibrated threshold is used, and errors are split into those the learner also makes on the clean spectrum and those the departure introduces.

Prevalence (Section 3.4; Table 6; Figure 4): three quarters of the classifier's clean errors are irrecoverable. Haze and spots remove no information the labels need, and 57 to 87 % of the errors they introduce are recoverable; a deep deck removes information on absent labels (51 to 82 % recoverable); threefold noise doubles the undecidable fraction and leaves 10 to 56 % recoverable. The clearest demonstration is the pair of retrievals on the same 954 hazed spectra: with the training model, the retrieval is confidently wrong on 99 % of present labels; with the haze density added as one free parameter it recovers the density on every planet and 222 of the 224 CH₄ labels the clean spectra decide, within 5 points of its clean accuracy on every molecule.

Recognition: at deployment the two classes are told apart by the retrieval on a flagged planet (Section 3.6), and the paper measures what each cheaper candidate can and cannot do. The shift detectors (the optical score and the distance rules) find the recoverable class under haze; nothing cheap finds the irrecoverable class (predicting decidability from bulk features reached AUROC 0.47 to 0.65), which is itself a result the mission needs.

On real data (Section 3.5): the methane misses on the sub-Neptunes are recoverable (the information is in the spectrum, since the AIRS-only learner finds it, and retraining with hazes repairs the planets whose spectra are muted beyond the simulator's prior and not the two that are not), while the five warm giants' muting, 3 to 5 times beyond their clear twins and beyond what the population can produce at their bulk parameters in 99 % of its planets, is a limit of the training prior that no normalised-input rule can see.

### The reviewer's points

**R-a. Novelty.** *"The general proposition that simulator-trained classifiers cannot be assumed reliable outside their training distributions is neither new nor particularly surprising ... the manuscript would be more appropriately characterized as a reproducibility and robustness benchmark ... To justify reconsideration ... establish a consequential, non-obvious result about the propagation of simulator misspecification into Ariel's scientific decision-making or population-level inference."*

Agreed, and the revised paper no longer rests on that proposition. Its results are population-level and decision-level: the prevalence of undecidable labels under the consortium's own prior (a third to a half), the finding that three quarters of the classifier's errors are of the class no method can fix, the separation of the departures into the two classes, the translation of each into Tier-2 targets lost at a fixed budget, the fact that the existing retrieval fails more confidently than the classifier under the one departure the real sub-Neptunes show, and the external test on ten JWST spectra. Two of the results were not anticipated by the pre-registered expectations and are reported as surprises in Section 3.7: the Δχ² identifiability test, used as a cheap proxy, is wrong in scale by an order of magnitude against the posterior; and the real warm giants lie outside the simulator population in feature amplitude while inside it in every bulk parameter and by every distance rule. The robustness benchmark of the original note is retained as Section 3.2, shortened, because it located the mechanism the rest of the paper tests.

**R-b. The haze mechanism.** *"Even this finding requires a stronger interpretation ... The author has demonstrated the magnitude of the vulnerability for a particular implementation, but has not established a new physical mechanism, a general mathematical principle ... the proposed remedies ... are familiar approaches."*

The mechanism is now tested in three ways it was not before. It is classed: the retrieval shows that haze leaves the labels decidable, so the haze errors are a property of the classifier, not of the spectra, and the errors noise introduces are the opposite (Section 3.4). It is shown on real planets: the optical points decide WASP-39 b's Tier-1 outcome (58 against 86 % detection of H₂O), and the three methane-bearing sub-Neptunes are missed with the optical inputs and found without them (Section 3.5). And it is shown to propagate to the existing method: the retrieval with the training forward model fails on the same spectra more confidently than the classifier, so the vulnerability is of the forward model's assumptions and not of the learner's architecture. The remedies are familiar, as the reviewer says, and the paper does not claim them as new; what is new is their measured value in targets, including the result that the familiar remedy of a retrieval fallback is worse than nothing unless the retrieval models the departure.

**R-c. Simulated robustness is not predictive validity.** *"A classifier that performs poorly under a particular simulated haze prescription is not necessarily equally unreliable under the actual distribution of atmospheric hazes encountered by Ariel ... The study quantifies sensitivity to selected modeling choices but does not independently establish the relationship between simulated robustness and real-world classification reliability."*

This is the point the external test answers, and the answer is specific rather than general. On the one real planet inside the training domain (WASP-39 b) the classifier is mostly right, and its one wrong learner is wrong for a reason the paper locates in the real spectrum's muting. On the real planets that carry the molecule the classifier was built to find (the methane sub-Neptunes) it is wrong on five of six reductions, for the mechanism the simulated haze had predicted. And the five real warm giants with published abundances are muted beyond the simulator population's reach at their bulk parameters, which says directly that the real distribution differs from the training prior in a measured way (Section 3.5; Figure 5). The paper does not claim that the simulated departures represent Ariel's target distribution; it says the opposite (Section 3.8) and measures the distance between the two where JWST allows it.

**R-d. "Trust" is a broader claim.** *"Trustworthiness is a substantially broader claim than robustness to a finite set of perturbations ... Without such information, the observed accuracy losses cannot be translated directly into expected mission-wide classification errors."*

Accepted. The word "trust" is gone from the title and the text. Every prevalence is reported under a named population (the consortium's POP-I prior at Tier-1 transit counts), the mixed-population results are given at stated hazed fractions (10, 20 and 50 %) rather than one, and Section 3.8 states that the true joint distribution of Ariel's targets is unknown and that the muting census is the measurement of how far ten real planets sit from the prior, not a replacement for it.

**R-e. The second classifier.** *"The comparison establishes only that two different classifiers respond differently to different perturbations."*

Accepted. The carbon-rich classifier is reduced to Appendix B, one paragraph, and is used for the one conclusion the reviewer allows: one classifier's error classes do not stand in for another's, so each needs its own measurement.

**R-f. Confidence, distance and conformal results are well established.** *"Their conceptual interpretation is already well established ... the manuscript does not develop a new uncertainty measure, establish a new failure theorem, or identify a broadly transferable relationship."*

Accepted. The decline-rule results are compressed to one paragraph of Section 3.2, presented as the expected outcome of Jaeger et al. (2023) in a physical setting, and they enter the rest of the paper only as the baselines the diagnostic is scored against. The one addition that is not a restatement is the budget result: the confidence rules are worse than a random budget on clean spectra and find none of the hazed planets on a mixed population, with the reason given (they send the budget to the undecidable labels, which is where the retrieval cannot help either).

**R-g. Aggregate accuracy is the wrong measure.** *"The more scientifically relevant quantity may therefore be the number and characteristics of valuable targets lost under a constrained selection strategy."*

Adopted as the primary metric. The selection simulation (Section 2.7) gives the 939 POP-I planets with valid spectra 60 % Tier-2 slots and scores every strategy by the planets carrying the molecule that are not selected; accuracy is reported beside it. Tables 8 and 9 and the abstract are in targets lost. The asymmetric-cost question the reviewer raises is handled in the one way the data allow: at a fixed slot count, slots wasted equal targets lost plus a constant, so the ranking of strategies does not depend on the weight (Table 10, last row), and the paper states that follow-up opportunities, priorities and observing costs are not modelled.

**R-h. Attribution to the re-implementation.** *"The demonstrated weaknesses should be attributed primarily to the reconstructed implementation and tested design assumptions, rather than treated as experimentally verified properties of the unavailable original classifiers."*

Accepted throughout, not only in the limitations: Section 2.1 defines "the published design as re-implemented" and the text uses it wherever a weakness is claimed; Section 3.8 repeats it.

### Changes not prompted by a specific comment

- The muting census (Section 2.6, Section 3.5, Figure 5): for each real planet, and for every planet of POP-I, the dispersion of the spectrum against its clear twin, which is the measurement behind the statement that the real giants lie outside the prior.
- A retraining test with hazed and deep-deck training populations, used not as a remedy but as the test of whether a real error is recoverable; it repairs the real methane planets whose spectra are muted beyond the prior and not the two that are not.
- Pre-registration extended to every new experiment, with the outcomes, including the expectations not met, in Table 10.
- Reproducibility: every result table is committed, every expectation is in the repository's history before its run, and the JWST conversion, the identifiability test, the retrievals, the diagnostics and the selection simulation are scripts in the public repository with the command that regenerates each table.
