# 3. Background

The pursuit of identifying habitable exoplanets has evolved rapidly over the past decade, heavily reliant on the technique of transmission spectroscopy. Historically, these observational efforts have focused on gaseous giants (Hot Jupiters) due to their significant transit depths and correspondingly high signal-to-noise ratios (SNR). Foundational work by de Kok et al. (2013) demonstrated the successful detection of carbon monoxide, water vapor, and methane in the high-resolution day-side spectrum of the exoplanet HD 189733b. Their methodology involved cross-correlating high-resolution near-infrared spectra against theoretical templates to disentangle the planet's signal from telluric and stellar contaminants. While highly successful for detecting prominent absorption bands in large planets, this traditional retrieval approach becomes computationally prohibitive and statistically challenging when applied to smaller, rocky, Earth-like planets where the transit depths are orders of magnitude smaller.

Consequently, recent research has shifted toward using machine learning algorithms to assist in the classification of low-SNR transmission spectra. Duque-Castaño et al. (2024) established a crucial framework for this approach, developing a machine-assisted methodology to classify noisy transmission spectra of TRAPPIST-1 e twins. Using Random Forests trained on synthetic data, they demonstrated the feasibility of classifying planets as "interesting" for follow-up observations based on the potential presence of methane and ozone, even with SNRs below 6. Their work emphasized the necessity of employing specialized classification metrics (such as Precision, Recall, and True Negative Rate) to balance the trade-off between wasting valuable telescope time on false positives and missing true biosignatures due to false negatives.

Furthermore, the advent of next-generation space telescopes, such as the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the upcoming Ariel mission, necessitates new computational paradigms. Yip et al. (2021) highlighted the limitations of standard Bayesian retrieval frameworks when processing the high-dimensional, high-resolution data expected from these missions. They pioneered the application of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs), including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), to infer atmospheric parameters directly from transmission spectra. By utilizing deep learning models as fast, data-driven surrogates, they demonstrated that neural networks could successfully constrain planetary parameters and molecular abundances while significantly reducing retrieval time. 

Building upon these collective foundations, our research integrates the high-resolution focus of de Kok et al. (2013), the supervised ensemble classification strategy of Duque-Castaño et al. (2024), and the deep learning and feature extraction paradigms explored by Yip et al. (2021). By combining rigorous Principal Component Analysis with both ensemble trees (XGBoost/Random Forest) and deep neural architectures (MLP/CNN), we aim to provide a robust, calibrated tool to optimize target selection for the Ariel mission.
