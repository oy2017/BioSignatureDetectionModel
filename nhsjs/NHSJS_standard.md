# A calibrated, robustness-characterized machine-learning benchmark for threshold-based biosignature labeling of synthetic exoplanet transmission spectra

**Authors and affiliations:** *(omitted — blind-review version)*

## Abstract

Characterizing exoplanetary atmospheres efficiently is essential for future survey missions such as Ariel, where only a subset of planets can receive the most detailed follow-up observations. Existing classification models have shown promise for this task but have predominantly been trained on low-resolution spectra, and report in-distribution accuracy without establishing the conditions under which such a tool stops working. Synthetic spectra were simulated at a resolving power of R = 200 (550 spectral bins) using MultiREx / TauREx 3. After invalid spectra were cleaned, four supervised classifiers—XGBoost, Random Forest, Multi-Layer Perceptron, and 1D Convolutional Neural Network—were trained on PCA-transformed spectral features. The gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble (XGBoost) performed best, at 88.17% mean accuracy: ahead of the Random Forest (86.51%) by a narrow but statistically significant margin, and of the Multi-Layer Perceptron and 1D-CNN by nine and thirteen points. XGBoost was also the best calibrated of the four, with an expected calibration error of 0.032 against 0.176 for the Random Forest and a near-diagonal reliability curve. Along the axes whose strength can be swept, accuracy degrades smoothly and monotonically rather than collapsing at a threshold, and each large loss reduces to an identified mechanism (amplitude suppression, chromatic distortion, a decision-threshold bias, or loss of the label-defining signal) or, on the extrapolation axis, to leaving the sampled parameter envelope. It is thus offered as a characterized triage benchmark—reliable within its training domain, with its limits measured rather than assumed—and not as a biosignature detector for real observations.

**Keywords:** exoplanet atmospheres, transmission spectroscopy, biosignatures, machine learning, Ariel, probability calibration, domain shift

## Introduction

With more than 6,000 confirmed exoplanets discovered to date (1, 2), a NASA flagship priority organized explicitly around planetary habitability and the search for life (3), and an ESA survey mission dedicated to a chemical census of exoplanet atmospheres (4, 5), the characterization of exoplanetary atmospheres has become a central problem in modern observational astrobiology. Identifying exoplanets capable of sustaining life requires the robust detection of atmospheric biosignatures—gases whose presence strongly implies a biological origin, but only when interpreted within sufficient geological, atmospheric, and stellar context, since many candidate biosignature gases can be produced or mimicked abiotically (6, 7).

The Ariel mission, launching in 2029 (5, 8), will survey approximately 1,000 transiting exoplanets using visible-to-infrared photometry and medium- and low-resolution infrared spectroscopy spanning 0.5–7.8 μm during a nominal four-year science mission (4). To optimize valuable telescope time, the mission employs a multi-tiered observation strategy: Tier 1 acts as a broad reconnaissance survey, Tier 2 conducts a deeper baseline chemical survey across hundreds of atmospheres, and Tier 3 is reserved for the most detailed observations, at the native resolution of Ariel's instruments, of a select sample of 50 to 100 planets (4, 8, 9). Between one in twenty and one in ten surveyed targets therefore reaches Tier 3, and fewer still can be granted Tier 4 phase curves or a full Bayesian retrieval. The ranking that decides which planets advance is thus a mission-level bottleneck: a rigorous triage process is needed to order the Tier 3 benchmark planets and determine which warrant dedicated Tier 4 phase-curve observations (8) or follow-up with ground-based Extremely Large Telescopes (ELTs).

In this work, a machine learning framework is designed that recovers a predefined abundance-threshold biosignature labeling from high-resolution synthetic transmission spectra representative of an Ariel-like dataset. Because the labels are computed directly from the abundances supplied to the forward model, the classification task is the recovery of a predefined labeling convention from spectra, not the inference of abundances from observations; the latter is what Ariel will ultimately require, via retrieval, with its attendant uncertainty, parameter degeneracy, and abiotic alternatives. The grid over which that labeling is learned spans 500–2500 K atmospheres and radii to 26 R⊕, and so contains no habitable-zone analogues; methane and ozone function here as a label-defining molecular pair over a broad Ariel-like population, not as a habitability indicator.

The most directly related work is Duque-Castaño et al. (19), who developed a machine-learning framework for classifying low-SNR synthetic transmission spectra according to which of methane, ozone, and water they contain, with methane and ozone treated as the biosignature pair and water as interference. Three elements are taken from that work and held fixed here: MultiREx-based spectral generation, the CH₄/O₃ biosignature pair, and the framing of classification as candidate screening rather than as a replacement for retrieval. Three are changed: the labeling is the stricter conjunction of both gases above explicit abundance thresholds rather than the presence of either; the parameter grid spans a broad Ariel-like range of planetary and stellar properties rather than a single TRAPPIST-1e-like configuration, though the delivered combinations are correlated, not independently sampled; and four architectures are compared on a single shared feature space. What is added is the part that decides whether such a tool can be used at all — probability calibration measured as a first-class criterion, and a seven-axis characterization of how the frozen pipeline degrades under domain shift.

**Contributions.** Stated concretely, this work contributes:

1. **A domain-shift robustness map** for a synthetic-trained biosignature-triage classifier: the frozen pipeline is stress-tested across seven perturbation axes, its degradation is smooth and monotonic wherever a perturbation strength can be swept, and each large loss reduces to one of a few identifiable mechanisms, or on the extrapolation axis to leaving the sampled parameter envelope, rather than to opaque failure.
2. **Probability calibration as a first-class evaluation criterion**, measured and reported alongside accuracy, because a triage tool's confidence estimates—not only its labels—drive how scarce follow-up time is allocated.
3. **A characterization of where label-discriminative information sits in the PCA feature space**: the two highest-variance components hold 98.4% of the spectral variance yet classify at chance, and dropping them costs no accuracy; the two molecules' imprints together occupy four components spanning the full variance range; and classification succeeds only by combining many individually weak components.

## Methods

### Dataset and simulation parameters

The synthetic dataset of atmospheric transmission spectra was generated using the MultiREx (v0.3.1) Python library, which leverages the TauREx 3 radiative transfer code (20). TauREx 3 is a modular framework designed to simulate the transit of starlight through a planetary atmosphere by integrating the Beer–Lambert law across discrete atmospheric layers. The molecular cross-sections used to generate every spectrum in this work are the Exo-Transmit opacity compilation (27)—built from the molecular data of Freedman et al. and Lupu et al. (28, 29, 30)—as distributed with MultiREx; this source is named explicitly because substituting an alternative compilation is one of the largest sources of out-of-domain error—and the largest among the forward-model choices—so the choice is not incidental to reproducibility.

A training set of 2,696 simulated planetary spectra was generated (reduced from 3,000 after removing spectra with physically impossible transit depths > 1.0 or NaN values), as well as five independent testing sets, each containing approximately 540 clean spectra (2,697 in total). Each sample contained a one-dimensional transmission spectrum (transit depth as a function of wavelength) with 550 spectral bins spanning wavelengths from 0.5 to 7.8 micrometers at a spectral resolution of R = 200, with a constant signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 15 applied to all observations, following the fixed-SNR noise treatment of Duque-Castaño et al. (19). The uniform R = 200 grid is adopted as a deliberate upper bound rather than as a model of what Tier 3 delivers: R = 200 is the top of the resolving power quoted for the AIRS instrument as designed (4), whereas Tier 3 is modeled at R = 100 for AIRS-CH0 in both the mission's radiometric model and the published target studies (9, 31) — so the grid is optimistic by about a factor of two even in Ariel's best spectroscopic channel. Performance is therefore established at the resolution ceiling and its degradation measured downward.

For supervised classification, binary labels were assigned based on the presence of a specific biosignature pair, methane and ozone, adapting the binary candidate-screening framework of Duque-Castaño et al. (19). A spectrum was labeled as a "Biosignature" case only if both gases were present above predefined abundance thresholds:

- Methane: log mixing ratio > −6.0
- Ozone: log mixing ratio > −7.0

All planets that did not meet both conditions simultaneously were labeled as "Non-Biosignature." The particular cutoffs are a convention adopted here, not a physical boundary and not one inherited: the closest prior work labels on molecular presence or absence, without an abundance cutoff (19). Because the values are therefore a choice of this study, their influence is tested directly — moving both cutoffs together by up to half a dex and relabeling changes overall accuracy by less than two points (86.99% to 88.91%), so the reported performance is not an artifact of the specific values chosen.

To obtain an approximately balanced dataset (50% positive, 50% negative) and limit majority-class bias, a stratified generation plan consisting of four disjoint composition profiles was applied; the shares below are sampling targets, before invalid spectra were removed:

1. **Biosignature Profile (50.0% of dataset):** CH₄ is drawn log-uniformly from [−6.0, −3.0], and O₃ from [−7.0, −1.0] (both gases strictly above thresholds).
2. **Non-Bio Methane Profile (16.66%):** CH₄ from [−6.0, −3.0], O₃ from [−10.0, −7.0] (methane only).
3. **Non-Bio Ozone Profile (16.66%):** CH₄ from [−9.0, −6.0], O₃ from [−7.0, −1.0] (ozone only).
4. **Non-Bio None Profile (16.68%):** CH₄ from [−9.0, −6.0], O₃ from [−10.0, −7.0] (both below thresholds).

All other physical parameters (planet radius, mass, stellar temperatures, and background gas abundances) were drawn from the absolute bounds outlined in Table 1 across all four profiles. Each is drawn uniformly over its tabulated range, but the draws are not mutually independent. Planet radius, stellar temperature and semi-major axis are collinear in the delivered sample (Pearson r ≥ 0.97), atmospheric temperature follows the same direction less tightly (r = 0.88), and planet mass, stellar radius and base pressure form a second coupled group (r ≥ 0.85); radius and mass remain independent of one another (r = 0.13). The six planetary and stellar parameters therefore explore roughly three independent directions rather than six. This was not intended; it arises in the generation step, not from the sampling bounds themselves. It does not reach the labeling chemistry: CH₄ and O₃ abundances are uncorrelated with the coupled directions (|r| < 0.02), and a classifier given the bulk parameters alone, with no spectrum, performs at chance (51.9% against a 50.3% base rate), so the coupling constrains which parameter combinations are represented rather than the accuracy reported below.

### Preprocessing and feature engineering

Z-score standardization was applied to the raw spectral flux values, fit across the training set separately for each of the 550 wavelength channels, so that every channel contributes on a common scale rather than in proportion to its absolute transit depth. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was then used to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset. Following the PCA transformation, a secondary standardization step was applied to the retained components using a StandardScaler fit on the training set and applied to all test sets, rescaling the 102 components to zero mean and unit variance. Because the PCA scores are already uncorrelated, the step amounts to whitening the feature space, and it is referred to as whitening below. It is an optimization remedy for the gradient-based architectures: without it the MLP is dominated by the two highest-variance components, while the 1D-CNN's deficit is larger and is not recovered by dropping that pair. The tree ensembles are essentially unaffected: XGBoost returns near-identical metrics with and without this step at every component range tested, as the approximate invariance of histogram-based node splitting to monotonic per-feature rescaling would predict.

The final feature set retained the first 102 principal components. This count was fixed in advance rather than derived from a cumulative-variance or scree criterion; performance is insensitive to it, varying by under three accuracy points across retained sets ranging from 50 components to the full 102.

### Machine learning architectures

Two families of machine learning models—tree-based ensembles and neural networks—were evaluated, spanning four architectures to determine which approach was best suited for this spectral classification task. Tree-based ensembles were included as established nonlinear supervised-learning baselines: Random Forests are robust ensembles of decision trees, while XGBoost is a scalable gradient-boosted tree method widely used in machine-learning applications (33, 34). An MLP was included as a fully connected neural-network baseline for learning nonlinear relationships among the PCA components (37). Finally, the 1D-CNN was included because convolutional models have been applied to spectroscopic exoplanet data to detect molecular patterns in the spectral dimension and handle challenging noise conditions (38).

For XGBoost, the optimal configuration was determined to be 200 estimators with a maximum tree depth of 5, a subsample ratio of 0.8, and a learning rate of 0.2, utilizing a standard log-loss objective function. The Random Forest model maximized performance using 300 decision trees with unbounded depth, requiring at least 2 samples per leaf and 2 samples per internal node split. The winning MLP configuration utilized a three-layer funnel with 256, 128, and 64 neurons, respectively; each dense layer was followed by Batch Normalization to stabilize training, a ReLU activation function, and a 0.3 dropout rate to mitigate overfitting (37), optimized using the Adam algorithm with a learning rate of 0.001 and trained in batches of 128 (40). The standard 1D-CNN consisted of two convolutional layers with filters of 32 and 64 respectively; each convolutional layer was followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, 1D max pooling, and dropout (p = 0.3). Models trained directly on the raw high-dimensional spectra failed to converge on generalizable features, yielding performance akin to random guessing (~50% accuracy). However, when the 1D-CNN was instead trained on the PCA-transformed feature space, performance improved substantially, indicating that dimensionality reduction was needed for the neural architectures at this training-set size.

### Validation framework

For the tree-based ensemble models (XGBoost and Random Forest), hyperparameters were systematically optimized using an exhaustive grid search with stratified 3-fold cross-validation. However, initial cross-validation trials with the deep neural networks (MLP and 1D-CNN) revealed severe underfitting due to data starvation when fragmenting the relatively small dataset (2,696 samples). Therefore, the neural network architectures were optimized and evaluated using a single-split validation approach, with 80% of the data reserved for training and 20% for validation and early stopping.

To assess robustness, five disjoint, independently generated test sets (~540 spectra each, with no overlap with the training set or with one another) were evaluated; the PCA basis and StandardScaler were fit on the training set only and applied unchanged to each. Each model was evaluated on all five sets, and the mean and standard deviation of four key metrics—Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score—are reported below.

All data generation, modeling, and evaluations were performed under a unified software environment using Python v3.10.6. The synthetic spectra were generated using MultiREx v0.3.1 built on the TauREx v3.3.2 radiative transfer engine. The tree-based ensembles were developed using scikit-learn v1.7.2 (for Random Forest) and XGBoost v3.2.0. The deep learning architectures (MLP and 1D-CNN) were compiled and trained using TensorFlow v2.21.0 and scikeras v0.13.0 wrappers. A single global seed (SEED = 42) was fixed across all partitions, initializations, and cross-validation folds.

### Robustness and ablation experiments

Beyond the in-domain evaluation, the trained pipeline was subjected to two families of controlled experiments. The first is a set of ablations that isolate the roles of the feature space and preprocessing—selective removal of principal-component ranges, whitening applied or omitted, supervised projections (partial least squares and linear discriminant analysis) at matched dimensionality, and alternative per-component weightings—each performed with the classifier held fixed so that only the preprocessing choice varied. The second is a characterization of robustness under seven axes of domain shift. In six of the seven axes — the cross-code comparison, alternative opacity data, clouds and hazes, stellar contamination, resolution and signal-to-noise, and radiometric instrument noise — the models were trained once and the entire preprocessing chain (raw scaler, PCA basis, and post-PCA scaler) was fit on clean training data only and applied unchanged; only the evaluation data was altered, so any change in accuracy reflects the perturbation rather than re-fitting. The seventh axis necessarily retrains: the out-of-envelope split trains on a radius-restricted subset together with a size-matched random-split control. The physical perturbations (aerosols and alternative opacity data) were applied by re-rendering the exact committed test planets, so each perturbed spectrum is paired planet-by-planet with its clear-sky counterpart and the degradation is a within-planet difference rather than a comparison across independently drawn samples.

Injected noise was anchored to the measured per-spectrum noise floor and swept from the nominal SNR = 15 down to SNR = 5, reaching the low-signal regime of the mission's faintest and most muted targets. Stellar-contamination coverage fractions (2–20%) sat within the range inferred for cool, active M dwarfs (42), which are present in Ariel-like target samples. The cloud-top pressures and haze densities ranged from spectra that retain nearly all of their feature amplitude to spectra muted toward the continuum, spanning the clear-to-cloudy continuum observed across transiting planets (15).

## Results

### Overall performance and model hierarchy

To ensure a fair comparison, all four architectures were trained and evaluated using the unified 102-component feature space (indices 0–101). Under these identical conditions, the gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble (XGBoost) demonstrated the strongest overall performance, achieving a top mean accuracy of 88.17% (± 1.99%) and an F1-score of 88.24% (± 2.22%). XGBoost decisively outperformed the Multi-Layer Perceptron (79.34% ± 1.47%) and the 1D-CNN (75.53% ± 1.05%), and held a narrower but statistically significant edge over the Random Forest (86.51% ± 1.96%). The margin separating the two model families is attributed to the way each handles the multi-scale variance structure of the PCA representation: the two highest-variance components carry 98.41% of the spectral variance but classify at chance when used alone, while label-discriminative structure is distributed across the low-variance components. Tree-based models, whose node-splitting is invariant to per-feature scale, exploit those low-variance components natively, with no rescaling required. This residual deficit is consistent with the broader finding that tree ensembles outperform neural networks on structured, medium-sized tabular feature spaces (21, 22), and is likely compounded by the modest size of the training set (2,696 samples), which constrained the neural architectures.

To rigorously evaluate the statistical significance of these results, a dual-validation approach was employed. First, pairwise McNemar's tests (43) were conducted on the models' predictions to evaluate paired classification agreement, on the five test sets pooled (n = 2,697). XGBoost holds a statistically significant paired advantage over the Random Forest (b = 151, c = 95; p = 0.0005) and a larger one over the MLP (b = 437, c = 169; p < 0.0001). The Random Forest in turn leads the MLP (b = 406, c = 194; p < 0.0001), and all three lead the 1D-CNN (p < 0.0001). Second, a paired bootstrap (44) with 10,000 iterations was performed over the pooled test planets: XGBoost's F1 exceeds the Random Forest's by 2.07 points, with a 95% confidence interval on the paired difference of [+0.92, +3.22] points (p = 0.0002). The two tests therefore agree: XGBoost's edge over the Random Forest is systematic on a planet-by-planet basis and survives resampling of the test population on a metric that balances precision and recall.

### The role of PCA in feature extraction

The contribution of PCA can be decomposed into two distinct steps. Trained on the raw 550-bin spectra, the 1D-CNN failed to learn generalizable features, reaching only 50.56% (± 1.58%) accuracy—barely above chance. Projecting onto the 102 principal components raised it to 64.51% (± 0.79%), and the subsequent per-component standardization raised it further to 75.53% (± 1.05%). The same step lifted the MLP from 74.81% (± 2.61%) to 79.34% (± 1.47%). Dimensionality reduction and variance equalization thus contribute separately to neural-network performance, while the scale-invariant tree models—unaffected by the per-component standardization—already operate near their ceiling on the PCA features.

No individual principal component is strongly discriminative: the maximum single-feature AUC across all 102 components is 0.663 on the training set (0.654 on held-out data), while the component carrying 97.1% of the variance is indistinguishable from chance (AUC 0.506). Classification performance arises from aggregating many weakly informative components rather than from a few dominant ones. Fourteen of the twenty most discriminative components fall outside the twenty highest-variance components, and retaining only the two highest-variance components yields 52.13% accuracy—chance, on balanced classes—while discarding them costs nothing measurable (88.58% → 88.25%, within the ±0.6-point binomial standard error). Variance rank and discriminative rank are therefore substantially decoupled in this feature space, as Figure 1 shows component by component. Small differences between the baseline XGBoost accuracy quoted across sections—88.17% (Table 2, averaged over the five test sets), 88.58% (the full 102-component pipeline used for these ablations), and 88.9% (the paired baseline of the robustness experiments)—are comparable to the ±0.6-point binomial standard error on 2,697 test planets and arise from separately fitted instances of the same 102-component pipeline evaluated along slightly different paths; they are the same result rather than conflicting ones.

In the middle panel the two curves are the training set and the five pooled test sets (maximum single-feature AUC 0.663 and 0.654 respectively); the bottom panel is computed on the training set only. The grey band in each panel marks PC0–PC1.

Each molecule's spectral signal concentrates in a small number of specific components. Projecting each imprint onto the components gives a participation ratio of 2.5 for methane and 1.9 for ozone. Ozone's imprint sits two-thirds on PC0 (67%), with most of the remainder on PC4 (28%); methane's sits mostly on PC3 (56%) and PC2 (24%), with 15% on PC0. Four components out of 102 therefore hold about 95% of both imprints. PC0 carries most of ozone's imprint and still classifies at chance, because that imprint is a negligible fraction of PC0's own variance.

That the task is intrinsically nonlinear is confirmed directly: linear classifiers on the same 102 features plateau far below the trees—linear discriminant analysis at 68.2% and linear PLS-DA at 73.9%, against roughly 89% for gradient-boosted XGBoost—as expected from the label itself, which is positive only where methane and ozone both exceed their thresholds, a conjunction whose positive region is not linearly separable. The discriminative structure must therefore be recovered by combining many components nonlinearly rather than read from any single linear projection. Low-variance principal components acting as important predictors is a long-recognized phenomenon in principal-component regression (45).

### Error analysis and physical dependencies

Accuracy is highest for planets in the interior of the sampled parameter ranges and falls toward both extremes. The models struggled with planets possessing low atmospheric temperatures and small radii: binning the pooled test set into quintiles, the error rate is 14.6% for the coldest fifth of atmospheres and 15.7% for the smallest fifth of planets, against 7.4–11.1% in the middle quintiles. This aligns with physical intuition: cooler temperatures compress the atmospheric scale height, which flattens the transmission spectrum and mutes the amplitude of diagnostic chemical absorption features beneath the noise floor. Binning instead by the measured per-spectrum feature amplitude—the scatter of transit depth across the 550 spectral bins, which measures how far molecular absorption features rise above the continuum—reproduces the effect directly—14.8% error in the lowest fifth against 8.0% in the middle—so amplitude suppression is the dominant driver at the low-amplitude end, where the error rate nearly doubles.

Misclassifications also clustered heavily around the predefined biosignature boundaries (log[CH₄] ≈ −6.0 and log[O₃] ≈ −7.0). Binned by how far a planet's abundances lie from that cutoff, accuracy rises from 61.9% within 0.25 dex to 95.3% at distances beyond one dex. Because class balance varies between bins, each bin is compared against its own majority-class baseline: in the nearest bin the classifier does not exceed that baseline at all, and its mean predicted probability is 0.515. Within a quarter-dex of the cutoff the labeling separates atmospheres that are physically near-identical, so no spectrum can recover the distinction; the errors concentrated there reflect the arbitrariness of a threshold convention rather than a limitation of the classifier. This regime accounts for 8.8% of the test set. The converse also follows: because abundances are drawn uniformly across several decades, 64.8% of test planets lie at least one dex from the cutoff, where the deciding abundance differs by more than an order of magnitude between the classes. Overall accuracy is therefore weighted toward well-separated cases, and the headline figure should be read alongside this breakdown—the method separates chemically distinct atmospheres reliably and approaches the labeling convention's own resolution limit as the distinction narrows.

The wavelength dependence of the pipeline was tested directly by masking one octave of the clear test spectra at a time and re-evaluating the frozen pipeline. Masking 3.9–7.8 μm is by far the most damaging, taking both label-diagnostic subsets below chance; this is the octave containing the 4.74 μm O₃ band and the 7.7 μm CH₄ band, though it also carries the 4.3 μm CO₂ band and much of the water and continuum structure, so the loss cannot be attributed to the label-defining molecules alone. Masking 0.5–1.0 μm, by contrast, costs the O₃ subset only six points, so the classifier does not read ozone through the Chappuis band near 0.6 μm. That dependence bears directly on deployment: the two octaves that cost the most when masked, 3.9–7.8 μm and 1.0–2.0 μm, are the regions Ariel delivers most coarsely — at R ≈ 30 and, over most of its span, R ≈ 15, against the uniform R = 200 assumed here. The pipeline relies most on the parts of the band the mission resolves least well.

### Probability calibration and mission triage reliability

When selecting a machine learning model to serve as an intra-Tier 3 prioritization filter, the decision depends heavily on the operational cost of classification errors. As a primary measure of calibration, the reliability diagram (Figure 2) is used, comparing predicted confidence against empirical outcome frequency independently of raw accuracy. It is computed on all five test sets pooled (2,697 planets) and binned by equal counts rather than equal width. With ~269 planets in every bin, XGBoost's curve tracks the diagonal across the whole probability range, no bin departing from its own predicted rate by more than 0.08. The Random Forest curve is far steeper than the diagonal—predicting 0.14 where nothing is positive and 0.86 where everything is, a compression of its probabilities toward 0.5 that reaches 0.26 at its widest. The MLP and 1D-CNN instead concentrate their predictions into a narrow band near 0.5 that spans outcomes from roughly 0.2 to 0.8, resolving the middle of the range poorly.

The expected calibration error—the mean absolute gap between predicted and observed rates across the bins of Figure 2, which isolates the calibration component—separates calibration from discrimination: XGBoost 0.032, 1D-CNN 0.102 (± 0.016), MLP 0.128 (± 0.018), Random Forest 0.176, the neural-network figures being means over training restarts. This ordering is not the accuracy ordering. The Random Forest separates the classes better than either neural network (AUC 0.949 against 0.904 and 0.881) yet is the worst calibrated of the four, so calibration quality on this dataset is not a by-product of discriminative power; XGBoost leads on both. The Random Forest's weaker calibration is consistent with a documented property of averaged tree ensembles: variance among the base trees biases predictions that should sit near 0 or 1 away from those values, compressing the ensemble's probabilities toward the center (52). XGBoost is therefore adopted as the primary recommendation—not because the alternatives cannot be calibrated, but because it delivers reliable probabilities within this benchmark without a separate calibration stage. This calibration advantage is measured in-distribution only: under injected observational systematics it degrades faster than accuracy—at an effective SNR of 10 the Brier score rises from 0.080 to 0.139 while accuracy falls eight points—so calibration should be re-established on data representative of the deployment distribution rather than assumed to transfer.

Because the tree ensembles produce strongly bimodal probabilities, the bins hold equal counts (~269 planets each) rather than equal widths, and error bars are 95% Wilson binomial intervals; legend values are the expected calibration error, the mean absolute gap from the diagonal. ### Deployment thresholds and operating-point selection

Deployment on the Ariel mission would likely involve moving the decision threshold to align with specific scientific objectives. At the default 0.5 threshold the classifier operates at 89.3% precision and 87.8% recall on the pooled test sets, with an average precision of 0.961 against a no-skill baseline of 0.503. Raising recall to 95% requires lowering the threshold to 0.18 and costs eight points of precision (81.0%); reaching 99% recall requires a threshold of 0.03 and costs twenty (69.0%). A recall-first pass over the Tier 3 catalog is therefore affordable: nineteen of every twenty positively labeled planets can be retained while roughly one flagged candidate in five is a false positive.

These operating points do not transfer across domain shift (Figure 3). Re-scoring the same planets rendered with an untrained-for grey cloud deck at 10⁴ Pa lowers the average precision to 0.890 and, more consequentially for deployment, moves the thresholds themselves: 95% recall now requires a threshold of 0.07, not 0.18, and returns 67.4% precision instead of 81.0%. A cut point chosen on clean synthetic spectra would therefore deliver neither the recall nor the precision it was selected for once the observed population departs from the training distribution. The predicted probabilities remain ordered well enough to rank candidates, but the cut point itself has to be chosen on data drawn from the deployment distribution, not carried over from a synthetic benchmark.

The dotted line is the no-skill baseline set by the 50.3% positive rate. Marked points are two candidate recall-first operating points, each labeled with the decision threshold required and the precision it costs. The thresholds that achieve a given recall in domain do not achieve it under the cloud deck, so an operating point selected on clean spectra does not carry over unchanged.

**Injected observational systematics.** Six perturbation families were each swept over four strengths above the clean baseline and applied only to the test sets at the raw transit-depth stage (Figure 4). Calibration-type errors are largely tolerated—a baseline offset and a gain ramp cost under three points—while correlated noise is the dominant vulnerability, costing 22 points at an effective SNR of 5 and remaining worse than white noise at equal SNR (66.9% vs 73.9%). Within the resolution family, the reference point for Tier 3 is R = 100, the value at which AIRS-CH0 is modeled: accuracy there is 78.4%, 10.5 points below the R = 200 baseline but still well above chance.

**Figure 4 | Accuracy of the frozen pipeline under six injected-systematics families, for XGBoost (unwhitened) and the MLP with and without whitening.** Each family is shown at the clean baseline (the leftmost point of every panel, 88.9% for XGBoost) followed by four increasing strengths. Degradation is smooth and, apart from sub-half-point fluctuations in the near-flat families, monotonic; correlated noise is the steepest (the dominant vulnerability), while a gain ramp and a baseline offset are nearly flat. The whitened MLP (green) starts above the unwhitened MLP (pink) on clean data but crosses below it under strong perturbation in the four families with a measurable effect, illustrating that the standardization step reduces robustness.

**Whitening reduces robustness.** A controlled comparison in which two otherwise-identical neural networks differ only in whether the standardization is applied—each trained five times to average over initialization—shows that the step reduces robustness. Averaged over restarts, the standardized network begins 3.6 ± 3.1 accuracy points ahead on clean data and ends 11.0 ± 1.7 points behind at an effective SNR of 5, with the curves crossing by SNR 12 in four of five restarts. The same asymmetry appears under a change of forward model rather than injected noise: re-scoring both networks on spectra recomputed for the same planets with the independent radiative transfer code described below, the standardized network loses 8.6 points (77.5% ± 0.9 to 68.9% ± 0.7) while the unstandardized one loses 1.0 (72.9% ± 6.4 to 71.9% ± 6.2), again over five restarts. Both support the concern that rescaling every low-variance component equally raises the weight of components dominated by systematics as much as those carrying signal. The recommended model, XGBoost, uses no standardization, so this does not affect the headline result.

**Independent radiative transfer code.** To separate the influence of the radiative transfer implementation from that of the underlying opacity data, transmission spectra were recomputed for the held-out test planets with Exo-Transmit (27). The molecular cross sections were held fixed: the opacity tables used by the training simulator originate from Exo-Transmit and were supplied to it unchanged, so the radiative transfer implementation is the only quantity that differs. The two codes agree closely on the spectra themselves (median per-planet correlation 0.997, mean transit depths within 0.7%), and classification accuracy falls from 88.9% to 84.8%, a reduction of 4.1 points.

**Alternative molecular opacity data.** The H₂O, CH₄, and CO₂ tables were replaced with ExoMol line lists—POKAZATEL, YT34to10, and UCL-4000 respectively (53, 54, 55)—supplied in TauREx format from the ExoMolOP database (56), with the code and structure fixed, and the planets re-rendered and paired. Accuracy falls from 88.9% to 72.8% (−16.1); replacing ozone with HITRAN (57) as well—extending the substitution to both label-defining molecules—lowers it to 66.0% (−22.9). The three-molecule substitution acts as a bias shift rather than signal loss: median feature amplitude barely moves (0.95× → 0.93×) while the predicted-positive rate moves from 49.5% to 61.8% against a true rate of 50.3%. Comparing the two experiments, substituting the opacity data costs roughly four times as much accuracy as substituting the radiative transfer code (six times with ozone replaced): the method's transferability is limited principally by uncertainty in molecular line lists rather than by the choice of forward model, a constraint shared by any retrieval-based analysis of real observations.

**Clouds and hazes.** Aerosols were introduced into the forward model—a grey cloud deck at fixed cloud-top pressures and, separately, the Lee et al. (58) Mie-extinction parameterization, applied here as a photochemical haze—with the classifiers trained only on aerosol-free spectra. Mild aerosols leave performance essentially unaffected: a deck at 10⁵ Pa costs 2.3 points, and a haze retaining 98% of the clear feature amplitude costs 0.04, within measurement noise. The degradation becomes substantial once an aerosol removes roughly a fifth of the clear amplitude, and reaches −25 points or worse at strong muting for both prescriptions. At the most opaque deck tested, 10¹ Pa, feature amplitude falls to 3% of its clear value and accuracy reaches 52.3%, near chance, because almost no diagnostic structure remains—a limit of retrievable signal rather than a specific failure of the classifier. Calibration degrades faster than accuracy along the same sweep: the Brier score nearly doubles by the 10⁴ Pa deck (0.080 to 0.151) and more than quadruples at 10¹ Pa.

**Physical stellar contamination.** Stellar contamination was modeled via the Transit Light Source Effect (42): unocculted starspots and faculae make the transit chord's stellar spectrum unrepresentative of the disc. The surface fluxes were taken from BT-Settl/PHOENIX model spectra (59), so the spot and faculae spectra carry their own molecular bands. Applying cool spots (T_spot = 0.85 T_eff) and hot faculae (T_eff + 100 K) to the held-out spectra through the frozen pipeline, accuracy falls from 88.9% to 85.7% at 2% spot coverage and to 65.7% at 20%. Unlike the aerosol case, accuracy loss does not track feature amplitude: amplitude falls to 0.033 at 10% coverage but rises to 0.045 at 20%, above its clean value of 0.042, while accuracy continues to fall. The mechanism is therefore chromatic distortion—structured, feature-like contamination added by the star—rather than suppression of the planetary signal.

**Realistic instrument noise.** Instrument noise was modeled with ExoRad2 (60), the open radiometric engine at the core of the second version of the Ariel radiometric model (31), driven by an Ariel payload built from published channel parameters. The resulting noise-to-signal ratio is strongly wavelength-dependent—a factor of 29.5 across 0.5–7.8 μm. Applied to the held-out spectra and compared against white noise at the same median level, this realistic coloring changes accuracy by at most 1.3 points. Realistic instrument-noise coloring is therefore a minor additional penalty; the dominant noise vulnerability is the time-correlated systematics identified above, which cost up to 22 points.

**Out-of-envelope extrapolation.** Training restricted to planets with radius ≤ 15 R⊕ and tested on radius > 15 gives 75.8%, against a same-sized random-split control at 84.1% and the full in-distribution 88.9%. Holding the training-set size fixed, the extrapolation penalty is −8.3 points; the remainder of the naïve gap is reduced training data. Because the generating parameters are not drawn independently, this split is not a radius extrapolation alone: the held-out planets are also hotter, orbit hotter stars, and sit at wider separations than anything in training, so −8.3 measures the cost of leaving the sampled envelope along several coupled directions at once rather than along radius by itself.

**Synthesis.** Taken together, the seven axes place the method within a quantified envelope. On the four axes whose strength can be swept—aerosols, stellar contamination, resolution and signal-to-noise, and instrument noise—degradation is smooth and monotonic: large in magnitude at the strongest settings, but reached progressively rather than at a cliff. Three axes—aerosols, stellar contamination and the opacity substitution—each reduce to a measured mechanism: feature-amplitude suppression (aerosols drive accuracy smoothly toward chance as amplitude vanishes), chromatic distortion (stellar contamination adds structure while leaving amplitude intact), and a decision-threshold bias (substituting three molecular opacities shifts the predicted-positive rate at near-constant amplitude, though replacing ozone as well crosses into outright signal loss). The largest sensitivities, comparable in magnitude at strong perturbation, are to the molecular opacity data (−22.9 points), strong aerosols (−25.6) or stellar contamination (−23.2), and time-correlated noise at an effective SNR of 5 (−22.0); realistic instrument-noise coloring and an independent radiative transfer code are comparatively minor. Within its training domain the pipeline is a fast, calibrated triage tool—it ranks candidates by biosignature-labeling status with reliable confidence, which is what prioritizing scarce follow-up time requires—and, unlike prior work, its failures outside that domain are now measured and reduce to a few identifiable mechanisms, so where it can and cannot be trusted is known rather than assumed.

## Discussion

This study evaluated the feasibility of using machine learning to recover a threshold-based biosignature labeling from exoplanet transmission spectra within an Ariel-like observational framework. By training and comparing four models—XGBoost, Random Forest, Multi-Layer Perceptron, and a 1D Convolutional Neural Network—on synthetic spectra generated with MultiREx and processed through a PCA-based feature extraction pipeline, this work established a calibrated benchmark for this task and characterized its behavior under domain shift.

Among the evaluated approaches, the tree-based ensembles (XGBoost and Random Forest) substantially outperformed the deep neural networks. Because their node-splitting is invariant to per-feature scale, the tree models used the low-variance components without any rescaling, whereas the neural networks required explicit standardization of the PCA features even to remain competitive and still fell short. Beyond aggregate accuracy, XGBoost was the best calibrated of the four models, with an expected calibration error of 0.032 against 0.176 for the Random Forest — whose deficit is consistent with the documented tendency of averaged tree ensembles to compress probabilities toward the center (52) — and it is the primary triage recommendation within this synthetic benchmark, pending validation on real observations.

No principal component isolates the biosignature signal. The leading pair, holding 98.4% of the variance between them, is individually uninformative and can be discarded at no cost. Classification succeeds by nonlinearly combining many individually weak components. Variance rank is therefore not a usable criterion for selecting components in this feature space: selecting by explained variance would retain the pair that classifies at chance and discard those that carry the label.

Under domain shift the pipeline degrades traceably rather than opaquely, and smoothly and monotonically wherever a perturbation strength can be swept, though the strongest perturbations are costly. Each large loss reduces to one of a small number of identified mechanisms, or on the extrapolation axis to leaving the sampled parameter envelope; among noise sources, time-correlated systematics are the dominant vulnerability, while realistic Ariel instrument-noise coloring, modeled with a radiometric simulator, costs only about one point beyond white noise. The same shift moves the operating point as well as the accuracy: a threshold chosen on clean spectra to deliver 95% recall returns neither that recall nor its precision once an untrained-for cloud deck is present, so the deployment cut point, like the calibration, has to be set on the distribution the tool will actually face.

Several limitations remain. The class labels are a deterministic function of the abundances used to generate the spectra, so the task is the recovery of a labeling convention rather than the inference of abundances from observations, which Ariel will require via retrieval. The models are trained on spectra from a single radiative transfer code; evaluated on spectra recomputed for the same planets with an independent code, accuracy falls from 88.9% to 84.8%, and evaluated with an alternative molecular opacity compilation, to 72.8%. The training data contain no aerosols, and the atmospheres are one-dimensional. The chemical disequilibrium that makes CH₄ and O₃ a biosignature pair is not enforced, so the positive class is a composition-grid convention rather than a set of self-consistent atmospheres, and the dataset cannot address abiotic false positives arising from chemistry. The clearest next steps are to replace the deterministic labels with retrieval-derived labels carrying realistic uncertainty—the primary remaining item—and to upgrade two of the three approximate robustness models: a metallicity-matched stellar grid, and the consortium's recently released public dataset of simulated Ariel observations (25), which supplies the detector-level systematics, pointing jitter and calibration products the present noise model omits.

Overall, this work measures a reliability envelope for one triage classifier on one synthetic grid: its behavior is characterized rather than assumed, across seven axes of domain shift and with calibrated probabilities that make its confidence usable for allocating scarce observing time. As machine-learning triage is increasingly proposed for missions like Ariel, it is this kind of quantified reliability, rather than headline accuracy alone, that will determine whether such tools can responsibly support target prioritization for atmospheric surveys.
