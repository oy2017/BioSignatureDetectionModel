# 7. Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the application of machine learning architectures to the detection of atmospheric biosignatures in high-resolution, low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) exoplanet transmission spectra. Our primary goal was to develop an efficient, highly sensitive surrogate model capable of classifying potentially habitable worlds—specifically prioritizing the identification of atmospheres in redox disequilibrium via the simultaneous presence of methane and ozone. Using the `multirex` framework to simulate 5,000-bin spectra for an Ariel-like mission, we evaluated four distinct models: XGBoost, Random Forest, a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and a 1D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN).

Our results highlight the critical importance of domain-aware feature engineering. We found that the first two principal components (PC0 and PC1) of the spectral data overwhelmingly captured broad-scale physical systematics—such as the mean transit depth ($r=0.961$) and stellar continuum—rather than localized chemical absorption features. By discarding these components and training our models on the subsequent 100 principal components (indices 2–102), we effectively isolated the subtle chemical fingerprints. Under this preprocessing regime, the XGBoost classifier achieved a top mean accuracy of 88.67% (± 1.52%). Pairwise McNemar's tests confirmed that the tree-based ensemble methods (XGBoost and Random Forest) significantly outperformed the deep learning architectures ($p < 0.001$) on this structured, tabular data representation. 

Furthermore, our analysis of model reliability using calibration curves revealed a distinct divergence in model confidence. While the MLP achieved an exceptionally high recall (94.56%), its calibration curve indicated severe overconfidence on incorrect predictions. More drastically, the 1D-CNN completely failed to discriminate on the raw spectra, acting entirely "undecided" and predicting only near-average probabilities (~0.5). Conversely, XGBoost demonstrated excellent calibration (Brier score: 0.072). We attribute the superior performance and calibration of the tree-based models to their inherent robustness against overfitting on smaller datasets (~2,700 training samples), whereas the end-to-end deep learning models succumbed to the curse of dimensionality. Consequently, we propose a tiered observation strategy: utilizing the high-recall MLP as an initial, sensitive "first-pass" filter to flag all potential candidates, followed by the highly accurate and calibrated XGBoost model to vet targets before committing expensive follow-up spectroscopic observation time.

Moving forward, several avenues exist to expand upon this foundational work. First, the models should be trained and tested on a much larger dataset to allow the 1D-CNN and ResNet architectures to converge properly and learn hierarchical features directly from the raw spectra without PCA. Data augmentation techniques, such as injecting varied, instrument-specific noise profiles or synthesizing different cloud deck altitudes, could improve model generalization. Finally, future work will aim to adapt this methodology beyond binary classification, exploring multi-label classification to independently quantify the presence and confidence intervals of individual molecular species in anticipation of the Ariel mission.
